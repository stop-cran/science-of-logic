# From School-Logic to Dialectic

*A reconstruction, guided by Hegel, of the historical-logical problems through which thought seeks a method adequate to its content.*

---

## I. The Standpoint of Traditional ("School") Logic

The school-logic of the textbook tradition — the syllogistic apparatus inherited from Aristotle through scholasticism, formalized in the handbooks of Wolff, in Kant's *Logic*, and in the introductory manuals that still follow their plan — presents itself as the science of correct thinking. Its content is the forms of thought: concept, judgment, syllogism; the laws of identity, of non-contradiction, of the excluded middle.

Hegel charges this logic with a striking contradiction between its **content** and its **method**: a science of *thinking itself* presents its own forms by external, taxonomic enumeration. The forms are picked up one after another, classified, and laid side by side — each indifferent to the next. Concept, judgment, syllogism appear as three separate "topics" whose order is asserted rather than derived.

The force of the charge is that **a science which teaches necessary connection leaves the connection of its own forms unexplained** (*SL* §§65, 1338; §27). It can distinguish valid syllogisms without showing *why* judgment should develop into syllogism, why "S is P" should require the mediation of a middle term. But the demand to derive the forms is not already entailed by teaching valid inference: Aristotle expressly distinguishes demonstration from knowledge of its first principles (*Posterior Analytics* I.3). Hegel's further demand is that logic account for the necessity of its own determinations, not merely for conclusions drawn within them. Whether thought can supply that account through its *self-movement* is not yet established here — that is what this corpus has to earn.

School-logic is *formal*: it abstracts from the particular content of what is thought to examine the forms of thinking. Hegel's objection concerns the status of those forms themselves. Their table supplies logic with a content, but an external arrangement does not establish the internal necessity of that content. Such arrangement exemplifies what he calls *external reflection*: thinking stands over against its object rather than exhibiting the object's own articulation.

The achievement is retained: distinguishing forms and valid inferences is indispensable, and Hegel acknowledges the received logic's correctness relative to its presupposed object (*Enc.* §20 Addition). The challenge is to its sufficiency as the science of thought, not to the validity of everything it teaches. The discrepancy between its explanatory vocation and its treatment of its own forms opens our inquiry; it does not by itself prove the historical route beyond it.

## II. The Historical-Logical Itinerary

Hegel treats historical and systematic philosophy as **the same development of thought in different forms** (*Enc.* §§13–14). History presents it through particular systems and their circumstances; systematic exposition is to exhibit the determinations' own connections. Earlier principles survive as **moments**, not discarded errors. This is more than a parallel between independent series: Hegel holds that, stripped of their outward historical form, the systems' principles follow in the main the order of the logical determinations (*History of Philosophy*, Introduction, A). Chronology nevertheless cannot replace logical deduction: necessity must be shown in the systematic connection, not inferred from succession alone. The question is how a principle's own limits require its transformation, and what is *aufgehoben* — annulled, preserved, and lifted up — in that transformation.

We organize the introduction around five standpoints: consciousness before explicit logic, old metaphysics, empiricism, critical philosophy, and dialectics. The grouping and the connections drawn here are this synopsis's reconstruction: they expose problems that motivate the next standpoint, not a proof that these five alone form a necessary succession. Hegel's own survey, the *Positions of Thought toward Objectivity* (*Enc.* §§26–78), takes old metaphysics first, then empiricism and critical philosophy together as a single position, then immediate knowing; he distinguishes its historical and inferential procedure from the *Phenomenology*'s derivation of the philosophical standpoint (*Enc.* §25). Our standpoints are not the categorial sequence of the *Logic*, nor non-overlapping historical periods: representation persists within philosophy, and empiricism and rationalism develop alongside one another. A standpoint characterizes a system by its governing principle, not by everything its author thinks. Developing or defending that principle may bring forth determinations it cannot yet adequately ground, without a conscious intention to abandon it.

### 1. Consciousness before Explicit Logic

**Achievement.** Before logic becomes an explicit science, its determinations are already at work in sensing, speaking, and representing a world. Sense-certainty and *Vorstellung* — picture-thinking, myth, religion in its representational mode — are not the same: representation already has universal content, though it presents that content in images, stories, or separately given ideas (*Enc.* §20). What we begin with is the **distinction between consciousness and its object**, and the trust that what confronts us can be grasped.

**Contradiction.** The "this" of sense-certainty, when one tries to *say* it, becomes a universal ("now," "here," "I") rather than the singular it meant to fix: the claim to wholly immediate knowledge already employs mediation. Representation's limitation is different. It can carry truths about God, right, or duty while leaving their determinations side by side, connected by a story or a list of attributes rather than by an exhibited necessity. The first difficulty concerns the supposed absence of universality; the second concerns the uncomprehended connection of a content already universal.

**Resolution.** The demand is to make the **thought-determination** (*Gedankenbestimmung*) explicit and to comprehend its connections, rather than rest in what is simply shown or narrated. Metaphysics takes up that demand by asking what the object is *in itself*. This identifies a task for conceptual thought; it does not derive the pre-Kantian rationalist school directly from sense-certainty.

**What is preserved.** The trust that the object *is* knowable; that thinking and being meet; the indelible character of all subsequent thought as *thought of something*.

### 2. Old (Pre-Kantian) Metaphysics

**Achievement.** Its principle is as old as Parmenides, but the standpoint examined here is that of the pre-Kantian rationalist school — Descartes and Spinoza through Wolff: the great proposition that **thought-determinations are determinations of the thing itself**. What reason finds in the concept is what *is*. Old metaphysics systematizes an inherited categorial vocabulary — substance, cause, necessity, infinity — and applies such determinations as predicates to soul, world, and God, subjects it takes over from representation (*Enc.* §§28–31). Its strength is the confidence that thinking can reach the unconditioned; its dependence on those given subjects marks how incomplete its emancipation from representation remains.

**Contradiction.** Its method, however, takes its categories ready-made, uses them in fixed opposition (finite *or* infinite, simple *or* composite, free *or* determined), and assumes that one of each pair must simply be true of the object. Hence its exposure to the **antinomies** that Kant would later exhibit, claiming equal cogency for opposed proofs: that the world is finite and that it is infinite, that composite substance consists of simple parts and that nothing simple exists anywhere, that there is a first cause and that the chain of causes regresses without end. Hegel contests the proofs while retaining the importance of their opposed determinations (*Enc.* §48; §15). For him, the failure lies in treating those determinations as independently complete accounts of the unconditioned. Metaphysics has the courage to claim that thought reaches being, but its procedure does not establish the adequacy of the categories through which it claims to do so.

**Resolution.** This difficulty makes two responses intelligible, without deducing their historical occurrence or excluding other responses.

- (a) If thought's fixed categories shipwreck on the object, then thought should *abandon* its a priori pretensions and turn to **experience** — empiricism.
- (b) Or thought should turn back upon *itself* and **critique** its own faculty — critical philosophy.

**What is preserved.** The conviction that thinking grasps the thing; and the categorial inventory itself — substance, cause, ground, force — which never disappears, only changes its status.

### 3. Empiricism

**Achievement.** Empiricism (Bacon, Locke, Hume) makes a decisive advance: it demands that the content of thought be **earned**, not assumed. The principle *nihil est in intellectu quod non fuerit in sensu* — "nothing in the intellect that was not first in the senses," an old tag Hegel notes is wrongly fathered on Aristotle (*Enc.* §8) — enthrones perception, observation, experiment. It states the experiential demand shared by the **modern positive sciences**: content of unprecedented richness, lawful regularities, the disciplined humility of *evidence*. Empiricism teaches thought to be *concrete*: to refuse the empty word, to demand the thing. Against speculative construction indifferent to experience, it insists that thought answer to the content it investigates.

**Contradiction.** Hegel's objection is that empiricism employs thought-determinations — "thing," "force," "cause," "law," "matter" — which are not simply given in sensation, even while treating experience as its sole authority (*Enc.* §38). The difficulty is sharpest in Hume: observation yields repeated succession, not a necessary bond between events; that connection is **not in the data**. What fails is the claim to extract strict universality and necessity from observed instances, not empirical inquiry as such. Hume himself explains our inferences through custom rather than abandoning them (*Enquiry*, V): lack of a demonstrative ground does not, on his account, abolish their authority. To demand more is to reopen the question of their ground, not already to refute Hume on his own terms.

**Resolution.** Kant takes the need for strict universality and necessity in experience as a demand that Hume's explanation does not satisfy. If sensation does not supply them, he seeks their ground in the **subject**: what must cognition contribute for what is given to count as an object of experience? This motivates the critical turn; it is not a conclusion Hume must accept merely by pursuing his own account.

**What is preserved.** The demand that thought be filled with content; the rigor of evidence; empirical inference and the sciences it sustains. Their philosophical grounding is disputed, not their entire achievement annulled. Hegel credits the empirical sciences with pressing philosophy beyond abstract universality: their laws and classifications supply content already worked through by thought, whose connections philosophy must comprehend (*Enc.* §§9, 12). Preserving that achievement requires continued engagement with scientific content, not simply retaining an old category table. Nor would a new inventory drawn from the sciences by itself establish the necessity of the categories it lists.

### 4. Critical Philosophy (Kant)

**Achievement.** Kant's Copernican revolution is the moment at which thought becomes **self-conscious about its own categories**. The forms of intuition (space, time) and the categories of the understanding (substance, cause, reciprocity, etc.) are recognized as the *a priori* contributions of the subject — the former conditioning how objects are given, the latter how they are thought as objects of experience. With this, the antinomies of old metaphysics are diagnosed: they arise when reason treats the totality of appearances as a whole existing in itself. Kant unifies the achievements of the prior two standpoints: empiricism's content (intuition) and metaphysics' categories (concept) — *"thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind"* (A51/B75). He establishes the indispensable principle: **the object of knowledge is constituted in the act of knowing it.** Necessity and universality are grounded not in the accumulation of observations but in the conditions of possible experience.

**Contradiction.** Hegel accepts the critical demand that thought examine its own forms, but disputes the limits Kant places on their knowledge of truth. Three objections define the advance he seeks beyond Kant.

- Knowledge through the categories is confined to objects of possible experience, not **the thing in itself**. Hegel's objection is that the supposedly inaccessible *Ding an sich* is itself a product of thought: abstract from every determination, and its emptiness follows from the abstraction, not from a failed attempt to discover hidden content (*Enc.* §44; §23). Kant's negative noumenon is a limiting concept, not a positively known hidden world; Hegel contests the finality of that limit rather than exposing a simple admission by Kant that we know what he says is unknowable.
- Kant offers a derivation of the **table of categories**: the same functions that unify representations in judgments unify the manifold of intuition. His metaphysical deduction addresses their origin; his transcendental deduction vindicates their objective validity. Hegel's objection concerns the starting inventory of judgment-forms: deriving categories from that table does not yet derive why precisely those forms are necessary (*Enc.* §42; §27). The dispute is about the adequacy of the offered derivation, not the absence of any attempt at one.
- Kant gives the antinomies a positive critical use: their opposed proofs support the transcendental ideality of appearances (*Critique*, Antinomy, VII). Hegel credits the discovery of necessary contradiction, but demands that it become the **positive engine** of the determinations' own development, rather than terminate in the critical restriction on their use (*Enc.* §48). The difference is between two accounts of what contradiction establishes, not between discovering a result and merely posting a prohibition.

For Hegel, the remaining task is therefore to make the content and connection of the categories themselves the object of criticism, not only their applicability to objects. This renews the challenge to school-logic on a higher plane. It does not erase Kant's derivations or establish Hegel's alternative in advance; that alternative must show the self-articulation it demands.

**Resolution.** Hegel's **dialectics** undertakes to exhibit contradiction as **the very life of the matter**, developing determinations rather than accepting their fixed separation as final. The opposition of subject and object must itself be comprehended, not simply abolished by declaring thought unlimited. Hegel assigns the derivation of this standpoint to the *Phenomenology*; the *Logic* presupposes it (§02). Our introductory comparison states the task and its claimed ground; it does not replace that derivation.

**What is preserved.** The entire critical achievement — that the categories are forms of thought, that the object is constituted in cognition, that intuition and concept must be unified, and the table of categories itself, now to be re-derived from within.

### 5. Dialectics (the Speculative Standpoint)

**Achievement.** Dialectical or speculative thought undertakes to give logic a **method adequate to its content**. Instead of picking up its forms from outside, it must exhibit their immanent development. Hegel's opening supplies the first case: *Being*, taken purely, shows itself indistinguishable from *Nothing*; their truth is *Becoming*; Becoming determines itself into *Determinate Being*. The undertaking extends through quality, quantity, measure, essence, the concept, the idea — a system whose connections must be earned in the exposition, not certified by this preview. Contradiction, as the self-undermining of a determination claimed to stand alone, is the **principle of all movement and life**, not a permission to assert incompatible propositions indiscriminately. What was an externally arranged table is to become a **necessary sequence**.

On this standpoint, the earlier achievements are to be preserved through their transformation:

- **Old metaphysics is rehabilitated**: thought-determinations are again determinations of the thing — but now because subject and object have been shown to share a single conceptual life, not by dogmatic assumption.
- **Empiricism is vindicated**: the concrete content is essential; the Idea is concrete or it is nothing — but this content is no longer the merely given, it is the determinate self-articulation of the universal.
- **Criticism is fulfilled**: the categories are forms of thought, but precisely *because* they are forms of thought they are forms of being — there is no thing-in-itself behind them, because the antithesis of "for us" and "in itself" is itself a posited and overcome moment.

**The contribution to the scientific paradigm.** Where school-logic examines rules of inference independently of their particular subject-matter, dialectic asks how science can comprehend the development of its own concepts. The contribution proposed here is science's **own self-understanding**: its concepts come under pressure from their content, and their achievements can be preserved in richer concepts that mediate what that pressure brings out. (In the sphere of Being that pressure takes the form of a passage into an opposite; it is not the only form the movement takes — see §27.) Some scientific advances invite such a reading — the passage from substance to function, from particle to field. The reading is a reconstruction, and these sequences are this synopsis's arrangement rather than divisions Hegel supplies for the sciences; §03 distinguishes the logical sequence from the *Philosophy of Nature*'s divisions, and both from a periodization of the sciences. The claim is categorial, not a deduction of physics: the Logic supplies forms for examining conceptual change, not a law of scientific history or a substitute for evidence.

The scientific method, on this view, is not exhausted by rules for inference, testing, and explanation, however indispensable those procedures remain (§03). The further question is how they answer to the **immanent logic of the matter under investigation**, as that logic is reconstructed in thought. Method is then not a scheme fixed in advance of every content; it must develop with the content it seeks to comprehend.

## Coda: The Ground of What Follows

We have reconstructed an *outer* itinerary of problems that lead toward speculative science, not deduced its standpoint from five historical stages. What remains is to enter its *inner* movement: to follow the categories from the simplest determination (*Being*) and examine whether their development earns the necessity the opening demanded.

The next installment will take up the question with which Hegel's *Science of Logic* itself begins: **"With What must Science Begin?"** — and will show why the answer to that question is already the first step of the method.
