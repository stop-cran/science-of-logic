# The Determinations of Reflection: Identity, Difference, Contradiction

*The twelfth installment of Section III — and the second in the **Doctrine of Essence**, read closely. §20 reached **determining reflection**, whose result is a **determination of reflection** (*Reflexionsbestimmung*): positedness that also subsists through reflection into itself. Here that structure receives its particular development. **Identity** is initially essence's whole negative self-relation, not the abstract sameness expressed by A = A; as a determination within that movement, it contains and contrasts with **difference**. Difference is first **absolute difference**, self-related negativity; then **diversity** (*Verschiedenheit*), whose moments stand indifferently apart and receive **likeness** and **unlikeness** through comparison; then **opposition** (*Gegensatz*), in which the **positive** and **negative** contain their relation to each other within themselves. Their completed self-subsistence becomes **contradiction** (*Widerspruch*): each excludes the other whose inclusion constitutes its own independence. The self-exclusion is also self-sublation; the opposed determinations **go to ground** (*zu Grunde gehen*), and their unity is **Ground** (*Grund*), self-subsistence restored through the negation of its own positedness. Hegel connects this development with the traditional **laws of thought** and with movement and life. We distinguish the categorial argument from the further claims made for it: neither the insufficiency of a tautology as an account of thought nor the dependence of opposed determinations is by itself a refutation of formal logic, and the logical development does not certify every mathematical or physical application.*

---

## I. Where We Are: From Reflection to Its Determinations

The previous installment developed reflection as essence's mode of movement — not *passing-over* into an other and vanishing, as in Being, but *shining* within itself, staying with itself in its distinctions. It ended at **determining reflection**, the unity of positing and external reflection, whose result is a **positedness that is at once a reflection-into-self**. Such a determinacy is a **determination of reflection** (*Reflexionsbestimmung*): it subsists through self-relation while containing its relation to other, not by becoming an independently immediate being. Hegel opens the new chapter on exactly that note: "Reflection is determinate reflection; hence essence is determinate essence, or it is an essentiality" — reflection being "the showing of the illusory being of essence within essence itself" (*SL* §860).

The determinations must now be developed, not merely named. Hegel announces the order: essence is first "simple self-relation, pure identity"; difference is then external or indifferent as diversity, and opposed as opposition; finally, "as contradiction, the opposition is reflected into itself and withdrawn into its ground" (*SL* §861). The chapter's main exposition follows that movement. Its Remarks examine the propositional forms in which some of these determinations have been made into **laws of thought**: identity, non-contradiction, diversity, and excluded middle. These are not three laws paired one-to-one with three categories. Non-contradiction is discussed with identity, the law of diversity with diversity, and excluded middle in a Remark following the resolution of contradiction. The final Remark then proposes contradiction itself as a counter-law (*SL* §955).

Hegel reports that the traditional propositions ranked as "the universal laws of thought" and were taken to be "absolute in themselves and incapable of proof" (*SL* §862). His objection concerns their independent and unconditional standing. Taken as exhaustive determinations, identity excludes difference, while diversity excludes bare identity; a list conceals the relation between them (*SL* §§867–868). But the proposition that something is identical with itself does not, by its formal meaning alone, deny that it differs from something else. The stronger exclusion belongs to the absolutized determination Hegel is criticizing. Our task is therefore double: reconstruct how he develops the determinations, and judge what that development establishes about the laws. An account of their categorial presuppositions is not automatically a proof that their use in inference is invalid.

## II. Identity

Essence is initially **identity with itself**, but it is identity reached through the sublation of immediacy. Its negativity is not an operation performed on an otherwise untouched substrate; it is essence's own being (*SL* §869). Accordingly, this identity is "not that equality-with-self that being or even nothing is, but the equality-with-self that has brought itself to unity," and is expressly "not abstract identity" (*SL* §870). We must not replace it at the outset with *A = A* and then try to extract the missing movement from the formula.

Hegel first considers identity as "essence itself, not yet a determination of it, reflection in its entirety" (*SL* §872). Reflection distinguishes, but the other it posits does not remain an independently given other: its non-being is also the sublation of the distinguishing. This is not the absence of differentiation; it is differentiation whose negative relation returns into itself. Hegel calls it "a distinguishing by which nothing is distinguished, but which immediately collapses within itself", and therefore "self-related, reflected difference, or pure, absolute difference" (*SL* §872). Identity is the return of this distinguishing into itself, not an inert sameness beside it.

The transition turns on the difference between the **whole movement** and a **moment within it**. As the whole, identity is self-related negativity; as simple self-equality contrasted with that negativity, it is one determination. Hegel writes that identity "is identity only as internal repulsion," a repulsion which takes itself back into itself (*SL* §873). Reflection thus posits its own identity as a moment: only in this sense is it "identity as such, as determination of simple equality with itself in contrast to absolute difference" (*SL* §874). The argument inherits reflection's negative self-relation from §20. It does not infer negativity from the bare assertion that a thing is itself, nor does it yet introduce the developed excluding reflection of opposition.

The Remarks then test **abstract identity** and its formula. Hegel grants that *A = A* "has no content and leads no further" (*SL* §875). Asked what a plant is, "A plant is a plant" is admitted to be true, but is also judged to say nothing (*SL* §879). The propositional form promises a determination and returns only what was given: "A is is a beginning that hints at something different to which an advance is to be made; but this different something does not materialise; A is—A; the difference is only a vanishing" (*SL* §881). For Hegel, even this barren expression exhibits a distinguishing and return which the abstract identity was supposed to exclude.

The achievement and the limit should be kept together. Hegel's charge is that the utterance undertakes to give a determination and does the opposite: "Such identical talk therefore contradicts itself" (*SL* §880). He has just reported the admission that the statement is true; the failure concerns its promise to say what the thing is and its claim to express absolute truth. That failure does not by itself make the identity statement formally false. Nor should the vanishing distinction in the proposition be recast as Hegel's claiming that the two occurrences of *A* designate non-identical objects. His stronger verdict — that the law, taken to express abstract identity against difference, "is not a law of thought, but rather the opposite of it" (*SL* §884) — must be assessed at that stated scope. The positive result is that essence's identity cannot be exhausted by a formula abstracted from the differentiation through which it is constituted.

Hegel treats **non-contradiction** here too: "A cannot at the same time be A and not-A" is the negative expression of identity (*SL* §882). The expression names *A* and its other, distinguishes them, and refers them to one *A*. Hegel therefore calls it synthetic rather than merely analytic, and says that it contains "absolute inequality, contradiction per se" (*SL* §883). That is his interpretation of the reflective movement expressed in the prohibition, not a formal inference from mentioning opposed predicates to asserting them together. The distinction will matter again when contradiction itself has been developed.

## III. Difference in Itself — the Absolute Difference

**Absolute difference** is not yet a comparison of two already given things. It is the negativity internal to reflection: "the negativity which reflection has within it, the nothing which is said in enunciating identity" (*SL* §885). Hegel insists on its simplicity. In the absolute difference of *A* and not-*A*, "it is the simple not which, as such, constitutes it" (*SL* §886). Unlike the immediate otherness of Being, this difference has its relation to its other within itself.

As **self-related difference**, it distinguishes itself from itself. Its other is identity; yet the difference which contains this other also remains identical with itself. Difference therefore contains identity and its relation to identity, just as identity contains the negative movement of its own return. "Difference is the whole and its own moment, just as identity equally is its whole and its moment" (*SL* §887). They are not interchangeable names: each is the whole relation under a distinct determination and each is also a moment of that relation. Hegel already calls this "determinate difference"; it does not lose itself in an external other, but contains identity within itself (*SL* §888).

Why, then, **diversity**? Because each moment is not merely related to the other but reflected into itself. Identity is self-relation; difference, as reflected difference, is self-relation too. Considered on this side, each has its own self-identity and the moments stand indifferently apart. Hegel makes precisely this connection: difference, "in that it has two moments that are themselves reflections-into-self, is diversity" (*SL* §889). Their self-relation supplies the standing through which they can appear independent of their relation. This is a further determination of reflection, not an accidental degradation of an otherwise finished unity.

## IV. Diversity — Difference Gone External

In **diversity** (*Verschiedenheit*), identity is not related to difference, nor difference to identity, in their own respective self-relations. The distinguished moments are therefore not determined *against* one another; their difference is external to them (*SL* §§890–892). Reflection has become external to itself. Its two sides are the self-relation of the diverse and the externally posited determination of their relation (*SL* §§893–894).

That external identity is **likeness**, and external difference is **unlikeness**. Each thing is taken as what it is independently of whether it is like another; likeness or unlikeness is "the verdict of a third party distinct from the two things" (*SL* §895). Comparison moves between these determinations, assigning likeness in one respect and unlikeness in another (*SL* §§896–897). Two objects may, for example, be alike in colour and unlike in weight. That description does not assert both likeness and unlikeness in the same respect.

Hegel's objection concerns the attempt to make **likeness and unlikeness themselves** mutually indifferent. Each means what it does through its distinction from the other: "both essentially have this relation and have no meaning apart from it" (*SL* §898). If their relation is suppressed, each is only an abstract self-relation, each simply like itself; the distinction between them disappears. The comparer's movement between them initially seems an external act, but the connection belongs to the determinations compared: "this negative unity is, in fact, the very nature of likeness and unlikeness" (*SL* §899). Each contains its relation to its other, instead of receiving that relation as an indifferent addition.

Hegel argues that the opposed determinations' own self-reference both distinguishes them and returns them into the same relation. Consequently, the supposedly independent diverse terms no longer provide a basis indifferent to that relation: their difference is posited within it. His conclusion is that "Diversity, whose indifferent sides are just as much simply and solely moments of one negative unity, is opposition" (*SL* §901). This reconstructs the transition under the account of reflection already developed. It does not abolish the legitimate distinction between colour and weight, or prove that likeness in one respect entails unlikeness in that same respect.

The **law of diversity** brings the issue into propositional form. Merely saying that things are many supplies no determinate difference between them. The stronger claim, that no two things are completely alike, requires unlikeness as well as likeness (*SL* §§903–904). Hegel connects it with **Leibniz**, retelling the search for two identical leaves, but insists that the proposition "surely stands in need of proof; it cannot be set up as an immediate proposition" (*SL* §905). The required proof would develop identity into difference and determinate difference; he points back to the reflection of external diversity into itself. His verdict is stronger than a demand to connect two principles: "This involves the dissolution and nullity of the law of diversity" (*SL* §906). Likeness and unlikeness are moments of one relation; diversity has passed into opposition. We have followed that categorial argument, not independently demonstrated a distinguishable physical property for every pair of things. The claim that the terms of comparison require one another and the universal application of Leibniz's principle remain distinguishable questions.

Hegel expressly refuses to treat the qualification by different respects as a resolution. It removes the unity from the things only to relocate it in the external reflection that distinguishes and relates both moments in one activity: the contradiction is "not resolved but merely shifted elsewhere" (*SL* §907). Our distinction of colour and weight does not answer his question about the unity of comparing. We accept that comparison contains and relates the two determinations; we do not accept that their occurrence in one activity, by itself, establishes a formal contradiction. His stronger verdict therefore requires the reflective argument about the determinations' own negative self-relation, not merely their presence in a single comparison. A claim of contradiction in an actual comparison must exhibit that structure; its two predicates alone do not.

## V. Opposition — the Positive and the Negative

**Opposition** (*Gegensatz*) completes determinate reflection as the unity of identity and diversity (*SL* §908). A textual distinction matters here: the online English transcription reads "determinate rejection" and "identity and difference"; the [German text](http://www.zeno.org/Philosophie/M/Hegel,+Georg+Wilhelm+Friedrich/Wissenschaft+der+Logik/Erster+Teil.+Die+objektive+Logik/Zweites+Buch.+Die+Lehre+vom+Wesen/Erster+Abschnitt%3A+Das+Wesen+als+Reflexion+in+ihm+selbst/Zweites+Kapitel%3A+Die+Wesenheiten+oder+die+Reflexionsbestimmungen/B.+Der+Unterschied/3.+Der+Gegensatz) says *bestimmte Reflexion* and *die Einheit der Identität und der Verschiedenheit*. Our exposition follows those distinctions; it does not present a silently repaired English sentence as Miller's wording. Difference was already determinate at §888. Its completion here is the reflection of likeness and unlikeness into themselves, each containing its other as a moment of its own self-relation (*SL* §§909–910).

The **positive** is self-likeness containing its reference to unlikeness; the **negative** is unlikeness containing its reference to likeness. Each is both positedness and reflection into itself, but differently determined: the positive reflects positedness into self-likeness, the negative into unlikeness (*SL* §911). Their self-subsistence is consequently mediated, not an immediate independence subsequently connected by an observer (*SL* §912).

Hegel develops **three aspects**. First, they are simply opposed moments of one relation: each "is, only in so far as the other is", and equally "is, in so far as the other is not" (*SL* §913). Second, each is reflected into itself and indifferent to being assigned either sign; on this side, positive and negative can be interchanged (*SL* §914). Third, the relation to other is taken back into each as its own determination. The positive is the "not-opposite, the sublated opposition, but as a side of the opposition itself"; the negative is the opposition based on itself against the positive (*SL* §§915–917). Their exclusion of each other now constitutes what each is **in and for itself**, not an external designation (*SL* §918). This third aspect, not mere interdependence, prepares contradiction.

The arithmetical Remark makes the differences tangible. An eastward journey and an equal westward journey cancel as displacement; liabilities are negative for the debtor but positive assets for the creditor. The assignment depends on a standpoint (*SL* §922). Hegel also distinguishes the cancellation of opposed magnitudes from their common magnitude and from the sum of their separately considered magnitudes (*SL* §§923–925). These are distinct relations, not permission to substitute one numerical result for another in an unchanged calculation.

Multiplication raises a further question. Hegel holds that the factors, as unit and amount, have a qualitative relation, and that the negative must here be taken as intrinsically negating rather than merely as the interchangeable opposite of the positive (*SL* §§928–929). He explains the positive product of two negatives through negation of negation (*SL* §930). The explanation has a definite target: merely naming two magnitudes as opposites does not yet explain their operation as factors. But the logical formula alone does not establish the rule of multiplication. In ordinary algebra the rule follows from distributivity and additive inverses: *a · (b + (−b)) = 0* gives *a · (−b) = −(a · b)*, and applying the same requirement to *a + (−a)* yields *(−a) · (−b) = a · b*. This is a mathematical warrant, not the derivation of those operations from reflection. Hegel's categorial interpretation and the algebraic proof answer related but different questions.

The relational examples in the later Remark are equally instructive: above and below, right and left, father and son (*SL* §960). A father can exist apart from the son-relationship, but then he is being considered simply as a man, not under the determination *father*. The relation is constitutive of the determination, not necessarily of every existence of its bearer. Hegel takes these cases further, toward contradiction, because their opposed terms are both negatively related and self-related in the same respect. That further step must be exhibited; dependence alone does not make every relation contradictory. Nor does calling magnetic poles or electric charges positive and negative establish that their physics realizes the full development. A scientific application needs its own account of the relevant relation, not merely a pair of opposed names.

## VI. Contradiction — and Its Resolution into Ground

In the completed opposition, each side contains its other and is independent through that inclusion; yet each excludes that very other. Hegel states the mechanism exactly: the self-subsistent determination "that contains the opposite determination, and is self-subsistent in virtue of this inclusion, at the same time also excludes it; in its self-subsistence, therefore, it excludes from itself its own self-subsistence" (*SL* §933). The point is not simply that one thing needs another. Each is the **whole excluding relation**, not one self-contained item related to an external item; its exclusion therefore reaches the relation constituting its own standing. Because the exclusion is one act of distinguishing, "each side in its own self excludes itself" (*SL* §934).

The positive posits its identity by excluding the negative, but thereby makes itself the negative of what it excludes — its own opposite (*SL* §935). The negative, in turn, is identical with itself precisely in opposing identity; its exclusion of identity excludes the self-relation through which it is negative (*SL* §§936–938). The asymmetry is real: "the positive is only implicitly this contradiction, whereas the negative is the contradiction posited" (*SL* §937). Contradiction is thus the developed self-exclusion of these determinations, not a conclusion licensed by the word *relation* wherever it occurs.

The first result is the ceaseless vanishing of the opposites, "the null" (*SL* §939). But the same excluding reflection is **positing reflection**. What it negates is its own positedness: "Their own negation of themselves sublates the positedness of the self-subsistence. It is this which in truth perishes in contradiction" (*SL* §940). At first each side's self-identity still stands as a determination opposed to the other. Excluding the other makes that identity itself a negative relation, a positedness; exclusion therefore negates its own way of standing (*SL* §941).

The positive result depends on this **second negation**, not on adding a reconciler after the collapse. The negation is no longer merely directed toward an immediate other: it sublates the positedness into which excluding reflection has converted itself. Hegel therefore calls it "a uniting with itself, the positive unity with itself", and concludes: "Self-subsistence is thus through its own negation a unity returned into itself, since it returns into itself through the negation of its own positedness" (*SL* §942). The determinations are preserved, but no longer as self-subsistent opposites standing apart from the unity of their own movement (*SL* §943).

This is **Ground** (*Grund*). Opposition **goes to ground** — *zu Grunde gehen*, both perishing and withdrawing into its ground. Hegel calls ground "this completed self-subsistence", in which opposition and its contradiction are "as much abolished as preserved" (*SL* §945). Ground is not a substrate newly inferred from two incompatible properties. It is essence determining itself, excluding its own positedness and remaining self-identical in that negation. The result preserves determination as self-sublating determination; it does not merely remove the sides' independence and leave their relation unexplained.

The order of priority also changes. As the starting point, opposition appears first and ground is its result; as resolved opposition, it is shown to be essence's posited determination, not an independently immediate foundation of essence (*SL* §944). This is logical priority, not a later act manufacturing an earlier reality. The movement develops the unity of positing and presupposing examined in §20 in this determinate form. At the opening of the next chapter Hegel distinguishes pure reflection from ground's **real mediation**, containing reflection as sublated reflection (*SL* §967). Ground is a further determination of mediation, not its first occurrence.

The Remarks draw wider conclusions. Hegel proposes the counter-law that "everything is inherently contradictory" (*SL* §955), and calls contradiction "the root of all movement and vitality" (*SL* §956). His claim about motion is literal and strong: "Something moves, not because at one moment it is here and at another there, but because at one and the same moment it is here and not here, because in this 'here', it at once is and is not" (*SL* §958). Life likewise holds together self-containedness and deficiency, and is the power to sustain contradiction (*SL* §959). These are Hegel's claims about actuality, not merely metaphors for revising a description. Their application must still be tested; the general derivation does not exempt them from that burden.

Nor does resolution promise that every contradictory existent survives. Hegel expressly distinguishes living unity's capacity to sustain contradiction from an existent incapable of doing so, which falls to the ground (*SL* §959). A thing can be contradiction resolved within its own sphere while that sphere is itself finite and contradictory in relation to a further ground (*SL* §963). Ground is therefore neither a guarantee of empirical preservation nor a final reconciliation beyond all further determination.

## VII. Three Misreadings, Answered Directly

**The first is the licence-for-incoherence misreading**: if contradiction is essential, any assertion and its negation may be accepted without further work. Hegel's procedure does not permit this. It requires the particular determination to generate its own contrary and the resolution to preserve the content of that movement. But it would be equally misleading to protect him by saying that he never challenges non-contradiction, or that his contradiction is only a process and not something asserted. He does challenge the law's unrestricted authority, and he explicitly asserts contradiction in the same moment and respect. The question is whether the relevant argument warrants that claim.

The law of excluded middle requires the same distinction of questions. In its ordinary formal reading, either a predicate or its negation applies. Hegel dismisses that reading as trivial rather than false, contrasting it with the important claim that everything is an opposite, positive or negative (*SL* §953). It is against this stronger opposition-reading that he identifies the common *A* underlying *+A* and *−A* as the third the law excluded (*SL* §954). His positive result is the unity within which the opposed determinations subsist, developed more profoundly as ground. But the substrate of opposed predicates is not, merely as their substrate, a third truth-value or a case satisfying neither predicate nor its negation. Consequently, that result does not by itself refute excluded middle in its formal sense. This bound preserves Hegel's own distinction rather than answering a blanket rejection he has not made here: his argument criticizes the adequacy of a bare alternative as an account of the relation's unity.

Motion supplies a corresponding scientific test. In the ordinary kinematic representation *x(t) = v · t*, with *v ≠ 0*, the object has one position at each time and a nonzero velocity; *[x(t + h) − x(t)] / h = v* for every nonzero *h*. This represents motion without asserting both occupancy and non-occupancy of the same position at the same time. It does not derive motion's categorial conditions, and Hegel can still challenge the adequacy of representing movement through such determinations. But that further challenge requires an argument; the representation cannot be dismissed as inconsistent merely because its instantaneous position is determinate. Our conclusion is bounded: the derivation of contradiction within reflected opposition is not itself a demonstration of the simultaneous here-and-not-here asserted in *SL* §958.

**The second is the empty-tautology misreading**, in two forms: either identity is *A = A* and therefore philosophically empty, or that formula is sufficient to express the whole truth of identity. Both confuse the formula with essence's negative self-relation. A valid identity statement can be indispensable in inference while failing to explain how a content is determined. Conversely, the reflective determination contains differentiation and return rather than merely repeating a sign. Hegel's statement that "truth is complete only in the unity of identity with difference" (*SL* §876) concerns this fuller account. Preserving the formal achievement need not make it the final account; exceeding its explanatory scope need not abolish its validity.

**The third is the everything-is-relative misreading**: if fixed determinations disclose their opposites, any alternative is as good as any other. The chapter instead demands **determinate resolution**. Ground follows, in Hegel's argument, because excluding reflection negates its own positedness, not because any difficulty may be renamed a higher unity. Nor may an investigator force a pair of empirical differences into opposition just to obtain a dialectical result. The relation, the self-exclusion, and the positive content of the resolution must each be established. Hegel's injunction that the truth of reflective determinations lies in their relation to one another (*SL* §951) is a demand for that work, not a substitute for it.

## VIII. What the Method Did Here — Observations

The first observation is that **the determinations are reconstructed in their movement rather than merely collected**. Identity as negative self-relation becomes a moment of the differentiation it contains; the self-relation of the moments lets them stand indifferently apart; their determination through one another develops opposition; its excluding self-subsistence negates itself and returns into ground. These are distinct transitions. The general doubleness of reflected determinacy established in §20 does not prove them in advance. Nor are the traditional laws thereby all derived as formally valid theorems, or all refuted as false. Their categorial content, their isolated claim to completeness, and their use within a determinate form of inference are different objects of assessment.

The second observation is that **the relation to the other belongs to the determination itself**. Comparison initially seems to attach likeness and unlikeness from outside; developed opposition contains the reference to other within each side's self-relation. Hegel's examples also test apparently settled assignments. Virtue is not simply positive beside vice: it is internally conflict; innocence is not yet either good or evil, although this indifference itself has a determination (*SL* §950). In the same passage he assigns negativity to light's expansion and activity, and positivity to darkness's undifferentiated self-identity. He also claims that light's relation to darkness determines colour. That physical claim does not follow merely from the logical reversal of the labels; it requires an optical account. The example illustrates the intended movement without certifying the physics.

The third observation is that **contradiction must be developed, not merely celebrated**. The decisive step is the exclusion of what constitutes a determination's own self-subsistence. Mere diversity, dependence, conflict, and an inconsistent assertion are not interchangeable descriptions of that step. Hegel distinguishes ordinary thinking, which moves between related determinations without grasping their transition, from intelligent reflection, which grasps their contradiction, and from thinking reason, which develops diversity into essential opposition (*SL* §961). For our use of the method, the criterion is therefore not whether an opposition can be found, but whether its necessity and its determinate resolution can be shown.

## IX. What Comes Next

With **ground**, the opposed determinations have their standing as moments of essence's self-mediation. The relation has to be developed further:

- **§22 — Ground**: ground and grounded; form and essence, form and matter, form and content; formal, real, and complete ground; conditions and the emergence into **Existence**. A ground's name is not yet an explanation of the content it is supposed to ground.
- **Beyond**: **Appearance** — the thing and its properties, appearance and law, the essential relations — and then **Actuality**, with possibility, contingency, necessity, substance, causality, and reciprocity. Ground is not the completed account of these relations; their development is still required.

## Coda

Identity does not lose its necessity because it is not the whole truth; difference does not become intelligible by being left beside it. The chapter follows their relation until opposition claims self-subsistence through the very inclusion it excludes. There contradiction is not an objection brought from outside: it is the determination's own self-exclusion. Its resolution is equally specific — the negation of that positedness returns self-subsistence into its ground. *The methodological gain is not permission to contradict, nor a promise that every conflict saves what enters it. It is the demand to show how a determination produces its own limit, what its negation preserves, and why this result — rather than an arbitrarily chosen successor — follows.*
