# Understanding and Reason — and the Dialectical Reconstruction of Scientific Practice

*The three sides of the logical (Verstand, Dialektik, Spekulation), and what becomes of induction, hypothesis, experiment, and mathematical formalism when science is grasped dialectically.*

---

## I. Two Faculties, or One Activity in Two Moments?

The distinction between **Understanding** (*Verstand*) and **Reason** (*Vernunft*) is older than Kant, but the version Hegel inherits and contends with is Kant's. There Understanding is the faculty of distinct concepts, fixed definitions, sharp boundaries; Reason is the faculty of Ideas, of the unconditioned, of totality — a specification that belongs to the critical philosophy and should not be read back into the tradition at large. Kant assigns to Understanding the legitimate constitution of objects of experience and to Reason a regulative role only; for Kant, Reason cannot constitute its objects, and when it tries (in the antinomies) it falls into contradiction.

Hegel accepts the distinction but transforms its meaning. Understanding and Reason are not two separate faculties; they are **two moments of the one activity of thought**. The Encyclopaedia Logic (§79) gives the canonical formulation: every logical content has three sides —

1. **The side of the Understanding** (*das Verständige*) — abstract, fixed, holding determinations apart in clean opposition.
2. **The dialectical or negatively-rational side** (*das Dialektische*) — the immanent self-cancellation of these fixed determinations; their passing-over into their opposites.
3. **The speculative or positively-rational side** (*das Spekulative*) — the grasp of the unity of opposed determinations as moments of a richer concrete whole.

These are **not three separate operations** performed by three different faculties on three different occasions; they are the three constitutive moments of any genuine act of thinking. A concept thought through displays all three — though not always in the same shape, and the difference matters. In the sphere of **Being** the sequence is the familiar one: the determination is first held fast as itself, then shows itself to demand its opposite, then is grasped together with its opposite in a higher unity. That is the introductory pattern, and it is not the only one. In the Doctrine of the Concept the universal does not pass over into an independently opposed determination at all; its self-specification is, in Hegel's words, "**not a transition**, for this occurs only in the sphere of **being**; it is **creative power**" (§27). The three moments are constant throughout; the manner in which the second arises is not. To stop at the first moment is to give us *abstract* understanding; to stop at the second is *skepticism*; to traverse all three is *speculative cognition*.

This trinity is the architecture of every dialectical step we examined in synopsis 02, and it can be traced as well in the advances of the empirical sciences, whether or not the practitioners are aware of it. What is claimed is a structural kinship, not that every advance conforms to it.

## II. The Indispensable Achievement of the Understanding

It is essential to insist, against the common caricature of Hegel as an "irrationalist" or enemy of clear thinking, that **the Understanding is indispensable** and that Hegel says so emphatically. Without the Understanding's labor of fixing distinctions, there is no thought at all — only the inarticulate flux of representation. Without distinct concepts, there are no objects; without sharp definitions, no science.

The Understanding's accomplishments are the very substance of civilization:

- It carves the world into kinds and species.
- It draws the line between cause and effect, substance and accident, subject and predicate.
- It separates the variables of an experiment so that one can be held constant while another varies.
- It abstracts the mathematical structure of motion from the qualitative diversity of moving things.
- It is the principle of all law, of all classification, of all measurement, of all formal proof.

Hegel goes further: in the Preface to the *Phenomenology* he calls the activity of separating — the Understanding's power of holding determinations fixed and apart — the most astonishing and mightiest of powers, or rather the absolute power; for it is the power by which thought arrests the flow of intuition and lifts a determination into the universality of the concept. Without this arrest, there is nothing to think about and nothing to think with. The whole edifice of mathematical natural science — Galileo, Newton, Lavoisier, Maxwell, Mendel — is the work of the Understanding operating with magnificent rigor on its proper terrain.

The dialectical critique of the Understanding is not a critique of its work, but of its **finality** — and the fault must be located correctly from the start. In the Doctrine of the Concept Hegel insists on the **infinite force of the understanding** in splitting the concrete, and assigns the *fixity* of its determinations not to the understanding's own work but to a **subjective impotence of reason** (§27); the usual practice of setting understanding against reason as two opposed faculties he rejects outright. What goes wrong is that the determinations are held as *finite* and not carried further — absolutized, treated as the last word, with the further moments of dialectic and speculation refused. Thinking that stops there is what Hegel calls *abstract* or *one-sided* understanding, the dogmatic counterpart of the very metaphysics it imagines itself to have left behind. But the stopping is reason failing to go on, not the understanding overreaching.

## III. The Negative-Rational (Dialectical) Moment

The dialectical moment is the discovery that the determinations of the Understanding, taken at their word, **cancel themselves**. This is not skepticism — skepticism stops at the cancellation and concludes that thought cannot reach truth. Dialectic recognizes the cancellation as the **immanent self-criticism of a finite determination** and reads it as a positive result: the indication that this determination is one-sided and points beyond itself.

Examples are everywhere in the history of science. The Logic here locates a categorial *form*; the sciences supply illustration, not derivation, and the kinship pointed to below is structural.

- **The simple and the composite.** Atomism declares matter to consist of indivisible parts. Pursued, this generates contradiction: an indivisible *part* must still occupy space and so be divisible; or, if truly without parts, it is no longer matter. The dialectical result is that "simple" and "composite" cannot be absolutely opposed — actual matter is always the unity of both. Later physics has not settled the question in Hegel's favour and should not be enlisted as though it had: the Standard Model's quarks and leptons are treated as **elementary**, with no known internal structure, and neither entanglement nor field description shows them to be composite. What has not survived is the narrower thing the atomists were after — the simple part as an independently subsisting little body, self-contained and merely externally related to the rest. Fields, bound states, and non-separable quantum systems have displaced *that*, and the displacement illustrates the categorial point without vindicating it, since physics reached it on its own grounds.

- **Particle and wave.** Two perfectly clear concepts of the Understanding, mutually exclusive on their own terms, turn out — when the matter is pressed — to be moments of one and the same physical reality. The history of quantum mechanics is the dialectical odyssey by which physics was forced to think a unity that classical Understanding had pronounced impossible.

- **Continuity and discreteness.** The calculus of Hegel's day had no settled foundation, and its most widely used formulations rested on an "infinitely small" quantity that was neither zero nor finite — a magnitude their practitioners handled with confidence and could not justify. It was not the only foundation on offer: Newton's, Leibniz's, Euler's, and Lagrange's algebraic route were all in the field, and it is Lagrange's that Hegel privileges (§16), so the infinitesimal was the sore point rather than the whole story. Hegel devotes long pages of the *Logic* to the point, and his verdict is that the contradiction is not a defect to be rationalized away but the mark of a determination that has outgrown quantity taken as indifferent magnitude. His own resolution locates the result in the **qualitative relation** that obtains within the ratio, where the vanishing terms have significance only in their relation to each other and not as magnitudes at all — a result reached still within Quantity, before Measure is arrived at (§16, §17). The later rigorization of analysis secured the calculus by an entirely different route: Cauchy made the limit primary while still working with infinitesimals, defined as variables tending to zero, and only with Weierstrass and after was the infinitesimal dispensed with altogether; the categorial point stands or falls on its own ground and is neither confirmed nor refuted by that development.

- **Cause and effect.** The Understanding draws a clean line: the cause precedes and produces, the effect follows and is produced. Pursued, the distinction collapses — though not by the route usually given. That every cause is itself the effect of an earlier cause yields only an endless **chain**, and a chain is not yet reciprocity; the regress merely repeats the relation it was supposed to undo. What bends the line into a circle is that the effect turns back upon the cause that produced it: the substance acted upon **reacts** against the original agent, which is thereby made passive in its turn, so that the *qua-cause* status is not in the thing but in the relation, which is reciprocal — *causa transit in effectum, et effectus in causam*. (§26 sets out the step in full.) The dialectical result is the move from causality to **reciprocity** (*Wechselwirkung*). What lies beyond reciprocity, however, is not a richer causal picture: as §26 sets out, the formula that each "part" is at once cause and effect of every other still moves within the relation it was meant to explain, and Hegel calls it, taken as an explanation, an empty mode of representing. The genuine advance is the passage to the **Concept**, in which the reciprocally determined terms are grasped as moments of a whole that determines itself.

In each of these the dialectical moment is **not destructive**. The Understanding's determinations are not annulled — they are exhibited as moments of a more concrete category, in which they live on with their finite validity intact. Atomism is not simply cancelled; it is refuted in the only way Hegel counts as genuine — preserved as one moment of a richer concept of matter (§27). Wave and particle are not denied; they are recognized as inseparable aspects of an entity neither can name alone. Cause and effect are not abolished; they are enclosed in the wider category of reciprocity.

This is what *Aufhebung* — sublation — actually means in scientific practice: a more adequate concept supersedes a less adequate one *by containing it as a moment*. The finite concept is not erased; it is given its place.

## IV. The Speculative or Positive-Rational Moment

The speculative moment is the apprehension of the unity that the dialectical moment has uncovered. To "speculate," in Hegel's strict sense, is to think **the determinate unity of opposed determinations** — to hold together what the Understanding has put asunder, without losing the distinction.

Speculation is therefore not vagueness, not mysticism, not a refusal of distinctions; it is the precise opposite of all these. It demands more, not less, conceptual rigor than the Understanding alone, because it requires that one keep both opposed determinations fully alive in their distinction *and* think them as moments of one whole. The whole is not their average, not their compromise, not their alternation, but their living mediation — the concrete concept of which each is a one-sided expression.

The speculative result of any genuine dialectical step is therefore always a **richer, more concrete category** than either of the opposites it has subsumed. *Becoming* is richer than Being or Nothing; *reciprocity* is richer than mere causality; *organism* is richer than mere mechanism; *life* is richer than merely physical-chemical process — without any of the lower terms being lost in the higher.

It is in this sense, and only in this sense, that Hegel's logic is a **logic of the concrete**: every higher determination is more concrete than any lower one, because it integrates more moments. The popular image of philosophy as a movement from the concrete to the abstract is here exactly inverted — *abstract* means impoverished in determinations, *concrete* means rich in determinations, and the development of thought is a continual concretion, not a continual abstraction.

## V. The Reconstruction of Scientific Procedures

We can now ask what becomes of the standard procedures of science — induction, hypothesis, experiment, mathematical formalism — when scientific practice is understood through this trinity of moments. The reconstruction that follows is ours: the four procedures are not a division Hegel lays down, though the three moments are his. As with the examples above, the claim is that a categorial form is discernible in the material — neither derived from it nor imposed upon it.

### 1. Induction

The classical problem of induction (Hume's problem) is that no finite collection of singular instances can yield a strictly universal law. Empiricism, pursued to its limit, undermines its own claim to universality.

Dialectically, this problem is exactly what it should be: an immanent contradiction in the *standpoint of mere empiricism*. Induction taken as a self-sufficient method is finite and one-sided; it presupposes precisely the universals it pretends to derive (the very identification of two events as "instances of the same kind" already deploys a universal). Genuine inductive science is never the mere accumulation of instances; it is the **moment** in which the universal is sought *in* the singular and the singular *in* the universal — a moment that the Understanding alone cannot complete and that requires the speculative grasp of the universal as the inner essence of the singular.

The fruitful inductive sciences (chemistry from Lavoisier, biology from Darwin, genetics from Mendel) succeed not because they have surmounted Hume but because they have, in practice, treated their data as the **manifestation of an inner essence** that the data exhibit but do not constitute. On the reading proposed here they are, despite themselves, doing dialectic: passing from appearance to essence, from the phenomenon to the law that governs it. The description is this synopsis's own, offered as an interpretation of what those sciences accomplished — not as a claim their practitioners would recognize, still less as one they need endorse.

### 2. Hypothesis

Hypothesis — the conjectural positing of a universal that would explain a body of phenomena — is the moment in which the Understanding ventures beyond the given and reaches for the inner ground. Hypothesis is the Understanding's own **dialectical impulse**: its admission that the data do not speak for themselves, that an unobserved structure must be posited to make the observed intelligible.

The dialectical critique of hypothesis is not (as in some positivisms) that it goes beyond what is observable, but that it can rest at the level of **mere conjecture** — at a universal posited externally and tested only by its consequences. Comprehension demands more: that the hypothesized universal be exhibited as the **necessary** structure of the matter, not as one possibility among many that happens to fit. This is the move from **conjecture** to a universal exhibited as necessary, and from that to the system of categories within which such a universal has its place. Each step is a step from a more external to a more immanent determination. What is at issue is the ground on which a universal is held, not its rank in any nomenclature — and emphatically not the textbook ladder on which hypotheses harden into theories and theories into laws. Theories do not mature into laws; they explain laws, and both remain revisable.

The hypothetico-deductive picture of science is not wrong; it is **abstract**. It captures a real moment of scientific practice (the Understanding's reaching beyond the given) but misses the speculative moment in which the hypothesis ceases to be merely posited and is grasped as the necessary form of its content.

### 3. Experiment

Experiment is the moment of **practical mediation**: the question put to nature in such a form that nature must answer. Bacon's great insight — that nature, "vexed" by experiment, yields what it would never reveal to passive observation — is dialectical to its core. The experimenter does not simply *receive* nature; he *constitutes* the conditions under which a determination can show itself in pure form, isolated from its accidents.

Speculatively, experiment is the recognition that the Understanding's distinctions must be **realized in the matter** before they can be confirmed in thought. To separate cause from concomitant, the experimenter physically separates them. To distinguish electric from magnetic effects, the experimenter physically isolates them. The laboratory is the place where the distinctions of the Understanding are given material realization, and where nature is put in a position to answer them — including by refusing them.

Experiment, too, shows the dialectical moment in empirical clothing, as induction and hypothesis have already done. A pattern recurs in experimental and observational inquiry alike, though not uniformly: a successful program isolates a determination, gives it sharp definition, generates a body of laws — and then, at its boundaries, runs into anomalies that the very precision of the program brings to light. (The photoelectric effect. The negative result of Michelson–Morley. Mercury's anomalous perihelion, which is an observational rather than an experimental result and belongs here for the same reason.) These anomalies are not failures of inquiry; they are its dialectical fruit — the points at which the finite determination shows itself to be finite, and the call for a more concrete category becomes audible.

### 4. Mathematical Formalism

Mathematics is, for Hegel, the supreme instrument of the Understanding — the science of pure quantitative determination, in which the Understanding's power of holding determinations fixed and apart attains its most refined form. Hegel's respect for mathematics is profound; his criticism is precise.

Mathematics treats its objects under the form of **external relation**. Quantity is the determination in which the *what* of the thing is indifferent — a number is a number, regardless of whether it counts apples or atoms or angels. This is the source of mathematics' generality and of its limit. Where the matter under investigation is genuinely indifferent to its quality (geometry of space, kinematics of mass-points), mathematical treatment is adequate to it without remainder. Where the matter has an essential qualitative determination — life, mind, history, the political — a treatment that resolves it into indifferent magnitudes leaves out what is most essential. What is criticized is that reduction and not mathematization as such; the example from modern physics below makes the distinction concrete. The mistake is not to use mathematics; it is to take the mathematized aspect for the **whole subject-matter**.

The dialectical critique of mathematical formalism, then, is not anti-mathematical. It is anti-**mathematicist**: against the doctrine that the only real properties are those that admit of measurement. Hegel anticipates here the entire long debate between the natural sciences and the human sciences (*Naturwissenschaften* vs. *Geisteswissenschaften*), and his diagnosis is exactly right: where the matter is essentially qualitative and self-determining, the mathematician's "Procrustean bed" of measure flattens it — not because measurement is wrong, but because measurement is a moment of the matter, not its essence.

Modern physics affords an illustration, though not a confirmation: several of its most fundamental determinations (spin, charge, the indistinguishability of identical particles, entanglement) resist the picture of nature as an aggregate of independently measurable magnitudes. They are carried instead by structures — group representations, symmetry constraints, states that do not factorize — in which the qualitative character of the determination resides in the form of the mathematics rather than in a quantity assigned to a part.

## VI. The History of Physics as a Dialectical Sequence

The history of physics can be read along the same lines, provided the reading is taken for what it is — a reconstruction after the fact, and not a confirmation:

- **Mechanism** (Galileo, Newton): matter as inert mass acted on by external forces. The Understanding's clearest determination of the physical. *Generates its own boundary*: the inability to account for chemical combination, in which the parts cease to be mere parts and acquire new qualitative determinations.

- **Chemism** (Lavoisier, Dalton, Mendeleev): matter as bearer of qualitative affinities, of combination and decomposition. *Generates its own boundary*: not self-organization as such — chemistry reaches that in autocatalytic and oscillating systems, and §27 leaves open how far inorganic nature realizes the form — but the self-*maintaining* whole that reproduces the conditions of its own persistence, which is life.

- **Organism** (biology, from Cuvier and Darwin onward): the living being as the self-maintaining unity of its parts, in which each part is what it is only through its function in the whole. *Generates its own boundary*: the question of consciousness, of the subject that knows.

Two cautions are owed here. First, the sequence just given is this synopsis's arrangement of the history, not a division Hegel himself lays down: the *Philosophy of Nature* proceeds through **Mechanics, Physics, and Organics** (§04), while **mechanism, chemism, and teleology** are categories of Objectivity in the Doctrine of the Concept (§27) and are not a periodization of the sciences at all. The reading above borrows the vocabulary of logical Objectivity to organize a history. Second, the direction is not a law. At each step above a more concrete category supersedes a more abstract one without abolishing it — classical mechanics is preserved as a limiting case in relativity and quantum theory; chemistry is preserved as the level at which molecular regularities operate; mechanism remains the substrate of organism — but the Logic certifies no such transition in advance, and the history affords arrests, detours, and abandoned programs as readily as advances.

## VII. Summary: Three Moments, One Activity

| Moment | Character | What it does | What it cannot do alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| The understanding-side (*das Verständige*) | Abstract conceptual fixity | Distinguishes, defines, measures, classifies, infers within fixed terms | Recognize the finitude of its own determinations |
| The dialectical side (*das Dialektische*) | Negative reason | Exhibits the immanent self-cancellation of finite determinations | Hold fast the positive result it makes available; left to itself it ends in skepticism |
| The speculative side (*das Spekulative*) | Positive reason | Grasps the unity of opposed determinations as moments of a more concrete whole | Operate without the prior labor of the Understanding |

These are not optional moments. Where thinking genuinely advances — in a science or in philosophy — this synopsis has found the three moments exhibitable at work; the claim is a reading borne out by the cases examined, not a test that every advance must be made to pass, and the Logic certifies no particular case and predicts none. What is claimed is a structure discernible in the advance, not a sequence of mental episodes that a thinker must consciously undergo (§06 sets aside any psychology of thinking). Hegel does not ask scientists to adopt a new method; what philosophy contributes is to bring the logical moments discernible in such advances to consciousness.

## VIII. The Place Won

We have now reached a point at which the contours of Hegel's contribution to the scientific paradigm can be stated with precision:

1. The forms of thought are not external rules of inference; they are the **immanent articulations of any subject-matter**, and a science's method is the self-movement of its content.
2. The Understanding's fixed determinations are **indispensable** but **finite**; their finitude is not a mark of failure but the spring of further development.
3. The advances examined here proceed by passing from less concrete to more concrete categories, **preserving** the lower at each step — a pattern the Logic exhibits in its own material, and one this synopsis finds again in those cases, not a law it imposes on the sciences.
4. There is no privileged method standing over the sciences; there is the one logical movement, exhibited in different matters at different levels of concreteness — physical, chemical, organic, mental, social, historical, philosophical.
5. The justification of any science is the **circle of its development**: the beginning is grounded by the totality that issues from it. This concerns the order of a science's categories, not its empirical content: no such circle dispenses with findings, and each science must win its own starting-point by its own means (§02).
