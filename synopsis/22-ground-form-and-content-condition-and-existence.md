# Ground: Form and Content, Condition, and the Emergence into Existence

*The thirteenth installment of Section III — the third in the **Doctrine of Essence**. §21 reached **Ground** (*Grund*) through the self-sublation of the opposed determinations of reflection. Ground contains that reflection as sublated and is essence's **real mediation** with itself. Its development begins with **absolute ground**: **form and essence**, **form and matter** (*Form und Materie*), and **form and content** (*Form und Inhalt*). The determining movement and its apparently indifferent substrate prove moments of one mediation; their posited unity gives ground a determinate **content**. **Formal ground** presents the same content as ground and grounded; **real ground** distinguishes contents but leaves their connection externally determined; **complete ground** mediates that connection through another relation which it still presupposes. Its completeness is not completed explanation: it determines itself as **conditioning mediation**. **Condition** (*Bedingung*) and ground initially confront one another as relatively unconditioned sides. Their further examination shows each to contain the whole relation; the identity of their content and form is the **absolutely unconditioned**, the **fact itself** (*die Sache selbst*). This is not a heap of conditions made sufficient by adding one more item. The fact's mediation through ground and conditions sublates its own externality, and the result is **Existence** (*Existenz*), **mediated immediacy**. Throughout, the principle of **sufficient reason** is tested rather than accepted as a guarantee that any assigned reason suffices. Hegel's critique of tautological explanation has a definite methodological force; his applications to gravitation and crystalline form must also answer to the sciences they judge. Neither a logical category nor the word complete supplies a concrete scientific explanation.*

---

## I. Where We Are: From Contradiction to Ground

The previous installment followed the determinations of reflection into their resolved unity. Each opposed side excluded the other whose inclusion constituted its own self-subsistence; this excluding reflection also negated its own positedness. The sides **went to ground** (*zu Grunde gehen*), and self-subsistence returned into itself through their self-negation. That unity, holding the opposed determinations as sublated moments, is **Ground** (*Grund*). The new chapter opens: "Essence determines itself as ground" (*SL* §964). Ground is not an untouched substrate fetched in to save the opposition. It is the determination whose meaning is to be a sublated determination: "its determining is only the sublating of its being determined" (*SL* §965).

The advance is not from an absence of **mediation** to its first occurrence. "Reflection is pure mediation in general, ground is real mediation of essence with itself" (*SL* §967). Ground contains reflection as sublated reflection; the posited thereby acquires the standing of an immediate, self-identical in its negation. Essence's return into itself is also the positing of that immediacy. This is why Hegel can say that essence proceeds only from itself in determining itself as ground (*SL* §966), without making it a ready-made thing that first exists and subsequently produces its relations.

Hegel connects this determination with the **principle of sufficient reason**, associated with **Leibniz**: *nihil est sine ratione*, nothing is without a ground. Its ordinary demand is already significant: "we must not stop at immediate determinate being or determinateness as such, but must go back from this into ground" (*SL* §969). On that reading, adding sufficient merely repeats what was demanded. Hegel nevertheless credits Leibniz with a stronger sense: mechanical causes can determine particular features without comprehending their connection as an essential whole; sufficiency for that unity requires final causes. He immediately reserves this determination for the **Concept**, where teleological ground belongs. The present chapter must not be credited in advance with that later result. It investigates what grounding establishes and what its own presuppositions still leave ungrounded.

## II. Ground and Grounded — a Relation Within Essence

A **ground** is ground of a **grounded**, and the grounded is posited through its ground. But their correlation alone does not prove either that they have identical contents or that every explanation is circular. Hegel's first distinction is more specific. Ground is essence determined as not-posited over against positedness; the grounded is positedness as such, itself reflected into identity. Their unity is essence distinguished from this mediation, and their determinate relation is **form** (*SL* §§970–974). The very distinction between the subsisting substrate and its form has thus arisen within ground, not between two independently given things.

This also explains what real mediation adds to the pure reflection examined in §20. The determinations now have subsistence, but that subsistence is itself posited; they no longer stand as the self-subsistent determinations whose contradiction §21 developed. Hegel calls ground's mediation the unity of pure and determining reflection (*SL* §972). Neither an unchanging bearer beneath the movement nor a relation with no subsisting terms captures this result. The question is how a substrate which is itself a moment of the relation can appear indifferent to the determining movement it sustains.

## III. Absolute Ground: Form and Essence, Matter, and Content

**Form and essence.** Absolute ground first articulates the distinction just reached. **Form** (*Form*) is the whole determining movement of reflection; **essence** (*Wesen*), distinguished from it, is the simple substrate in which its determinations subsist. External reflection stops at their separation, although the distinguishing itself belongs to their unity. Hegel's two formulations must be read together: "simple essence, on the other hand, is the indeterminate and inactive substrate"; yet "form has in its own identity essence, just as essence has in its negative nature absolute form" (*SL* §975). It is therefore not a question of how an independently existing form is added to an independently existing essence. Form determines essence by sublating the distinction it posits and returning into the identity that sustains its determinations (*SL* §976).

**Form and matter.** If form's identity is held as its presupposed other, essence is determined as **formless identity**: **matter** (*Materie*). This is a further determination, not a second name for essence without qualification (*SL* §§977–978). Matter is indifferent to form, but this indifference is itself determinate — the self-identity into which form withdraws. Thus "matter is a sheer abstraction"; what can be seen or felt is "a determinate matter, that is, a unity of matter and form" (*SL* §979). The claim concerns matter abstracted from every form, not the non-existence of material things.

The reciprocal presupposition is not yet the result. Form is self-subsistent and yet essentially related to matter as its subsistence: in surrendering its separate standing it becomes posited in matter, while in sublating its positedness it gives itself subsistence. Matter, conversely, is not merely a passive other waiting for a foreign determination. Its indeterminacy is the result of negating form; it contains the form it excludes, initially only implicitly. The determining of matter is therefore the movement of the same contradiction on both sides (*SL* §§980–986). Hegel's compressed demand — "matter must therefore be formed, and form must therefore materialise itself" (*SL* §980) — is to be understood through that mediation, not as a physical event in which pure shape manufactures matter.

**Form and content.** Their unity is now not merely presupposed but **posited**. Hegel names the result "formed matter, or the form that has a subsistence": **content** (*Inhalt*, *SL* §988). Content has form and matter essentially within it; as their determinate unity it also stands over against form and appears indifferent to it. This double character matters. Content is not unformed stuff upon which a ground-relation is arbitrarily stamped: the relation is also its essential form. Conversely, ground now has a content (*SL* §§989–993). This is the passage to determinate ground. Neither finite form nor finite matter, taken separately, was ground of their unity; "only their unity is their truth" (*SL* §987).

## IV. Determinate Ground: Formal, Real, and Complete

**Formal ground.** At first the content is the identical substrate of ground and grounded, while their difference belongs to the form of mediation. Each side posits and is posited through the other; each is the whole form considered from one side (*SL* §§994–996). Here — not as a premise true of every kind of explanation — Hegel states: "there is nothing in the ground that is not in the grounded, and there is nothing in the grounded that is not in the ground." He expressly restricts the sufficiency thereby obtained to this relationship (*SL* §997). The content has not yet acquired a difference corresponding to the two sides. This is **formal ground**, not the completed account of ground as such (*SL* §998).

Its explanatory defect appears when reflection stops here. The same content is handed back as its own reason, once as an immediate fact and once as an essential power. Hegel calls this "a mere formalism and empty tautology" and charges that "the sciences, especially the physical sciences, are full of tautologies of this kind which constitute as it were a prerogative of science" (*SL* §999). His examples are pointed: planetary motion explained by an attractive force defined through that motion; crystalline form explained by the molecular arrangement identified with that very form; and a plant explained by a plant-producing force. In the last case, "something is no more explained by this formalism than the nature of a plant is known when I say that it is a plant" (*SL* §1000). Our methodological inference is exact but limited: a purported explanation that adds no determination beyond its explanandum has supplied no explanatory connection.

Hegel's **physical applications** cannot be protected by calling that criterion categorial. Against Leibniz's charge that Newtonian attraction is an occult quality, he replies that it is instead too familiar, having no other content than the phenomenon (*SL* §1000). But a force law is not exhausted by a name for the observed motion. Newtonian dynamics with an inverse-square force law, masses, and initial conditions constrains trajectories; its bound nonradial solutions permit the alternation of closest and furthest approach without an added force to reverse the motion, as the radial equation examined in §19 shows. That is a conditional mathematical explanation, not a derivation of its own assumptions, but it is more than repetition. Hegel's criticism reaches the merely renamed phenomenon; extending it to that dynamical achievement requires an argument the tautology diagnosis has not supplied (§19; MIT's *Classical Mechanics*, [chapter 25, §§25.3–25.4](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter25new.pdf)).

The crystalline example requires the same discrimination. Hegel says that "the existent crystalline form is this very arrangement that is adduced as ground" (*SL* §999). If arrangement merely redescribes the form to be explained, the criticism holds. It does not follow that microscopic structure and observable morphology are the same explanatory content: modern crystallography distinguishes them, and external morphology alone does not establish crystallinity (Ripoll and Cano, [*Crystallography in a Nutshell*, §1.1](https://chem.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Analytical_Chemistry/Crystallography_in_a_Nutshell_(Ripoll_and_Cano)/01%3A_Chapters/1.01%3A_New_Page)). This distinction does not certify every structural explanation. It identifies what must be investigated before Hegel's identity-claim can be applied to one. The logical criticism supplies a question for science, not an exemption from its answers.

The methodological Remark continues through the **order of exposition**. A supposed ground is inferred from a phenomenon, then presented as an independently established principle from which that phenomenon follows. Hegel objects to "premising as ground what is in fact derived" and to mixing hypothetical determinations with what is immediately observed (*SL* §§1001–1002). His indictment includes molecules, empty interstices, centrifugal force, the ether, the separate ray of light, and electrical and magnetic matter among the "hypotheses and fictions derived from an uncritical reflection" (*SL* §1002). This is more than a demand to label hypotheses. Our assessment retains the objection to presenting inferred determinations as immediately perceived, but it does not treat inference itself as proof of fiction: each such determination must answer to its evidence. Inferring a hypothesis and testing its further consequences is not the empty double movement he diagnoses. Nor is exposition from principles necessarily a pretence that those principles were historically discovered first. The retained demand is to distinguish the source of a hypothesis, its evidence, and what its use actually explains.

**Real ground.** The transition is not merely our decision to avoid tautology by selecting a different cause. Each side of the formal relation is the whole reflected into itself; the difference of form therefore becomes a distinction within content (*SL* §1004). Ground and grounded now have distinctive contents, and "the ground is realised" (*SL* §1005). Yet the grounded contains both the determination common with its ground and a further determination. Their connection is not mediated by the common determination alone. It rests in a something which combines them; the relation determining which is essential and which is grounded remains external to the contents (*SL* §§1006–1009).

Hegel's examples show the specific insufficiency. Gravity belongs to the foundation and the house, but does not by itself determine that there is a house rather than a falling stone; further determinations are required (*SL* §1010). Punishment has several essential aspects, any one of which can be selected as its ground without grounding the others or their connection (*SL* §1012). Hence the proliferation of reasons: "for any and every thing one or more good grounds can be given, and also for its opposite" (*SL* §1013). The point is not that a partial explanation establishes nothing. It is that selection of an essential feature does not establish its sufficiency for the whole. A law may genuinely explain what follows under conditions it does not itself determine; that achievement and its limit must both be retained.

**Complete ground.** The real ground-relation, itself only externally posited, now requires a ground. A further relation contains the same determinations and their connection, restoring formal identity while retaining the real difference of content (*SL* §§1014–1015). Hegel's construction has two somethings: in the first, determinations A and B are immediately connected; in the second, B is posited through A by virtue of that original connection. Not only B but A's standing as its ground is mediated (*SL* §§1016–1018). The completeness is that of the **ground-relation**, embracing both formal and real ground; it is not the abolition of every presupposition.

Indeed, the first connection is still supported by an immediate something. Hegel calls it "only a relative ground" and denies that this is the determinations' truly absolute relation (*SL* §§1016–1017). Transferring their connection therefore does not establish a self-sufficient explanation from their mere occurrence together. The whole relation both posits and presupposes its immediacy: formal ground presupposes content, and real ground presupposes the form through which content counts as ground. The result is **conditioning mediation** (*SL* §§1019–1020). Complete ground is the transition into this problem, not another name for its solution.

## V. Condition — the Relatively and the Absolutely Unconditioned

**Condition** (*Bedingung*) is "the immediate to which the ground is related as to its essential presupposition" (*SL* §1021). It is, first, something immediate and manifold; second, material for an other, and thus meant to lose its indifferent standing; third, the presupposition in which ground has its content. Yet its being a condition initially appears external to it: what is simply there seems indifferent to the grounding that will employ it (*SL* §1022). Conditions are not here things created beforehand by a ground that needs no presupposition.

Ground and condition must therefore first be distinguished. "Something is not through its condition; its condition is not its ground" (*SL* §1023). Condition supplies the immediate material; ground is the self-relating movement of mediation. Each stands as relatively unconditioned over against the other, yet each also presupposes the other. Hegel locates the contradiction precisely: "indifferent immediacy and essential mediation, both in a single relation" (*SL* §1024). Neither a bare condition nor a ground lacking its material is the whole.

The advance is **not to collect enough external conditions**. Hegel examines the supposed independence of each side. Condition's immediate being is itself the result of self-sublating mediation; its in-itself is posited, and its conversion into a moment is not a determination merely imposed from outside. Condition thus contains the whole form of the ground-relation (*SL* §1026). Ground, conversely, is not a form whose content simply lies elsewhere: its presupposing is its own negative relation to itself, and the immediate it opposes to itself is its own moment (*SL* §1027). Their content is accordingly one, not two stocks of material externally combined (*SL* §1028). This is the positive mediation missing from a picture of scattered conditions merely accumulating.

The **absolutely unconditioned** is the identity thereby reached: "this identity of their common content and unity of form is the truly unconditioned, the fact in its own self" (*SL* §1029). The **fact itself** (*die Sache selbst*) is not a third item added to ground and condition. Those sides are its moments, and their reciprocal presupposing is the one movement by which it conditions itself (*SL* §1030). Hegel accordingly distinguishes the familiar regress from one finite condition to another from the conditioning contained in this logical relation. Its resolution is not an assertion that a sufficiently long list has no further condition. It is the sublation of the sides' standing as independently given sources of the relation.

This is Hegel's claim about the fact's own mediation, not merely a rule for arranging our knowledge. Its application to a particular scientific object is nevertheless not established by naming that object a totality. The relevant content and connection must still be shown. Hegel himself distinguishes the conditions belonging to a specific fact from the non-essential circumstances with which they are mixed; for the absolute, unrestricted fact, he names the sphere of being itself as condition (*SL* §1031). Nothing in the word totality tells an investigator which concrete circumstances belong to the fact under investigation.

## VI. The Emergence into Existence

"When all the conditions of a fact are present, it enters into Existence" (*SL* §1033). The sentence must be read through the development that makes its all intelligible. The conditions are the whole content in the form of external immediacy; ground is the same fact as reflecting form. Each side's movement sublates the externality of its relation to the other. The conditions withdraw into ground, but this makes ground itself posited and sublated, not a final support left outside the result (*SL* §§1031–1032). Their convergence is the fact's relation to itself.

Hegel therefore writes: "The fact is, before it exists" — as essence or unconditioned, determinate in its conditions and in its ground (*SL* §1033). This before names its standing as essence, not the temporal pre-existence of a finished thing waiting in a hidden world for its material duplicate. Nor is the result the success of an external assembler. The conditions are already determinations of reflection, and "the positing of one essentially involves the positing of the others" (*SL* §1034). Their scattered immediacy is what is sublated.

The double cancellation matters: "they sublate themselves as immediate being and presupposition, and equally ground sublates itself." Accordingly, "the emergence into Existence is therefore immediate in such a manner that it is mediated only by the vanishing of mediation" (*SL* §1034). Ground does not remain beneath the fact as an independent substrate. Yet its disappearance is not the loss of everything the mediation achieved: the result is self-identical through that sublation. Hegel calls the fact both unconditioned and groundless, emerging from its own essential negativity, and names this immediacy **Existence** (*Existenz*, *SL* §1035). This does not prescribe the physical destruction of every material condition when a thing comes to be.

We have met a resulted immediacy before: **determinate being** (*Dasein*) followed Becoming (§10). The resemblance does not make the derivations interchangeable. Here the result contains the determinate mediation of ground and condition; it is not pure Being restored, nor a universal recipe for every transition in the *Logic* (§06). Essence has given itself essential being. "Essence must appear" introduces the next section (*SL* §1036), but Existence and the Thing still have to develop into Appearance proper; their first immediate unity is not yet the explicit relation of appearing and essential being (*SL* §§1037–1039; §23).

## VII. Three Misreadings, Answered Directly

**The first is the rationalist-license misreading**: that because a ground is demanded, any reason supplied from thought must suffice. The chapter tests that very presumption. Formal repetition, a selected real ground, and even the complete ground-relation leave different debts. This does not turn Hegel into an empiricist or make his logical derivation a laboratory procedure. It prevents us from crediting an assigned reason with a sufficiency its content and connection have not earned.

**The second is the ground-is-just-cause misreading.** Ground is essence's real reflective mediation, not yet the **causal efficacy** of substance. Identity of content will recur in formal causality, but there it belongs to substance actual only in its effect (*SL* §§1246–1248; §26). Yet when finite causality is used to explain a phenomenon by ascending from effect to cause, Hegel renews the charge of merely repeating one content (*SL* §§1251–1253). The later category does not abolish that explanatory problem. Ground's real mediation and causality's actuality are distinct determinations. Their relation cannot be understood by silently importing the later category into the earlier one.

**The third is the explanation-is-always-empty misreading.** The charge of empty repetition concerns formal grounding arrested in that form; the critique of real ground concerns an ungrounded connection or an unearned claim to sufficiency. Neither licenses dismissing every conditional scientific explanation. Conversely, following the logical sequence to Existence does not certify that a particular explanation is complete. A bounded explanation can be true without deriving all its presuppositions, while a promised total explanation must account for the connections it otherwise borrows.

## VIII. What the Method Did Here — Observations

The first observation is that **a principle is examined through its determinate content**. §21 distinguished the development of reflection's determinations from a blanket refutation of formal laws. Here sufficient reason is neither an axiom that settles its own application nor a demand simply discarded. Hegel distinguishes formal sufficiency, the selection of real grounds, the mediation of their connection, and the further demand for the unity he reserves for the Concept (*SL* §§969, 997, 1013). These are not interchangeable senses of having a reason.

The second observation is that **an explanation's achievement and its further debt must be separated**. Our methodological use of the chapter is to ask what a proposed ground contributes, what connects it with the grounded, and what that connection presupposes. The formal, real, and complete grounds are not grades certifying no explanation, partial explanation, and finished science. Their development diagnoses determinate forms of mediation. Applied to Hegel's own scientific verdicts, the same discipline preserves the criticism of mere renaming while refusing to make it a substitute for assessing an actual theory.

The third observation is that **mediation transforms the standing of its own presuppositions**. The advance from condition to the unconditioned required showing the relation within each apparently independent side; their dependence was not overcome by gathering them into a larger container. Existence is the immediacy of that relation returned into itself. The achievement is not the discovery that reasons should eventually be forgotten, but the determination of a result whose essence is no longer lodged in a separate ground behind it.

## IX. What Comes Next

The result opens the second section of the Doctrine of Essence:

- **§23 — Existence and the Thing**: Existence as essential immediacy; the **Thing** (*Ding*), the thing-in-itself and its external existence; **properties**, **matters**, and the passage into **Appearance**.
- **Beyond**: appearance and law, the essential relations, and **Actuality**; only later substance, causality, and reciprocity. The unconditioned fact reached here is not a shortcut past those determinations to the Concept.

## Coda

To demand a ground is to refuse to stop at the immediate. But the reason offered can itself be immediate: a renamed phenomenon, a selected feature, or a connection accepted in another case and carried across. Ground's development turns the demand back upon what was supposed to satisfy it. The complete relation does not end the investigation; it exposes its condition. The unconditioned is reached only when the distinction between grounding form and presupposed content is shown to be the fact's own mediation. Ground then **goes to ground**, not because explanation has failed indiscriminately, but because its result no longer has its essence in an independent support beyond itself. *What steps forth is neither the unexplained given nor a conclusion hung forever from an alien premise: it is Existence, the immediacy that contains its mediation as sublated. How that logical result bears on any determinate science remains a further question whose warrant the category cannot supply in advance.*
