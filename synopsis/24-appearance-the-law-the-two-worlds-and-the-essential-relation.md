# Appearance: The Law, the Two Worlds, and the Essential Relation

*The fifteenth installment of Section III — the fifth in the **Doctrine of Essence**, developing **Appearance** (*Erscheinung*) through **law** and the **essential relation**. §23's negative mediation contains a positive identity: the **law of appearance** (*Gesetz der Erscheinung*), the stable content present **in** appearance, not beyond it. Yet law initially lacks appearance's **negative form**, and its related contents do not themselves exhibit the necessity of their connection. Recovering that negative mediation transforms the **realm of laws** (*Reich der Gesetze*) into a **world in and for itself**, opposed to the appearing world. The **inverted world** (*verkehrte Welt*) does not endure as a second realm: each world proves to contain both self-relation and relation to otherness. Their unity is the **essential relation** (*wesentliches Verhältnis*), not yet substance. **Whole and parts** (*das Ganze und die Teile*) reciprocally presuppose each other while claiming independent subsistence; their mediation becomes **force and its expression** (*Kraft und Äußerung*). Force preserves itself in expressing itself, but is **conditioned** and requires **solicitation**. The reciprocity of soliciting and being solicited develops into **inner and outer** (*das Innere und das Äußere*). Here the identity of **content** and the immediate conversion of opposed **forms** must themselves be united: only that development reaches **Actuality** (*Wirklichkeit*). Hegel's scientific examples and polemics remain open to assessment; neither a logical derivation nor the exposure of an empty force-name decides the worth of a physical explanation. Nor does the identity of essence and manifestation certify complete empirical knowledge or by itself refute **Kant's** restriction on cognition.*

---

## I. Where We Are: From Existence to Appearance

§23 developed the Thing through property and self-subsistent **matters**. Their negative relation made their subsistence a **mediation through negation**, not the independent immediacy they claimed. Existence thereby determined itself as **Appearance**, the unity of **shine** and Existence: its moments exist, but their immediacy is itself a moment of reflection. The result does not yet abolish the distinction of essential and unessential existence; its further development is the present chapter's task. **Essence must appear** is the demand being worked out, not a statement that the work is already finished.

The word **Appearance** (*Erscheinung*) must be heard with care, for it names a **rise in truth**, not a fall into semblance. It is the truth of two categories already behind us. It is the truth of **shine** (*Schein*, §20): there the shine was essence's negativity shining **within itself**, an immediacy that *is* only as sublated, but still turned inward, not yet issued into a world. And it is the truth of **Existence** (§23): there the existent stood forth as a mediated immediacy that still *seemed* to rest on itself. Appearance is essence that has **stepped out** and now exists **as** its own showing. Its negativity is no longer submerged in an immediately self-subsistent thing; it belongs to the existent's own essential character. This is not yet the completed relation of inner and outer. Hegel states the initial unity: "Appearance is at first essence in its Existence; **essence is immediately present in it**… Existence as essential Existence is Appearance" (*SL* §1081). And he turns the dismissive idiom on its head: "if it is said that something is only Appearance, in the sense that contrasted with it immediate Existence is the truth, then the fact is that **Appearance is the higher truth**" — indeed "it is when Existence passes over into Appearance that **it ceases to be essenceless**" (*SL* §1083).

The difference from **Kant** concerns the standing of this reflection, not a contrast between real things and subjective illusion. Kant's **negative noumenon** limits the application of sensible cognition; it is not a positively known second world (§01; §23). Hegel instead develops appearance as the existent's **own reflection**, not as a status assigned by comparing it with an independently placed essence. His formulation is explicit: "This is **not an alien, external reflection** to which essence belongs and which, by comparing essence with Existence, pronounces the latter to be Appearance. On the contrary… **this essentiality of Existence which constitutes its Appearance, is the truth of Existence itself. The reflection by virtue of which it is this, is its own**" (*SL* §1082). This states the categorial result and Hegel's alternative. As §23 insists, developing that result does not by itself answer Kant's objection to cognition apart from the conditions of possible experience. Calling the reflection objective cannot replace the argument about the warrant for knowing it.

## II. The Law of Appearance

Appearance is not first a sheer flux to which a stable law is subsequently added. Its **negative mediation already contains a positive identity**. An existent has its subsistence in another; but that other is itself not an independent support. Its negation is likewise negated, and the existent thus returns into itself through the very relation that denied its immediate independence. Hegel begins: "**Appearance is the existent mediated by its negation, which constitutes its subsistence**" (*SL* §1088). The positive side is consequently not an empirical regularity merely noticed among unrelated changes. It is the identity of the existing content within this mediation: "**It relates itself in this its negation or in its other, which is itself a sublated moment, to itself**" (*SL* §1089).

This identity is determinate. The content contains distinct sides whose subsistence is connected: "**the positedness of the one is also the positedness of the other**" (*SL* §1090). Their common subsistence, initially taken in its positive identity, is the **law of appearance** (*Gesetz der Erscheinung*). Hence the **realm of laws** (*Reich der Gesetze*) is the **stable copy** (*das ruhige Abbild*) of the appearing world, not a second stock of things: "**law is not beyond Appearance but is immediately present in it**" (*SL* §1094). Law and appearance share this essential content. But their identity is not yet exhaustive, for "**Appearance is the same content, but contains still more**" (*SL* §1093).

That **more** first includes the concrete determinations of this appearance, connected with law but not posited by it (*SL* §1095). More fundamentally, it is appearance's **negative form**: its reflection into another, its alteration and return into unity. The law initially retains the stable result without containing this movement as its own. Thus "**Law does not contain this side of restless form or negativity**"; appearance contains law and also "**the moment of self-moving form**" (*SL* §1096). The defect is not simply that a general statement fails to reproduce every feature of an individual stone. The law claims to be the essential identity of a mediation whose negative moment it still leaves outside itself. Hegel accordingly calls it the substrate, **not yet the ground**, of appearance (*SL* §1098).

The same defect appears **within the law**. Its sides are essentially connected, yet their respective contents are still diverse and mutually indifferent; the identity is therefore only internal, not exhibited as their own necessary relation. Hegel's example is the law of fall, in which "**spaces passed through vary as the squares of the times elapsed**". Empirically ascertaining this connection does not itself supply its proof: "**the law as such does not contain this proof and its objective necessity**" (*SL* §1097). This criticism distinguishes a stated law from the mediation of its necessity; it does not establish that every scientific law is an unexplained regularity. In a modern, conditional derivation, constant acceleration and release from rest give *s = ½ · g · t²*. The square follows from those assumptions; their applicability and the determination of *g* remain further questions. This is our scientific comparison, not Hegel's derivation of space, time, or gravitation.

The two deficiencies thus concern **one missing negative mediation**, not two independent demands for more information. Outwardly, law stands beside the movement whose identity it is; inwardly, its sides are connected without each containing that connection in its own determination. Hegel names the requisite advance: "**The essential unity of the two sides of the law would be their negativity, that is to say, the one would contain its other within itself**" (*SL* §1097). This is more than asking for an additional cause behind a given formula. The further logical development must show how the identity already reached is itself a **negative unity**. Its success would still not supply the determinate premises of every scientific explanation.

## III. The Two Worlds and the Inverted World

The advance begins from **what the law's identity already is**. Appearance is reflected into another which itself has no independent subsistence; its return into itself is consequently the negation of that otherness, not an identity detachable from it. Law, as this reflection-into-self, is therefore appearance's negative unity as well as its stable substrate (*SL* §§1098–1099). Its sides likewise cannot remain indifferent contents externally joined: each has its subsistence in the positing of the other, and thus the negation of its own separate subsistence within its own determination. Each contains the other while distinguishing itself from it (*SL* §1100). With this negative form restored, law no longer occupies only one side opposite appearance. Its content is **determinateness in an essential connection developing into totality**, rather than an indefinite collection of separate laws (*SL* §1101). This is the **world in and for itself** (*die an und für sich seiende Welt*), also called the "**supersensuous world**" (*SL* §1103).

Yet this totality is **negative relation to itself**. It distinguishes itself into the essential world and the **world of appearance** (*die erscheinende Welt*), thereby becoming also one side of the totality it claims to be (*SL* §1104). The essential world is now appearance's **determinate ground**: not merely its shared positive content, but the total connection containing its alterations. Because the sides contain their determinate other, their identity is an identity of **opposites**, not a relation to an unspecified beyond (*SL* §1105). This is the **inverted world** (*verkehrte Welt*): the ground of the appearing world is at the same time its opposed world. Hegel says that "**the world in and for itself is the inversion of the manifested world**" (*SL* §1106).

His examples give that claim a provocative physical and moral form: "**The north pole in the world of Appearance is in and for itself the south pole**"; "**positive electricity is in itself negative**"; and "**What is evil, a misfortune and so on, in manifested existence is in and for itself, good and a piece of good fortune**" (*SL* §1107). These are Hegel's assertions, not scientific results established by the preceding mediation. Calling them categorial does not exempt them from assessment. The logical inversion of a total ground relation does not by itself identify its realization in magnetism or electricity, nor establish the goodness of an evil or a misfortune. Those applications require a physical account or moral criterion which this passage does not supply. What its logical continuation examines is whether **two independently subsisting worlds** can sustain this opposition.

The dissolution is **reciprocal**. The appearing world's relation to another is already relation to a self-sublating other, hence reflection into itself: it contains law, not merely external dispersion. Conversely, the essential world is the complete reflection of appearance and therefore contains difference and negative self-relation, not merely changeless identity. It opposes and inverts itself; as ground containing its own negation, it is also **sublated ground and immediate Existence** (*SL* §1108). This is not an inference from being related to something visible to being visible oneself. Each world has proved to contain both forms of reflection. Thus each has its self-subsistence only in the unity with its other (*SL* §1109). There is one world, **present as it appears and as it essentially is**, but its sides are not yet adequately determined simply by announcing their sameness. The law's inner identity has become explicit in sides each containing its other: "**Thus law is essential relation**" (*SL* §1111).

## IV. Whole and Parts — the Mechanical Relation

The **essential relation** (*wesentliches Verhältnis*) contains sides that are self-subsistent totalities, yet have their subsistence only through each other. Hegel expressly warns that it is "**not as yet the true third to essence and Existence**" (*SL* §1113). Its identity is still a relation whose self-subsistence seems to lie outside it, in the related sides; **substance is not yet before us** (*SL* §1114). The task is therefore to develop this unity, not to install a finished substance beneath it. The first form is **whole and parts** (*das Ganze und die Teile*), the immediate relation of reflected and immediate self-subsistence.

The **whole** inherits the reflected unity of the essential world; the **parts**, the immediate manifoldness of appearance (*SL* §1118). Each is the entire relation under one determination. The whole is nothing without its parts, and parts are parts only through the whole; yet each is also taken as independently subsisting, with the other reduced to its moment or external relation. Their contradiction lies in maintaining **independent subsistence and dependence in the same relation** (*SL* §§1119–1121). This is not simply the thing and its properties renamed: the two sides are now themselves totalities reciprocally positing their presupposition. Hegel accordingly distinguishes this developed reciprocal conditioning from the earlier condition-relation of §22 (*SL* §1122).

Merely asserting their equality does not resolve the contradiction. The whole equals the parts **together**; but their togetherness is precisely the whole. Conversely, the parts equal the whole **as divided**, which is precisely the parts. Each supposed equation therefore returns one side to itself while leaving the difference unexplained (*SL* §§1124–1126). Yet the sides cannot retain this indifference: a whole without internal differentiation is only abstract identity, and parts without unity are no longer parts. Each side's claimed self-subsistence negates itself and refers it to the other; neither can serve as an independently given first (*SL* §1127).

The result is **mediation**, not an oscillation accepted as final. The reflected unity goes outside itself into immediate manifoldness; that immediacy, equally unable to stand alone, returns into reflected unity. Each movement is also conditioned by the other. Hegel's conclusion is that "**The truth of the relation consists therefore in the mediation; its essence is the negative unity**" (*SL* §1128). The immediacy of the sides is now posited as self-sublating rather than independently given. This transforms whole and parts into **force and its expression** (*SL* §1129): the unity is no longer indifferent to the manifoldness through which it subsists.

Hegel explicitly recollects the earlier antinomy: "**The antinomy of the infinite divisibility of matter was considered above under the Notion of quantity**" (*SL* §1130; §15). A part isolated as self-subsistent ceases to be a part and is treated as a whole, which again has parts; the progress repeats because each determination is asserted apart from the other it entails (*SL* §1131). This recollection neither turns discreteness into a demand for physical atoms nor supersedes §15's distinctions between Hegel's reconstruction, Kant's argument, and a scientific account of matter. Likewise, calling whole and parts a **mechanical** relation does not make analysis illegitimate. Hegel grants its use while denying its adequacy to the living unity of organs (*Enc.* §135). The limit belongs to treating external composition as the complete relation.

## V. Force and Its Expression

**Force** (*Kraft*) is this negative unity as expressing itself. Hegel contrasts it with the "**dead, mechanical aggregate**" whose unity remains external to its matter (*SL* §1132). Force goes into **expression** (*Äußerung*), and the expression returns into force as its ground. But this is not simply becoming and vanishing: the unity preserves itself in the difference it posits. Hegel says that force "**in this alteration posited by itself remains what it is**" (*SL* §1133). The same paragraph adds the qualification essential to the whole development: this unity is itself a moment, **mediated and conditioned by its other**. Expression is not yet unconditioned self-determination.

Consider first its **condition**. If force is a determination merely attached to an independently existing thing, the thing supplies no ground for possessing it: the connection appears imposed from outside (*SL* §1134). Hegel associates this quiescent determination with **matters**: he returns to the form of self-subsistence examined in §23, citing magnetic and electrical matters and the "**subtle aether which holds everything together**" (*SL* §1135). But the immediate subsistence contained in force has already become a self-sublating moment, not an indifferent thing beneath it. Force itself posits the externality in which it exists (*SL* §§1136–1137). This does not yet remove conditionedness: its activity still presupposes an external immediacy. Since that other is now likewise a self-relating negative unity rather than a bare thing, the condition is **another force** (*SL* §§1138–1140).

The external impulse therefore **solicits** force; it does not simply deposit an alien determination in a passive recipient. Force's expression is its own positing of externality, and it makes the impulse a moment of this activity (*SL* §1143). Nor can one side be fixed as the original solicitor and the other as merely solicited. Each presupposes the other through its own relation to it. The soliciting force acts only with another over against it, and is thus itself solicited to solicit; the solicited force, by its own activity, determines the other as its solicitor (*SL* §§1144–1145). Hegel concludes that expression is **reaction** in the specific sense that force posits the externality as its own moment (*SL* §1146). The active and passive determinations belong to **each** side; they are not immutable properties dividing two independently given forces.

This is the **infinity of force** reached in the greater *Logic*: its relation to the other proves to be its own mediated relation, so that "**its passivity consists in its very activity**" and "**its externality is identical with its inwardness**" (*SL* §1147). The result is not that every finite force has become self-sufficient. Hegel begins the same paragraph with its limit: "**Force is finite in so far as its moments still have the form of immediacy**". The *Encyclopedia* also insists that conditioned force acts **blindly**, not as an intrinsically determined Concept or end (*Enc.* §136). What has been developed is the identity expressed through the mediation; its next form is **inner and outer**.

The *Encyclopedia* both attacks the explanatory use of force and grants a determinate achievement to it. Hegel calls explanation by a force a tautology: an inward name adds nothing to the content and law discovered from the phenomenon. He presses the objection against the assurance that talk of forces implies no theory of their nature, asking why the form is then introduced into science at all. Yet the Addition says that the totality of manifestation conceived as law makes the force itself known, and defends the formal right of empirical science to seek determinate knowledge rather than rest in mere faith in divine governance. He nevertheless maintains that the **necessity connecting its content**, and the determinate limits of that content, have not been comprehended (*Enc.* §136). Thus a bare **plant-producing force** repeats §22's empty explanation, but the category of force is not itself only that mistake. Our assessment distinguishes the disclaimer Hegel attacks from an absence of explanation: a force law can supply determinate relations and derivations without claiming knowledge of an intrinsic mechanism behind them. That contribution must be assessed through the theory and its evidence; it neither follows from naming a force nor by itself establishes an unconditioned account of it.

## VI. Inner and Outer — the Passage into Actuality

The **inner and outer** (*das Innere und das Äußere*) first name the result of force's mediation. The inner is reflected immediacy, or essence; the outer is the form of being. Their distinction remains, but not as a separation of independently subsisting forces: "**the expression of force is only a mediation of the reflected unity with itself**" (*SL* §1148). The next development distinguishes **two identities** which must not be mistaken for an already completed unity.

**First, identity of content.** Inner and outer are one **fact**, not two contents requiring an external bridge. Yet this identity of content is initially treated as indifferent to its forms; it consequently becomes another inner opposed to their externality (*SL* §1149). **Second, identity of form.** Taken as the opposed abstractions of reflection-into-self and immediate externality, each converts immediately into the other. An inner held apart from existence is itself merely immediate, externally opposed to what it excludes; an outer held apart from essence has its essential determination still only implicitly. Hence "**something which in the first instance is only an inner, is for that very reason only an outer**", and conversely (*SL* §1151). This formal conversion is not yet the content-filled totality: Hegel calls its negative unity "**the simple point devoid of any content**" (*SL* §1151).

The distinction matters for the examples. Mere inwardness need not mean that there is **nothing to develop**. Hegel's Remark places this immediate identity at a thing's **beginning**, before it has developed its moments through its own activity (*SL* §§1152–1153). The *Encyclopedia* gives the child: reason is inward as capacity, outward as the authority and instruction through which it is to become the child's own (*Enc.* §140 Addition). Its further polemic against unfulfilled claims to genius or virtue likewise has a qualification: outward circumstances can frustrate well-meant designs. Hegel nevertheless judges a person through the course of the person's actions, not a claimed inward excellence immune to manifestation (*Enc.* §140 Remark and Addition). Neither bodily appearance nor one thwarted act is therefore a sufficient disclosure of character.

**The two identities must now be united.** The content cannot remain an indifferent substrate behind the forms: it is the unity of inner and outer, and hence of the very determinations supposedly external to it. Conversely, each form is the totality only through its other: reflected inwardness contains immediacy, while the outer, posited as unessential and withdrawn into its ground, contains inwardness. The content is thus form reflected into itself, and the form is the content's own differentiation (*SL* §§1154–1155). Hegel expressly says that the identity is not only immediate: "**it is also their mediated identity; for it is precisely through its other that each is what it is in itself, the totality of the relation**" (*SL* §1155). This is the step lost if the formula about mere inwardness is made to yield actuality at once.

The result is **Actuality** (*Wirklichkeit*), the unity of essence and Existence. Externality is no longer only reflection into another but also the thing's own reflection into itself: "**What something is, therefore, it is wholly in its externality**" (*SL* §1156). Its content and form are identical in this manifestation, rather than an inner content awaiting an alien expression. Hegel closes: "**The essential relation, in this identity of Appearance with the inner or with essence, has determined itself into actuality**" (*SL* §1157). The *Encyclopedia* describes the result as immediacy in which inner and outer are absolutely identical (*Enc.* §141). This immediacy is the result of the mediation, not its erasure; nor is manifestation the same as exhaustive disclosure to an observer.

## VII. Three Misreadings, Answered Directly

**The first makes the realm of laws a permanent beyond.** Hegel does develop a supersensuous world, but not as an independently subsisting second realm in which inquiry may finally rest. Its opposition to appearance dissolves because each side contains both self-relation and relation to otherness (*SL* §§1108–1111). This does not reduce law to a spectator's summary of impressions; it denies the separation of law's essential content from the appearing world whose own reflection it is.

**The second either enthrones force as an occult cause or dismisses it as an empty name.** Both miss the positive development. Force is a more adequate relation than external composition because its unity preserves itself in expression; it remains finite where its condition confronts it as independent. The critique of mere renaming does not abolish this mediation. Nor does the identity of force and expression prove that everything about a particular force is known: Hegel himself distinguishes the exhibited content from the necessity still needing comprehension (*Enc.* §136). The scientific question is what a proposed explanation actually adds and establishes.

**The third makes appearance a subjective sham.** That misstates Kant's position as well as Hegel's (§I; §23). For Hegel, appearance's reflection belongs to the existent itself; its immediate self-subsistence has been reduced to a moment, not replaced by a spectator's illusion. The further identity of essence and manifestation does not hand a knower the complete content of every appearance. Nor does asserting its objective standing settle Kant's question of the conditions under which that standing can be cognized.

## VIII. What the Method Did Here — Observations

The first observation is that **identity is a problem to develop, not a licence to stop**. Law's positive identity omits its negative form; the two worlds' identity yields an essential relation not yet adequate to its own unity; whole and parts assert equality through two tautologies; inner and outer require the identity of content and of form to be mediated together. §22 similarly distinguished the common content and unity of form of ground and condition rather than declaring every explanation complete. These developments cannot be replaced by a universal instruction to find the same content on both sides. What matters is **which independence has failed, through what mediation, and what new distinction remains**.

The second observation concerns **scientific explanation**. A stable connection, its empirical ascertainment, and a derivation of its necessity are distinguishable achievements. So are a force-name and a theory supplying determinate relations. Hegel's criticisms sharpen those distinctions, but their scientific applications must answer to the content of the theories criticized (§22). The logical treatment of law does not deduce a falling body's acceleration; the reciprocity of solicitation does not establish a quantitative law of interacting forces; the inverted world does not establish a moral vindication of misfortune. These are limits on what the arguments have proved, not a protection against examining what Hegel says.

The third observation is that **recollection concerns earlier distinctions in a transformed relation**. The Something of §11 and §12 had being-in-itself and being-for-other as its moments; §23 developed thing-in-itself and external existence; the present inner and outer are forms within a mediated totality. Their kinship does not make them interchangeable. Hegel likewise expressly returns to divisibility at the level of whole and parts, while the role of the opposition is now different from its role in quantity (*SL* §§1130–1131). The necessity of the new relation must be shown from its own content. A recollection can illuminate that development; it cannot perform it.

## IX. What Comes Next

The unity of inner and outer has become **Actuality**, and the Doctrine of Essence turns to its final section. This result is a new beginning, not a completed account of all its determinations:

- **§25 — The Absolute and Actuality**: the unity first appears as **the Absolute**, whose exposition must become its own movement. Then come **possibility** (*Möglichkeit*), **contingency** (*Zufälligkeit*), and **necessity** (*Notwendigkeit*), developed through formal, real, and absolute necessity.
- **§26 — The Absolute Relation**: **substance and accident**, **causality** (*Kausalität*), and **reciprocity** (*Wechselwirkung*). Force has prepared a problem of conditioning and expression; these later relations must develop their own mediation in the element of actuality. Their result opens the **Concept** (*Begriff*), not a final resting place in mere reciprocal dependence.

## Coda

The movement has not stripped a succession of hidden things away to leave only a visible surface. It has developed the **mediation within what appears**. Law preserves the identity of change but must recover its negative form; the essential world cannot hold its own other outside itself; whole and parts cannot sustain the independence each claims; force expresses itself through the condition it initially confronts as alien. Finally, the inward content and the outward form cease to be indifferent to one another. **Actuality** is this identity as manifestation, not an essence left behind its expression. *The achievement is not that inquiry has nothing more to discover, but that an unexplained beyond can no longer do the work of the explanation.*
