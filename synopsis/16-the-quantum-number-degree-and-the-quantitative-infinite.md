# The Quantum: Number, Degree, and the Quantitative Infinite

*The seventh installment of Section III — a close reading of the second moment of Quantity. The **quantum** (*Quantum*) as quantity with a limit, and the peculiar **indifference** of that limit; **Number** (*Zahl*) as the quantum's complete posited form, the unity of **amount** (*Anzahl*) and **unit** (*Einheit*), with Hegel's species of calculation read as the progressive **equalization** of those two moments; **extensive** and **intensive magnitude** (*Grad*, degree) as not two kinds of quantity but one and the same determinateness in two forms, where the qualitative makes its first and formal appearance; the **quantitative infinite** — the quantum's restless self-transcendence into *more* and *less*, and the **spurious** quantitative infinite of the endless series and the asymptote (the form diagnosed in §13, recurring here with a decisive difference: the quantum is affirmatively self-related in its infinite as the finite never was), together with Hegel's critique of the modern sublime, of astronomy's prestige, and of Kant's first antinomy; the **turn**, in which the endless progress negates not only every quantum but its own beyond, and the quantum, by that second negation, reverts to quality and is posited as **ratio** — the transition itself, made in the bare determinations of the progress and without any mathematics; and only then the long-deferred reading of the **differential calculus**, where the vanishing magnitude proves to be nothing outside its **ratio** and that ratio's content proves **qualitative** — not the vehicle of the transition but its most striking illustration, and the redemption of the Section II promissory note.*

---

## I. Where We Are: from Pure Quantity's Self-Limitation to the Quantum

The previous installment left pure quantity in the act of limiting itself. Pure quantity was magnitude as such — the open dimension of more-and-less, continuous and discrete in its two moments, but not yet cut at any definite point into *this much*. Its discrete moment, the moment of the unit and the many ones, would not let it stay open: discrete magnitude has the one as its determinateness and therefore as its first negation and limit, and that limit, being itself a one and so self-related, is an *enclosing, encompassing* limit — a limit drawn within the magnitude by the magnitude's own moment rather than laid upon it from outside. Quantity giving itself such a limit is the **quantum** — magnitude determined as *this much*, *so many*, *this far and no further*: not merely "quantity with a limit," but quantity drawn into a *determinate cut* within the open field of possible magnitudes, *this* one marked off from all the others it might equally have been.

This installment develops the quantum through its three forms. First the quantum gives itself its complete and proper expression as **Number**. Then the quantum shows that it can be taken in two ways — as **extensive** magnitude (a plurality of units) and as **intensive** magnitude or **degree** (a simple intensity) — which prove to be one and the same determinateness in two forms. Then the quantum's limit, pressed, reveals the same restlessness the finite had: every quantum points beyond itself to a larger and a smaller, and this is the **quantitative infinite**. Following that restlessness to its truth — through the spurious infinite of the endless series, and then through the second negation in which the endless *beyond itself* vanishes — we will watch the quantum so thoroughly transcend itself that what remains is no longer a quantum at all, but a *qualitative* relation: a **ratio**. That transition is made in the bare determinations of the progress, with no mathematics in the argument; the differential calculus arrives afterwards as its most striking illustration, and Hegel's long Remarks on it are read here in that light. This is the moment quality returns within quantity, and it is the hinge on which the whole sphere turns toward Measure.

## II. The Quantum and Its Indifferent Limit

Recall the peculiarity of the quantitative limit, for everything that follows is its unfolding. The *qualitative* limit was the thing's very being: a Something was *this* up to its limit, and to cross the limit was to cease to be what one was. The *quantitative* limit is indifferent to being. A magnitude can be larger or smaller and remain magnitude; the thing whose magnitude it is can take on more or less and remain that thing — the heap is still a heap, the line still a line, the sum of money still a sum of money. And yet the quantitative limit is a *genuine* limit, a real determinacy: the quantum is *this* amount and not another.

So the quantum carries a contradiction in its very constitution, and it is a sharper contradiction than first appears. The quantum *is* its limit — it is nothing but *this much*, wholly determined by the point at which it is cut. And the quantum is at the same time *indifferent* to its limit — it could be otherwise and still be a quantum, still be *this* thing's magnitude. A determinacy that is at once the whole being of the thing-as-magnitude and a matter of indifference to it: that is the quantum. We will see this double character drive the quantum, exactly as the analogous double character drove the finite, beyond every limit it reaches.

## III. Number — the Quantum's Complete Form

The quantum's proper and complete expression is **Number** (*Zahl*). Number is where the two moments of pure quantity — continuity and discreteness — reappear, now within the determinate quantum, as its two constitutive determinations.

The first is the **unit** (*Einheit*). Every number is counted in units of *something*: a unit that is self-same, repeatable, one-and-the-same throughout the count. The unit is the continuous moment within number — the self-equality in virtue of which the many counted ones are ones *of the same kind*, members of one number. The second is the **amount** (*Anzahl*) — *how many*: the determinate plurality of units, the discrete moment within number, the manyness that says *this* number is seven and not five. Number is the unity of the two: a determinate amount of a determinate unit. "Seven" is seven (amount) ones (unit); and the two are inseparable, for an amount is always an amount *of* units, and a unit is a unit only as countable *into* an amount.

This is more than a definition; it is the key to why arithmetic has the structure it does. The operations of arithmetic — Hegel calls them the *species of calculation* (*Rechnungsarten*) — are, read dialectically, ordered by a single principle, and the principle is Hegel's own: the progressive **equalization of amount and unit**. Numbers begin "simply *unequal*"; then "the next determination is the *equality* of the numbers which are to be counted. Through this equality they are a *unit*, and number thus acquires the difference of *unit and amount*"; and finally "the further equality is that of unit and amount themselves" (*SL* §§456–459). The series accordingly begins with numbering and then falls into three pairs of ascending intimacy (*SL* §§450–459).

- **Numbering** is the first and most external: simply positing an amount of units, aggregating ones into a number. Here unit and amount are merely brought together from outside.
- **Addition and subtraction** relate numbers that are still wholly indifferent to one another — any number to any number. The relation is external: 7 and 5 have nothing to do with each other until I choose to add them.
- **Multiplication and division** tighten the relation: now one number is taken *as* a unit and the other *as* the amount of those units (3 × 4 = four threes, or three fours). The two numbers play the *roles* of unit and amount with respect to each other; the relation has become internal to the operation.
- **Raising to a power and extracting a root** complete the movement: the number is multiplied *by itself*, at once that-which-is-counted and how-many-times. Amount and unit, which began as two indifferent determinations brought together from outside, have collapsed into identity. But Hegel restricts the completion carefully, and the restriction matters: it is **the square alone** that achieves it. Beyond the square "the continuation is *formal*: partly, with even exponents, there is only a repetition of squaring, and partly, with uneven powers, **inequality again enters**" — in the cube, the factor is, as unit, unequal to the amount (3 against 3 times 3). Hence "the arithmetical square alone contains an immanent absolute determinedness; for which reason equations with further formal powers must be reduced to it, just as in geometry the right-angled triangle contains an immanent, absolute determinedness which is expounded in Pythagoras' theorem" (*SL* §459). (Extracting the root is the *inverse* operation — the analytic return from the power back to the self-related base it presupposes — and so belongs to the same final pair, the one in which the quantum has folded back upon itself.)

That last point is worth pausing on, because it is the seed of everything in this installment's second half. In the square, the quantum is related to *itself*: it is its own unit and its own amount. A self-relation has appeared within the merely external realm of number. And a self-relation is precisely the form of the *qualitative* — recall that quality was the determinacy that *was* the being, the thing's self-relation. So in squaring, the qualitative makes its first faint return *inside* quantity, in the very heart of arithmetic. This is not yet developed; but mark it, for the power-relation will prove to be exactly what Hegel says the calculus is really about.

## IV. Extensive and Intensive Magnitude — One Quantum, Two Forms

The quantum, as number, presents itself first as **extensive magnitude** (*extensive Größe*): magnitude as an *amount*, a plurality of units spread out — a hundred is a hundred ones, a length is so many inches, a duration so many seconds. The "extensive" name marks that the determinacy lies in the *manyness*, the units laid side by side.

But the same quantum can be taken in another form. The hundredth degree on a scale, the temperature, the pitch, the intensity of a colour or a force, the *degree* of anything — these are magnitudes that are *not* presented as a plurality of units within themselves. The hundredth degree is not "a hundred units of heat" lying side by side inside it; it is a *simple* determinateness, an intensity, a single *grade*. This is **intensive magnitude** or **degree** (*Grad*).

The understanding takes extensive and intensive magnitude for two different *kinds* of quantity — even for two different *realms*, the extensive belonging to the countable and divisible, the intensive to the qualitative, the inward, the unmeasurable-from-within. Hegel's analysis dismantles this, and the dismantling is one of the quietly decisive moves of the sphere.

First: **extensive and intensive magnitude are the same quantum in two forms, and each passes into the other.** The degree, though simple, is a *determinate* degree — *this* intensity and not another — and its determinateness is nothing other than its place in the scale, its relation to the degrees above and below. But a place in a scale is a *number*: the hundredth degree is the hundredth, the amount one hundred has not vanished but only been gathered up into the simple "hundredth." And conversely, every extensive magnitude can be gathered into an intensive one: the hundred units of length *are* the magnitude "one hundred," which can be read off as a single point on a measuring rod. The two are convertible — once a scale is in place — because they are the same quantum, taken once as spread-out amount and once as gathered intensity.

Second — and this is the polemical edge — **the degree's apparent inwardness is not the self-containment it seems.** Because the degree is *simple*, having no plurality of units *within* itself, it appears to have nothing in itself by which to be the determinate degree it is: what makes it the hundredth and not the ninety-ninth seems to lie wholly *outside* it, in the series of other degrees, in the whole scale. Hegel presses exactly this — and then refuses to stop there, which is where a careless reading goes wrong. The determinateness of degree, he insists, has **two sides**: "in the first place, in so far as it is a simple determinateness it is determinate *relatively* to other degrees… But, secondly, **it is determinate in its own self; this it is in the amount as its own amount**, not in the amount as excluded, nor in the amount of other degrees. **The twentieth degree contains the twenty within itself**" (*SL* §480). The amount is not abolished in the degree; it is *its own*. And with just this the two forms prove to be not two determinacies but one: "Extensive and intensive magnitude are thus **one and the same determinateness** of quantum; they are only distinguished by the one having amount within itself and the other having amount outside itself" (*SL* §481). Whereupon, Hegel adds, "**the *qualitative something* makes its appearance**" (*SL* §482) — a first and still formal showing of quality within quantity: not yet Measure, but not nothing either.

So the correction to the received picture is subtler than a simple inversion, and more interesting. Hegel does not turn the old view on its head; he grants it a partial right and then exhibits its one-sidedness. Of the dynamic conception in the philosophy of nature — density as a *degree* of space-filling force rather than an aggregate of parts — he writes that "the relation of force and its expression which corresponds to intensive magnitude is **in the first instance truer** than that of whole and parts, yet this does not make force, as intensive, any less one-sided" (*SL* §484). Degree is therefore neither quantity become genuinely inward nor a bare mask: it is the *same* quantum, with its amount gathered into itself instead of spread out. (The scholastic *latitude of forms* — the doctrine of the intension and remission of qualities — is the historical ancestor of the view Hegel is correcting; the connection is our gloss, not his citation.) His own examples make the point without any appeal to a hidden inwardness: mass appears extensively as weight and intensively as pressure; heat has its degree and equally its mercury column; "The higher note is, as more intensive, at the same time a greater number of vibrations"; and "high intensity of character, of talent or genius, is bound up with a correspondingly far-reaching reality in the outer world" (*SL* §§488–491).

(A consequence for the sciences follows, and we mark it as our own extension rather than Hegel's result. Where something already stands under a quantitative determination, its presentation as a simple intensity is no barrier to setting it against a scale and a number: the intensive is not a fortress against quantification but quantification in its most concentrated form. Hegel's argument, however, concerns what *already* manifests quantitative character (*SL* §§485–486); it does not by itself establish that any given "intensity" — utility, felt pain — admits such determination at all. That is a further question, and one the *Logic* does not settle. What genuinely resists is in any case not intensity but *quality* — and quality's showing in the degree, formal as §482 leaves it, is not yet the decisive return the road ahead will bring.)

## V. The Quantitative Infinite — the Quantum's Self-Transcendence

Now the quantum's indifferent limit (§II) does its work. The quantum is *this much* — but because its limit is indifferent, because it could always be more or less, the quantum points beyond itself, of its own nature, to a larger quantum and a smaller. And the larger and smaller are themselves quanta, each again pointing beyond itself. The quantum, simply by being the kind of determinacy it is, *sends itself past every limit it reaches*. This self-sending-beyond is the **quantitative infinite**.

We have seen this shape before — but the resemblance must be stated together with the difference Hegel himself insists on, and the difference is not a detail. It is the structure of the finite and its spurious infinite from §13, now in quantitative dress; yet the transposition is more than a change of costume. "The **difference** between the qualitative and quantitative infinite is that in the former the finite and infinite are *qualitatively* opposed and the transition… lies only in the *in-itself*… the finite continues itself into its other only **implicitly, not affirmatively**. The quantitative finite, on the other hand, **is self-related in its infinite**, in which it has its absolute determinateness" (*SL* §499); here "the limit in its own self dispatches and continues itself into its beyond" (*SL* §500). The quantum does openly and affirmatively what the finite did only in itself — which is precisely why the quantitative sphere will be able to reach its own resolution rather than merely suffer its contradiction. There, the finite's nature was to pass beyond its limit, generating the endless progress — the "and so on," the always-one-more, the beyond that recedes as fast as it is approached. Here the quantum's nature is to pass beyond its limit, generating the endless number series — *n*, *n*+1, *n*+2, … with no last term; the always-larger and always-smaller; the asymptote that the curve approaches forever and never reaches; the infinite series whose terms never come to an end. This is the **spurious quantitative infinite** (*das schlechte quantitative Unendliche*), and Hegel's verdict on it is the same as before: it is no true infinite at all. It is the perpetual *positing* of a beyond and the perpetual *failing* to reach it; it remains forever entangled with the finite quantum it was supposed to surpass, producing at every step another finite quantum. The "infinitely large" and "infinitely small," taken as ever-growing and ever-shrinking quanta, are spurious infinites: they are quanta still, only quanta with the flag of "infinite" planted on a magnitude that never stops being a magnitude. Hegel's own diagnosis is blunter. The infinitely great "is supposed to be *great*, that is, a quantum, and *infinite*, that is, not a quantum" — a straightforward self-contradiction; and the decisive consequence, which no amount of ingenuity evades, is that "**the increase of quantum brings it no *nearer* to the infinite; for the difference between quantum and its infinity is essentially not a quantitative difference**" (*SL* §502). The progress is therefore "not a real advance but a repetition of one and the same thing… **an impotence of the negative**" (*SL* §503), and the two pictures that govern it, the infinitely great and the infinitely small, "turn out to be **nebulous shadowy nullities**" (*SL* §530).

Hegel devotes a whole Remark to the *prestige* of this progress, and it is worth recording because the prestige survives intact today. The endless advance "is commonly held to be something sublime and a kind of divine worship"; but "this modern sublimity does not magnify the object… but only the *subject*" (*SL* §504). He runs the case through Kant's starry heavens, where "thought succumbs, the end is falling and faintness" (*SL* §§505–506), and through Haller's poem on eternity, noting that "the poet himself declares this so-called terrifying journey into the beyond to be futile" and that "**only by giving up this empty, infinite progression can the genuine infinite itself become present to him**" (*SL* §§507–508). Then comes the sentence that most concerns a synopsis written for the sake of scientific method. Astronomy, Hegel says, is admirable — "**not on account of such quantitative infinitude** but, on the contrary, on account of **the relations of *measure* and the laws which reason recognises in these objects, and which are the infinite of reason in contrast to that other, irrational infinite**" (*SL* §509). The awe proper to a science is not awe at magnitude. It is directed at law and measure — which is to say that Hegel is already pointing, from inside the critique of the quantitative infinite, at the sphere this installment is travelling toward.

The same diagnosis dismantles Kant's *first* cosmological antinomy — the properly quantitative one, on whether the world has a beginning in time and a limit in space. Hegel's verdict is that thesis and antithesis alike smuggle in what they undertake to prove: the thesis assumes "some or any *given point of time* up to which an eternity has elapsed," and a given point of time "means nothing else than a definite limit in time" — the very thing to be demonstrated (*SL* §§520–524); the antithesis likewise presupposes the unlimited continuation it purports to establish (*SL* §§526–527). Both amount to "nothing but the opposite assertions, that a *limit is*, and that the limit equally is only a *sublated* one" (*SL* §528) — the two halves of the quantum's own indifferent limit, set against each other and frozen. And Hegel's objection to Kant's transcendental solution is one of the sharpest sentences in the sphere: "It shows an **excessive tenderness for the world** to remove contradiction from it and then to transfer the contradiction to spirit, to reason, where it is allowed to remain unresolved. In point of fact it is spirit which is so strong that it can endure contradiction, but it is spirit, too, that knows how to resolve it" (*SL* §529).

The mathematicians' own unease testifies to this. The "infinitely large" number that is yet always a definite number under the limit-sign; the "infinitely small" that must be neither a finite magnitude nor zero; the convergent series "equal to" its sum only in the limit, "at infinity," which is to say never as a completed term — all of this is the spurious infinite generating the very puzzles that the foundations of analysis spent two centuries trying to lay to rest. (We are not adjudicating those technical efforts, and Hegel must not be made a partisan in them: his interest is not in repairing the calculus but in reading the *logical form* that its difficulties expose. His stance toward the mathematicians is nonetheless a good deal more critical than mere non-interference, as §VIII will show.) What he wants is not a better handling of the endless progress but its *transformation* — the recovery, within the quantitative, of a **true** infinite. That recovery is made in the section that follows; and it is essential to see that it is made **without mathematics**. The calculus will then arrive as its most striking illustration, not as its vehicle.

## VI. The Turn: the Beyond Itself Vanishes

Here is the hinge of the entire sphere, and it is easy to miss, because the Remarks on the mathematical infinite that surround it are so much longer and so much more famous than the argument they interrupt. The transition is not made by the calculus. It is made in the bare determinations of the infinite progress itself, and Hegel makes it in a few dense sentences.

Take the progress as we found it. What is usually noticed in it is only half of what is present — and Hegel names the missing half with a precision that reads like a diagnosis of every account that stops where we stopped in §V: "In the infinite progress as such, the only reflection usually made is that every quantum, however great or small, must be capable of vanishing, of being surpassed; **but not that this self-sublating of quantum, the beyond, the spurious infinite itself also vanishes**" (*SL* §533). That is the whole turn. The endless series negates every quantum — but it equally negates *the beyond it keeps positing*, since each beyond, the moment it is reached for, is found to be another quantum. So "in it we have the sublating of quantum, but equally too of its beyond, therefore **the negation of quantum as well as the negation of this negation**. Its truth is their unity in which they are, but only as moments" (*SL* §533). The second negation is the one the endless progress performs without noticing.

The ground for it was laid a step earlier, in the degree. Degree has its determinateness outside itself, in the scale; but that outside is *itself quantitative*, itself a quantum — so the beyond "is sublated, is itself determined as quantum which, therefore, **in its negation is with itself**" (*SL* §531). Hence "in the infinite progress the ***Notion* of quantum is *posited***" (*SL* §532). Nothing has been added to the quantum from outside. The quantum always *was* the determinacy that is itself only by being external to itself; the progress merely drives that constitution into the open, where it can be read.

And with the second negation, the sphere turns. The quantum "is posited as having its determinateness in another quantum by means of its non-being, of infinity; that is, **it is *qualitatively* that which it is**." Hegel then states the result without hedging: "The immediately following determination is that **the quantum has reverted to *quality***, is from now on qualitatively determined. For its peculiarity, its quality, is the externality, the indifference of the determinateness; and quantum is now posited as being in fact itself in its externality, as self-related therein, in simple unity with itself" (*SL* §534). **This** — not the differential — is the return of quality within quantity, and it is won by the quantum's own movement, from the quantum's own indifference, with no mathematics whatever in the argument. What the quantum has *become*, in having its determinateness only in another quantum, is a **ratio**; and the ratio is the business of §17.

Hegel then names the result three times over, and each formulation earns its place. First, what the endless progress was really after: "The infinite, which in the infinite progress has only the empty meaning of a non-being, of an unattained but sought beyond, **is in fact nothing else than *quality***" (*SL* §535). The series was never chasing a larger number; it was groping, in the only way quantity can grope, for a determinateness of its own — and a determinateness of one's own is precisely what quality is. Second, why the recovery is a *restoration* rather than an importation: "quantum is sublated quality; but quantum is infinite, goes beyond itself, is the negation of itself. Thus its passage beyond itself is, therefore, *in itself* the **negation of the negated quality, the restoration of it**" (*SL* §536). Quantity was quality suppressed; quantum's own self-transcendence is therefore already the negation of that suppression, and gives quality back. That is what a double negation always does in this logic, and it is why nothing had to be fetched from outside. And third, the name of the result: "Quantum is thus posited as repelled from itself, with the result that there are **two quanta** which, however, are sublated, are only as moments of **one unity**… Quantum as thus *self-related* as an indifferent limit in its externality and therefore posited as qualitative, **is *quantitative ratio***" (*SL* §537). The ratio is not promised here. It is *derived* here; §17 only unfolds it.

Why then the enormous Remarks on the calculus? Because the result just won is exactly what the calculus does, in the one place where mathematics was forced to do it and could not say what it had done. Hegel is not deriving his logic from the calculus; he is reading the calculus by the logic he has already derived. The next section takes it in that spirit.

## VII. The Calculus: the Vanishing Magnitude and the Qualitative Ratio

Here the Section II promissory note is redeemed. This is the most technically contested stretch of Hegel's *Logic* — the precise relation of his reading to Newton, Leibniz, Euler, Lagrange, and to the later rigorization is disputed in the literature, and we should hold the *interpretation* loosely. But the *logical* point is clear, and it is profound.

Hegel's own exposition does not begin with the differential. He builds a ladder of "the various stages in the expression of a quantum as *moment of a ratio*" (*SL* §551), and the differential is only its topmost rung. The first rung needs no calculus whatever, and it is by far the best entry into the doctrine: **the ordinary fraction**. In 2/7, Hegel observes, the two sides "are not valid as 2 and 7, but only in their relation"; their "quantitative determinateness remains — but as one which is *in itself* qualitative"; and decisively, "their place can be taken by infinitely many others without the value of the fraction being altered" — 4/14, 6/21, and so on without end (*SL* §552). A common fraction therefore already contains the moment of infinity, and contains it as a *completed, present* determinateness. The second rung generalizes the terms into letters, *a*/*b*, where the terms still "have the meaning of some finite quantum" but the ratio alone carries the sense (*SL* §554). Only after these does Hegel come to *dx*/*dy*.

And now the comparison that carries the whole argument — one that anyone can check without a line of analysis. Set the fraction 2/7 beside its decimal expansion 0.285714…. Which of the two is the *infinite* expression? Ordinary usage answers at once: the series, obviously, since it is the one that never ends. Hegel answers the exact reverse, and shows why. The series "contains and exhibits the contradiction of representing that which is a relation possessing a qualitative nature, as devoid of relation, as a mere quantum, as an amount" (*SL* §558); it is therefore "even *inferior*" to the fractional expression (*SL* §559). Whereas "what is called the finite expression or the sum of such a series **lacks nothing**… the beyond is recalled from its flight" (*SL* §560). The reason is structural, and it is the same reason that governed §13: "in the infinite series the negative is *outside* its terms" — it survives only in the ellipsis, the *and-so-on* — whereas "in the finite expression which is a ratio, the negative is *immanent* as the reciprocal determining of the sides." Hence Hegel's verdict, which inverts the received vocabulary root and branch: "**the usually so-called sum, the 2/7… is in fact a ratio; and this so-called finite expression is the truly infinite expression. The infinite series, on the other hand, is in truth a sum**" (*SL* §561). With the moral for anyone tempted by the prestige of endlessness: "The word infinite, even as used in infinite series, is commonly fancied to be something lofty and exalted; this is a kind of superstition, the superstition of the understanding; we have seen how, on the contrary, **it indicates only a deficiency**" (*SL* §561).

One boundary must be marked here, because the claim invites over-extension. Hegel himself notes that series which cannot be summed — he instances those containing "a higher kind of infinity… namely an **incommensurability**" — keep the spurious form without possessing any finite expression to be reduced to (*SL* §562). The thesis is therefore not that every infinite series has a closed form waiting behind it. It is that *where a determinate relation is what the series is groping toward*, the relation and not the series is the true expression. That a rule is determinate does not by itself yield a finite expression; and a series' sum, its generating function, and a derivative are not interchangeable objects. We keep the logical claim and decline the mathematical over-reach.

Take the differential, the "infinitely small" *dx*. The understanding asks: what magnitude is it? And it cannot answer. *dx* cannot be a *finite* magnitude — no finite magnitude is infinitely small, and wherever the dependence is more than linear a finite *dx* leaves the calculation in general merely approximate. (The restriction is not a concession but the point. Where a finite difference gives the exact answer *for every* non-zero increment — as it does for *f*(*x*) = 3*x* + 2, whose difference quotient is 3 throughout, and not merely at some isolated increment — nothing qualitative is at stake; and those are precisely the cases Hegel excludes from the calculus' proper business, as we are about to see.) And *dx* cannot be simply *zero* — zero would annihilate the calculation, give 0/0, yield nothing. So *dx* is a magnitude that is *vanishing*: not a fixed quantum at all, but a quantum *in the act of disappearing*, on the very threshold between something and nothing. As an independent quantum, *dx* is indeed nothing; the understanding is right that, taken by itself, it has no determinate value.

Hegel's resolution — and this is a *logical* reading of the historical infinitesimal notation, not a rival rule for assigning *dx* a value (least of all the modern stipulation *dx* = 0) — is to deny that it should be taken by itself at all. **The differential has no meaning as an independent quantum; it has meaning only as a moment of a *ratio*.** Outside the quotient *dy/dx*, *dx* is nothing and *dy* is nothing. But the *ratio* *dy/dx* is not nothing — it has a perfectly determinate value. Hegel says it exactly: "*Dx*, *dy*, are no longer quanta, nor are they *supposed* to signify quanta; **it is solely in their relation to each other that they have any meaning, a meaning merely as *moments***. They are no longer something… but neither are they nothing; not empty nullities. **Apart from their relation they are pure nullities**" (*SL* §570).

Here is the decisive inversion, and it deserves to stand alone: **two magnitudes that have each *individually* vanished to nothing nonetheless stand in a *determinate ratio*.**

The quotient is not the indeterminate 0/0 the understanding fears; it is determinate — but it is determinate as a **ratio**, not as a quantity. The terms have vanished *as quanta* precisely so that what they were holding apart — their *relation* — can stand forth pure. "That which has being solely in the ratio is not a quantum… the said infinite magnitudes are not merely *comparable*, but they **exist only as moments of comparison, i.e. of the ratio**" (*SL* §574).

There is a standing objection to all this which Hegel answers by sending the reader back to the beginning of the *Logic*, and the back-reference is worth following, because it shows how tightly the work is built. The objection is that a "vanishing magnitude" names an impossible *intermediate state* between being and nothing. Hegel's reply is that the objection has already been met in the fourth Remark to Becoming: "the unity of being and nothing is, of course, not a *state*… on the contrary, this *mean* and unity, the vanishing or equally the becoming is alone their truth" (*SL* §573). The differential is Becoming itself, returning in the element of quantity. What looked like a scandal peculiar to the calculus is the very first category-movement of the *Logic*, met again three hundred pages later in mathematical dress — which is as good an illustration as the work affords of what it means for a logic to be *systematic* rather than a list.

And what is that relation? It is **qualitative**, not quantitative. *dy/dx* is not the quotient of two surviving magnitudes; it is the *rate*, the slope, the *kind* of dependence that *y* has on *x* — the law of the curve *at a point*, the function's qualitative character there. It is one number, yes, but a number that is the expression of a qualitative determinateness: how, in what manner, this variable depends on that. (A caution about the word is needed here, since the synopsis leans on it constantly. "Qualitative" does **not** mean lawful dependence *as such*; Hegel is a great deal narrower. "The relation of a magnitude to a **power** is not a quantum, but essentially a **qualitative relation**; the power-relation is the feature which is to be regarded as the fundamental determination," and accordingly "the calculus is concerned not with variable magnitudes *as such* but with **relations of powers**" (*SL* §569). A merely linear function such as *y* = *ax* is, he says, "only *formally* a function of variable magnitudes" — which is exactly why the affine example above fell outside the calculus' proper business. He is no kinder to the received vocabulary: "The expression 'variable magnitudes' is therefore very vague and ill-chosen" (*SL* §568).) The differential coefficient is a *quantum that has become the sign of a quality*. This is why the operation works: in passing to the differential we have not found a smaller quantum but *left quantum behind*, distilling out of the vanishing magnitudes the qualitative relation they embodied.

This is the **true quantitative infinite** in mathematical dress — the same result §VI won from the bare progress, now made visible in the most powerful instrument the exact sciences possess. The quantum has so thoroughly transcended itself — pushed its self-sending-beyond all the way to the vanishing of magnitude as such — that what remains on the far side is no longer a quantum awaiting a still-larger or still-smaller successor (that was the spurious infinite, the endless series), but a *qualitative relation* in which the beyond is *present*, gathered, determinate. Hegel's own formulation is the warrant for everything this installment has claimed: the infinite quantum is "a quantitative determinateness in **qualitative form**; its infinity consists in its being a *qualitative* determinateness. As such moment, it is in essential unity with its other… **Apart from this relation it is a nullity**" (*SL* §550). The infinite is no longer the unreachable next term; it is *here*, in the ratio, as the curve's actual slope. Note well what this does and does not claim: it is not the exchange of one infinite for another, larger one. The "infinity" of the true infinite is the very *opposite* of the series' endlessness — a determinacy complete in itself, owing nothing to a beyond. The calculus does not out-run the endless regress with a bigger infinite; it *ends* the regress, drawing out the finite relation the regress was groping toward. (The parallel to §13 is close but not exact, and §V has already registered the difference: there the finite continued into its other only *implicitly*, whereas here the quantum "is self-related in its infinite" — *SL* §499. The true infinite was not the endless line but the circle, the beyond brought home as a moment of a present whole; the true quantitative infinite is that same victory won again in the element of magnitude, and won more openly.)

Hegel pauses here to credit a predecessor, and the credit matters for a thread this synopsis takes up again at §25. The distinction between the true and the spurious infinite is Spinoza's before it is his. Spinoza sets *the infinite of the imagination* — the endless series, infinity as an unbounded quantum — against *the infinite of thought*, the *infinitum actu*, infinity as a present determinateness. The emblem Spinoza chose for it, printed as the motto of the *Ethics*, is the space between two non-concentric unequal circles: a space perfectly bounded, and yet infinite, "because the nature of the fact surpasses every determinateness" (*SL* §§564–566). No summing of parts will ever produce that space; it is given at once, by a relation — which is the fraction 2/7 again, drawn rather than written. Hegel's qualification is the one he always enters against Spinoza: "with Spinoza, substance and its absolute unity has the form of an **inert** unity… the Notion of the negative unity of the self, i.e. **subjectivity**, is still lacking." The true infinite is grasped, but not yet as self-moving. (He notes in passing a terminological trap worth keeping in mind, since it inverts modern usage: in this literature the "metaphysical" infinite is the merely *relative* one — *SL* §563.)

Recall §III's seed: in the square the quantum was first related to *itself*, amount and unit coinciding — the first faint return of self-relation, of the qualitative, within number. The power-relation is that seed grown. The differential lives among the *powers* for a reason: Hegel locates the whole "qualitative" content of the calculus in the relation a magnitude bears to its own powers, and the self-relation that first stirred in the square is here fully posited as a qualitative relation quantity carries within itself.

A correction is owed on the historical attribution, because it is natural to assume Hegel favours the algebraic route through power series that Lagrange developed. He does not. Reviewing the historical definitions, it is **Newton** who receives the palm for the concept: "**The thought cannot be more correctly determined than in the way Newton has stated it**" (*SL* §576) — the doctrine not of indivisibles but of *vanishing divisibles*, and of ratios taken not as ratios of determinate parts but as "the **limits** of the sums and ratios," the ratio "with which they vanish." Hegel's reservation about Newton is of a different order: that he did only what "the scientific method of his time demanded," supplying an explanation of what was to be understood — "a subjective presumption, or a historical demand" — and that "decreasing without limit" still merely expresses the progress to infinity (*SL* §§577–578). Lagrange enters mainly as an *objector*, holding that the limit-ratio yields no clear concept once the terms have become zero; to which Hegel replies that "the understanding must, indeed, transcend this merely negative side… and must grasp them **positively, as qualitative moments**" (*SL* §585). Leibniz fares worse: in him "the call to *neglect*" is "more strikingly prominent," and it is "chiefly" this that "has given this calculus the appearance of inexactitude and express incorrectness" — since "an absolutely exact result could not emerge from an inexact method" (*SL* §§582–583). Euler comes nearer, treating the infinite difference as "a nil only of quantum, **not a qualitative nil**," but errs in calling the increments zeros outright, "for a zero no longer has any determinateness at all" (*SL* §584). And running through all of them Hegel names one persistent error: the conceptions of increment and growth of *x* by *dx* "are to be regarded as **the fundamental vice in these methods** — the permanent obstacle to disengaging the determination of the qualitative moment of quantity in its purity from the conception of the ordinary quantum" (*SL* §581).

## VIII. Three Misreadings, Answered Directly

The quantum invites three confusions worth meeting head-on.

The first is the **mathematician's foundational misreading**: that Hegel is offering a *rival foundation* for the calculus — a philosophical theory of infinitesimals competing with limits, or with Weierstrass's *ε–δ*, or with Robinson's non-standard analysis. He is not, and to read him so is to lose his point and to expose him to easy refutation. His claim is *logical*: that the success of the technique, however one founds it, exhibits a determinate logical form — the passage of quantum, through self-transcendence, into qualitative ratio. Whether one reaches *dy/dx* through vanishing magnitudes, through limits, or through non-standard infinitesimals, what one *reaches* is a qualitative determinateness wearing quantitative dress, and that is what the logical thesis requires. (This is why the contested status of his infinitesimal-talk does not touch the load-bearing claim — though foundation-indifference is our gloss on that thesis, and not all Hegel himself demands, as the next paragraph shows.)

That disclaimer must be calibrated, however, or it becomes a misreading in its own right — the opposite error, and one this synopsis previously committed. Hegel is emphatically **not** neutral about the state of the foundations, and it will not do to have him politely leave the matter to the mathematicians. Mathematics, he writes, "has not yet succeeded in justifying its use of this infinite by the Notion… it is even admitted that the operation itself is **incorrect**" (*SL* §538), and consequently "is unable to determine the scope of its application and to secure itself against the misuse of it" (*SL* §539). Against the standing defence that the results come out right, he is uncompromising, in a sentence worth having in any age tempted to accept a method because it delivers: "**success does not by itself justify the mode of procedure**… For mathematical knowledge is *scientific* knowledge, so that the proof is essential" (*SL* §542). The operations "would therefore have to be justified **solely by the Notion**" (*SL* §572). What is true is that Hegel does not compete on the mathematicians' own ground; what is false is that he concedes it to them. (He is also honest about the reach of his own reading, and the concession should be recorded: "in the actual application of the method of infinitesimals, the genuine Notion of the infinite cannot exercise any influence; on the contrary, there is a return of the finite determinateness of quantity and the operation cannot dispense with the conception of a quantum which is merely relatively small" — *SL* §586. The logical truth of the calculus does not do the calculating.)

The second is the **intensive-as-qualitative misreading**: that degree, being simple and inward, is already the return of quality within quantity — that intensity is where magnitude becomes qualitative. §IV is the answer, in its corrected form: degree is not quantity become genuinely qualitative, but the *same* quantum with its amount gathered into itself instead of spread out — extensive and intensive being "one and the same determinateness" (*SL* §481). Something does stir here, and Hegel grants it: with that identity "the *qualitative something* makes its appearance" (*SL* §482). But this is a first and formal appearance, not the threshold, and it is not the sphere's turning-point. The genuine return of quality comes through the second negation of §VI, and is displayed in the ratio — through the power-relation, not the grade.

The third is the **bad-infinity-as-achievement misreading**: that the endless number series, the ever-larger and ever-smaller, the completed infinite of set theory, is the mathematical realization of the true infinite. It is the opposite: the endless series is the *spurious* infinite par excellence, the quantitative form of the very "and-so-on" that §13 unmasked. The true infinite in mathematics is not the inexhaustible series but the *finite expression* — the function, the ratio, the closed form — in which the series' endless beyond is brought home as a present, qualitative determinateness. (Two things here are ours and not Hegel's, and we own them as such. First, the *application* to Cantor: Hegel wrote well before transfinite set theory, and extending his critique to it is our argument, not a report of his. Second, that extension is genuinely contestable, and we think honestly so. A completed infinite set is precisely *not* an endless successive construction — which is the feature Hegel's critique actually targets — and an exact sum of a convergent series requires no final summand. A reader who holds that Cantor's actual infinite stands nearer to the *infinitum actu* than to the spurious progress has a real case, and Spinoza's bounded-yet-infinite space above rather strengthens it. What we take to be secure is the narrower claim, and it is Hegel's own: the endless progress *as such* — the and-so-on, the always-one-more — is a deficiency and not an achievement.)

## IX. What the Method Did Here — Observations

Three observations, each carrying past this installment.

The first: **quality returns within quantity by quantity's own movement, not by importation from outside.** This is the structural heart of the whole sphere and the answer to the scientism §15 warned against. Quantity set out as the determinacy indifferent to quality — the realm where the *what* drops away and only the *how-much* remains, the ground the measuring sciences stand on. We have now watched that very indifference undo itself *from within*. The quantum's indifferent limit drove it past every magnitude; the second negation of §VI posited it as *ratio*; and the ratio's content turned out to be *qualitative* — in Hegel's strict sense a relation of powers, of which the rate and the law of the curve are the readings we take. No quality was smuggled in; the quantitative, followed honestly to its end, *produced* the qualitative as its own truth. The sciences that take the quantitative as bedrock have, in their most powerful instrument — the calculus — been operating across this very threshold all along, computing qualities in the guise of computing magnitudes. (The wider list — fields, gradients, conservation laws — is our conjecture and not Hegel's result; his instance is the differential coefficient, and the reader is invited to test the extension rather than receive it.)

The converse deserves recording too, because the doctrine it refutes keeps returning under new names: the attempt to treat *all* difference as difference of degree. "That all opposition is only quantitative was for some time a cardinal thesis of recent philosophy," Hegel notes — and the defect is diagnosable in exactly the terms just won. The quantitative is "only the imperfect, as yet only *first*, negation, **not the infinite, not the negation of the negation**. When being and thought are represented as quantitative determinations of absolute substance they too, as quanta, become **completely external to each other and unrelated** as, in a subordinate sphere, do carbon, nitrogen, etc." (*SL* §517). A programme that dissolves every distinction into a difference of degree does not thereby unify its subject-matter. It guarantees the opposite: that the terms it has levelled will fall apart into mutual indifference, since indifference is precisely what the quantitative determination *is*.

The second: **the calculus is Hegel's own model of what scientific comprehension is.** This is the sentence in the whole Remark that bears most directly on an inquiry into method, and it is easy to walk past among the mathematics. Describing the purification of the quantitative ratio into the qualitative, Hegel writes: "This purification of the quantitative ratio is thus **analogous to grasping an empirical reality in terms of its Notion**. The empirical reality is thereby raised above itself in such a way that its Notion contains the same characteristic features as it has itself, but these are grasped **in their essentiality** and are taken **into the unity of the Notion**, in which they have lost their indifferent, Notion-less existence" (*SL* §580). That is a general account of comprehension, delivered in miniature and with a worked example on the table. What a science does when it grasps a phenomenon by its concept is what the calculus does to a ratio of magnitudes: it adds nothing from outside and discards none of the determinations it found; it *raises* them out of indifferent juxtaposition into a unity in which each is what it is only through the others. Every ascent in the *Logic* so far has had this shape — and here, for once, Hegel names the shape while demonstrating it.

The third: **the same logical form recurs across spheres in a new element, and recognizing it is most of the work.** The quantitative infinite is not a new and separate problem; it is the finite-and-its-spurious-infinite of §13 transposed into magnitude, resolved in the same shape — the beyond brought home into a present whole (there the circle, here the ratio). But it is recurrence *with difference*, and §V recorded Hegel's own insistence on the point: the quantum is affirmatively self-related in its infinite where the finite was so only implicitly (*SL* §499). Nor does the recurrence license a master key. To have grasped the form once is to be *equipped* to recognize it — not excused from looking, and not entitled to expect it at every step; §13 entered that caution on its own account, and §27 will show the limiting case, where the closing move of the whole *Logic*, the Idea's release into Nature, is expressly *not* a transition of this kind. What the reader who learned in §13 to distrust the endless progress carries forward is not a formula but a trained eye — enough to see, in *dy/dx*, not a paradox of the infinitely small but the quantitative homecoming of the infinite.

## X. What Comes Next

With number, degree, and the quantitative infinite, the quantum is complete — and §VI has already *derived*, not merely promised, its truth: the quantum, self-related as an indifferent limit in its own externality and thereby posited as qualitative, **is** quantitative ratio (*SL* §537). The next installment therefore does not have to establish the ratio; it unfolds what has been established. It develops the third and final moment of Quantity, the **Quantitative Ratio** (*das quantitative Verhältnis*), through its forms:

- The **direct ratio** (the simple proportion, with its **exponent**), where each quantum is what it is only in its proportion to the other, and the exponent — itself a quantum — already does a *qualitative* work, being what makes *this* ratio this ratio.
- The **inverse ratio** and the **ratio of powers**, in which the qualitative role of the exponent deepens until the relation, not the related quanta, carries the determinacy.
- The passage by which the quantitative determinacy, now inseparably bound up with a qualitative one, ceases to be merely quantity: the threshold of **Measure** (*Maß*), the unity of quality and quantity — and with it the close of the whole second sub-circle of the Doctrine of Being, and the gateway (through the nodal line of measure-relations) toward Essence.

The example we deferred from §15 — the quantization of energy — finds its home there, in Measure, as a nodal point: a quantum welded to a quality, where crossing a threshold is a qualitative leap.

## Coda

We entered Quantity at the place modern thought takes for its bedrock, and we have now watched the bedrock open. The quantum looked like the simplest thing in the world — *this much*, a definite magnitude, the very atom of the measurable. And we have seen it carry, in its indifferent limit, the same restlessness the finite carried; seen it send itself past every magnitude into the spurious infinite of the endless series; and seen that restlessness, pursued without flinching, negate not only every quantum but *its own beyond* — and by that second negation deliver up not a smaller quantum but a *ratio*, whose content is no longer a how-much but a *how*, a quality. Only then does the most quantitative instrument humanity possesses, the calculus, disclose that it has been computing qualities in the costume of magnitudes all along: not the source of the lesson but its most vivid confirmation. This is the sphere's great irony and its deepest lesson: quantity, the determination that defined itself by its indifference to quality, cannot sustain that indifference, and discloses quality again as its own innermost truth — not by abandoning the quantitative but by carrying it through to the end. *The number, followed far enough, stops being merely a number; in the ratio of its own vanishing it speaks — foreshadowed faintly in the square and in the degree, now decisively — the language of the qualitative, and what speaks there is already on the way to Measure, where the two are one.*
