# The Quantum: Number, Degree, and the Quantitative Infinite

*The seventh installment of Section III — a close reading of the second moment of Quantity. The **quantum** (*Quantum*) and its **indifferent limit**; **Number** (*Zahl*) as the unity of **amount** (*Anzahl*) and **unit** (*Einheit*), with the species of calculation ordered by their equalization, but expressly not presented by Hegel as an immanent development of the Concept; **extensive** and **intensive magnitude**, or **degree** (*Grad*), derived as two forms of the same quantitative determinateness; the **quantitative infinite**, where the demand to escape every limit reproduces the limit, and Hegel's criticism of the sublime and of **Kant's First Antinomy**, distinguished from Kant's own resolution; the **second negation**, in which both the quantum's independence and its opposed beyond are sublated, yielding **quantitative ratio**; and the long-deferred examination of the **differential calculus** — Hegel's interpretation of its **qualitative** content, his differentiated assessments of Newton and Lagrange, and the boundary between reconstructing a logical form and establishing a mathematical proof. The return of quality is derived before these mathematical Remarks; neither their historical achievements nor their limitations may be settled by the derivation alone.*

---

## I. Where We Are: from Pure Quantity's Self-Limitation to the Quantum

The previous installment left quantity in the act of limiting itself. Pure quantity was the unity of continuity and discreteness, initially in the form of continuity, without a posited quantitative limit. Their distinction was then developed into continuous and discrete magnitude. Discrete magnitude, posited as one magnitude and distinguished from continuous magnitude, has the One as its determinateness and first limit; self-related within the unity, this is an *enclosing, encompassing* limit. But the continuity it limits passes beyond it and remains indifferent to it. Both forms thus become **quanta**: quantity has acquired a limit, without the argument yet determining a particular amount. It is this limited quantity, rather than pure quantity defined in advance by more-and-less, that the present installment develops.

The movement has three main stages. **Number** gives the quantum its completely posited form. **Extensive and intensive magnitude** develop the relation between its plurality and its simple determinateness. Their contradiction — determination within itself which is nevertheless determination through externality — becomes explicit in the **quantitative infinite**. The endless progress first opposes every quantum to an unattained beyond; its resolution gathers both into a unity whose moments are related quanta. This is **quantitative ratio**, not the disappearance of quantity. Its further development belongs to §17. Hegel's mathematical Remarks examine forms in which he finds this qualitative determination of quantity; they do not supply the logical transition in its place (*SL* §§437, 530–537).

## II. The Quantum and Its Indifferent Limit

The quantitative limit is indifferent to being, but not meaningless. A line may become longer without ceasing to be a line; yet a length of seven units is not a length of five. **The limit determines this quantum without determining the being of what has it.** This is the distinction developed from quality, where the limit belonged to what the Something was. It does not assert that every concrete thing can sustain unlimited quantitative alteration: the connection of amount with qualitative change remains to be developed in Measure.

The quantum therefore combines two determinations. It is *this much*, and its limit is constitutive of that amount; but this limit has no qualitative authority to hold quantity at this amount rather than another. The One, retained within quantity as its own moment, is both continuous with the many ones and an enclosing, other-excluding limit (*SL* §§439–440). The resulting determinacy is not an independently existing stuff plus a boundary imposed afterwards. Its complete form must exhibit the limit in the very plurality it limits.

## III. Number — the Quantum's Complete Form

That form is **Number** (*Zahl*). The limit is now posited as a determinate plurality: the enclosed ones constitute the **amount** (*Anzahl*), the form of discreteness within number; their continuity, their being equal ones of the same number, is the **unit** (*Einheit*). Number is the unity of these moments (*SL* §§441–442). Seven is seven ones, not an unbounded plurality to which a seventh item happens to attach a stop.

Hegel makes this last distinction explicit. In a hundred, it may appear that only the hundredth one supplies the limit. But no one has precedence over another: each is equally eligible to be counted last, and none can be dispensed with while preserving the hundred. **All the ones belong to the limit**; the number itself constitutes the limitation (*SL* §443). Its indifference to other numbers is consequently joined to an externality within itself — its own plurality of equal ones. Number holds these together in an immediate unity; the subsequent development exposes the contradiction rather than importing a relation into something originally relationless (*SL* §444).

The arithmetic Remark orders the **species of calculation** (*Rechnungsarten*) by the progressive equalization of amount and unit (*SL* §§450–460). Its principle is intelligible without treating each operation as a dialectical transition:

- **Numbering** collects ones into an amount.
- **Addition and subtraction** combine or separate numbers without requiring equality between those combined.
- **Multiplication and division** make equal numbers a unit and determine the amount of such units: four threes can be counted as four units, each of three.
- **Raising to a power and extracting a root** bring the two determinations into a further equality, completed, for Hegel, in the **square**: in three threes, unit and amount are each three. Higher powers introduce no new equality of these moments; with odd powers, inequality between unit and amount reappears. Taking a root is the corresponding inverse operation.

The square thus gives the later discussion of powers an elementary point of reference, but not its completed derivation. Hegel calls the square alone arithmetically endowed with an immanent absolute determinedness and connects this with the demand to reduce higher equations to quadratics; he also says that equations with odd exponents can be determined only formally (*SL* §459). Calling that determination formal is not, by itself, a denial of an exact algebraic value. **The ordering does not establish that mathematical demand.** Equality of unit and amount supplies neither a procedure for reducing a given equation nor a proof of that procedure's range. Those require a mathematical argument; the arithmetic comparison alone cannot supply it.

More decisively for method, Hegel himself warns that this ordering is **not an immanent development of the Concept**. Its material is intrinsically self-external, and its progressive determination can therefore take place only externally; imposing an inappropriate form would distort both the subject matter and the Ideas. He nonetheless recommends the ordering for elementary instruction (*SL* §461). The following critique of numerical symbolism likewise distinguishes the abstraction achieved by number from its inadequacy as a substitute for concrete thought; even the educational assessment grants the importance of an inward, non-sensuous activity while rejecting mechanical calculation as the principal education of mind (*SL* §§462–471). We retain both sides: a reasoned organization of arithmetic is an achievement, but neither counting nor arranging its operations supplies the Concept's self-development.

## IV. Extensive and Intensive Magnitude — One Quantum, Two Forms

Number immediately exhibits the limit as an amount, a plurality within the quantum: this is **extensive magnitude** (*extensive Größe*). It is not a synonym for continuous magnitude, nor is intensive magnitude another name for discreteness. Continuity and discreteness were moments of quantity; the extensive–intensive distinction concerns the **form of its determinate limit** (*SL* §§472–473).

Why does the extensive form pass into the intensive? Its many ones are equal and continuous with one another. Their mere dispersion therefore does not express what makes the number *this one number*: the plurality is gathered into its simple, self-related determinateness. Hegel describes the many as collapsing into oneness. The limit, previously exhibited as an amount, is now posited as a simple determination, a **degree** (*Grad*): not twenty degrees added together, but the twentieth degree (*SL* §§474–476). This movement is not produced by reading a measuring instrument. The instrument can illustrate a determinateness whose logical mediation has already been given.

The degree is nevertheless *this* degree. Since the plurality is no longer exhibited within its simple form, its quantitative determination refers beyond it to other degrees; it is self-related through an externality of which it cannot be independent (*SL* §§477–479). But rank among other degrees is only one side. Hegel insists on the other: “**The twentieth degree contains the twenty within itself**” (*SL* §480). The amount is its own amount, not merely the amount of neighbouring degrees. Insofar as that amount is its own determination, degree is equally an extensive quantum. Thus the two forms are “**one and the same determinateness**” (*SL* §481): the extensive many gathers into the simple degree, and that degree requires the amount as its own.

With their identity “**the qualitative something makes its appearance**” (*SL* §482). The mediation matters. The one determinateness persists through the negation of its extensive and intensive forms as independent differences. This self-related unity is a Something confronting those quantitative determinations as *its* determinations, indifferent to its quantitative determinateness. The result is therefore not that intensity has mysteriously become a quality. It is the distinction between a qualitative Something and the extensive and intensive quantum it has. This is not yet Measure, where quantitative determinacy itself belongs to qualitative determinacy.

Hegel's examples include the degree of heat and the height of a mercury column, or the higher pitch of a note and the greater number of vibrations. His intensive determination of pitch should not be confused with acoustic intensity or loudness; he treats the louder note separately. He also grants that the dynamic relation of force and expression is “**in the first instance truer**” than that of whole and parts, while refusing to make its intensive side self-sufficient (*SL* §§484–491). These are illustrations, not deductions of a physical scale or of a law connecting different magnitudes. His qualification is explicit: the argument applies to an existence **insofar as it manifests a quantitative character** (*SL* §486). It does not prove that pain, talent, or utility admits a numerical measure, nor that an ordinal ranking alone establishes an amount. Our methodological inference is correspondingly bounded: where quantitative determinacy is established, its intensive presentation is not by itself a reason to declare it unquantifiable.

## V. The Quantitative Infinite — the Quantum's Self-Transcendence

The quantum's alteration is not merely an option exercised by someone measuring it. Its own determinateness is continuity with an other: to be this amount is to possess a limit indifferent to quantity, a limit through which quantity continues. Hegel therefore develops not just the possibility but the necessity of alteration in the category (*SL* §§493–497). The quantum goes beyond itself into another quantum, which equally goes beyond itself. This is the **quantitative infinite** in its first form.

Its resemblance to §13 must not obscure either development. Hegel contrasts the initial qualitative opposition, in which the finite and its beyond appear as mutually excluding, with the quantitative relation, in which the quantum continues into its other without ceasing to be quantity (*SL* §§499–500). He is not denying the affirmative infinity already achieved in §13. The difference concerns the form in which the opposition is initially present, not the capacity of one sphere alone to resolve it.

If the demanded infinite is sought merely as an ever-greater or ever-smaller quantum, the search repeats what it would escape. “**The increase of quantum brings it no nearer to the infinite; for the difference between quantum and its infinity is essentially not a quantitative difference**” (*SL* §502). Each advance supplies another finite determinacy and renews the demand for a beyond. This is the **spurious quantitative infinite** (*das schlechte quantitative Unendliche*): not an insufficiently large number, but the unresolved opposition between quantum and what is supposed not to be quantum. Hegel calls its progress “**an impotence of the negative**” (*SL* §503).

His Remark attacks the prestige of this endlessness: the sublime journey through vast times and spaces does not become comprehension by becoming more exhausting. Haller's poem supplies a case in which the journey itself declares its futility (*SL* §§504–508). Astronomy deserves admiration not for sheer vastness but for “**the relations of measure and the laws which reason recognises in these objects**” (*SL* §509). Hegel also records Kant's dissatisfaction with an exaltation which cannot satisfy reason (*SL* §512). The issue is not whether wonder has a place in inquiry; it is whether enlarging its object supplies the determination the inquiry seeks.

**Kant's First Antinomy** gives the opposition a cosmological form. The thesis argues that a beginningless past would require an infinite successive synthesis to have been completed; its spatial argument likewise concerns the synthesis of an allegedly given infinite whole. The antithesis argues that empty time supplies no distinguishing condition for the world's beginning, while a spatial boundary would involve the world's relation to empty space, to no object. These are arguments under Kant's conditions of sensible representation, not simply the assertion and denial of a largest number (*Critique of Pure Reason*, A426/B454–A433/B461).

Hegel reads the opposed proofs as presupposing their results: that a limit is, and that it is equally sublated (*SL* §§518–528). He anticipates the distinction between a present endpoint and an absolute beginning. His dilemma is that a merely flowing, quantitative now cannot close an elapsed infinite series, whereas a completed, qualitative boundary breaks time off and thus supplies an absolute beginning (*SL* §523). He reinforces this by treating time as pure quantity and the completed now as an abstract point (*SL* §524). **This criticism must itself be tested on its premises.** A series described as elapsed *up to* a given time need not include the later states: completion of that represented series and interruption of time itself are different claims. Hegel's identification of the two therefore requires more than the endpoint's being determinate; this objection does not, conversely, prove Kant's claim that an infinite successive synthesis is impossible. Against the temporal antithesis, Hegel argues that requiring an antecedent temporal condition already presupposes the continuation to be proved; against the spatial antithesis, he contests the relation to empty space itself (*SL* §§526–527). These are challenges to Kant's premises, not merely replacements of them by an assertion of unlimitedness.

Kant's own resolution rejects the world-totality as a given thing in itself and makes continued empirical regress a regulative demand, not a dogmatic assertion of an actually infinite world (*Critique of Pure Reason*, A518/B546–A523/B551). Both dogmatic alternatives lose their common presupposition. Hegel's charge that contradiction is transferred to reason and left unresolved (*SL* §529) does not by itself answer that dissolution. Kant distinguishes the persistence of a natural illusion from continuing to be deceived by it; his rule for inquiry does not promise a completed world-totality. Indefinite continuation is therefore not, merely as such, a failed attempt to attain that totality. But the dispute is not only about failure: Kant's rule still requires inquiry to pass beyond every given condition. Hegel also criticizes Kant's transcendental definition of infinity, given in the observation on the thesis, as an uncompleted synthesis which distributes contradiction between object and subject (*SL* §548). That argument presupposes a quantum completed in itself, whereas Kant's resolution does not grant a world-quantity given in itself. Whether the regulative demand escapes the unresolved ought of Hegel's progress, and adequately accounts for the opposed determinations rather than developing them into a unity, remains a distinct philosophical question — not one settled by the physical extent or age of the universe.

The same distinction protects the mathematical discussion. An endless instruction to seek one more term is not the same object as an infinite sequence, its law, or the exact sum of a convergent series. A sum need not be a last partial sum; an asymptote need not be a destination the curve fails to reach. Hegel's criticism bears on the demand for an escape from limitation by mere quantitative continuation. Its application to a particular mathematical construction must establish that this demand is actually operative there. The logical resolution follows next; the mathematical Remarks then test its expression in their own subject matter.

## VI. The Turn: the Beyond Itself Vanishes

The progress seems to say only that every quantum can be surpassed. Hegel asks us to consider what happens to the beyond as well: “**this self-sublating of quantum, the beyond, the spurious infinite itself also vanishes**” (*SL* §533). Every allegedly non-quantitative beyond is again determined as quantum. Neither the immediate quantum nor its opposed infinite maintains the independence attributed to it. The truth is their unity, with each retained as a moment — not a last member reached by running the series farther.

Degree had already exhibited the ground: its simple self-relation is determination through an externality which is itself quantitative. Quantum is thus with itself in what was presented as its non-being (*SL* §§531–532). The progress makes this explicit. Its externality is no longer simply somewhere else; it belongs to the quantum's own determination. Hence Hegel's result: “**the quantum has reverted to quality**” (*SL* §534). Quantity is qualitatively determined insofar as this relation to its other constitutes what it is.

Hegel also calls the movement a restoration of negated quality (*SL* §§535–536). That formulation summarizes the mediation; it is not a general recipe by which negating anything twice restores its predecessor. Quantity retained qualitative negation in the indifferent limit. The quantum's attempt to place its determination wholly beyond itself has now disclosed that externality as its own moment. Quality returns in this determinate form, not as an unchanged content released from suppression.

The result accordingly retains **two quanta**, “**only as moments of one unity**”; quantum so self-related in its externality “**is quantitative ratio**” (*SL* §537). What has been sublated is their independent indifference to the relation, not every quantitative determination. The ratio is derived here; its direct, inverse, and power forms remain to be developed. The return of quality is therefore not yet the completed transition into Measure.

The mathematical Remarks follow this derivation. Hegel reads the calculus as exhibiting a related transformation: determinations formerly taken as independent magnitudes have their meaning in a relation. This is his interpretation to be examined, not a warrant to declare in advance that mathematics performs the movement without understanding it.

## VII. The Calculus: the Vanishing Magnitude and the Qualitative Ratio

Hegel begins with the **ordinary fraction**. In *2/7*, the numbers count according to their relation; *4/14* or *6/21* can replace them without altering its value (*SL* §§551–552). Their quantitative determinateness remains, but their role within the fraction is relational. The next paragraph expressly limits this achievement: the numbers can still be taken outside the relation as independent quanta, and the ratio's exponent is itself a quantum. This expression of infinity is therefore **imperfect** (*SL* §553). Replacing the numbers by letters does not remove the limitation, since the letters still stand for finite magnitudes (*SL* §554).

Set the fraction beside *0.285714…*. Hegel calls the apparently finite expression the truly infinite one: it presents the determinate value which any finite continuation of the decimal leaves incomplete. In the series, as he considers it, the negative lies outside the terms already present; in the ratio, the sides reciprocally determine one another (*SL* §§558–561). This identifies a real difference between a determinate relation and an unfinished enumeration. It does **not**, under modern mathematical definitions, establish that the infinite decimal lacks the value of the fraction: the decimal denotes the limit of its partial sums, not one of those partial sums. Nor does lack of a closed form imply lack of an exact sum. Hegel himself distinguishes incommensurability from the mere incompleteness of a serial presentation (*SL* §562), but his distinction must not be converted into a universal equivalence between truth and finite notation.

His discussion of **Spinoza** gives another bounded example. Hegel credits the distinction between an infinite sought in an endless aggregate and an infinite actually present; he invokes the unequal, non-concentric circles of Spinoza's letter on the infinite (*SL* §§564–567). In [Letter 12 to Meyer, as transmitted in Leibniz's excerpts and translated by R. T. W. Arthur](https://www.humanities.mcmaster.ca/~rarthur/trans/Spinoza.on.infinite.pdf), what exceeds every assignable number is the inequalities of the intervening space and the variations of motion through it, although maximum and minimum distances are known. The claim is not that the bounded region has an infinite area, nor that its area cannot be computed by integration. Its comparison with the fraction is Hegel's interpretation, not an identity between the two mathematical objects. He also judges Spinoza's affirmative unity insufficiently self-mediated (*SL* §565); the separate question of that judgment's warrant returns in §25. We do not infer it merely from the geometrical example.

The next stage is a **relation of powers**. In *y²/x = p*, the quotient of *y²* and *x* is fixed, but that of *y* and *x* is not; the powers through which the magnitudes are related belong to the relation's determination. This, rather than variability alone, is Hegel's principal criterion for the qualitative content of the calculus (*SL* §§568–569). He regards a linear or affine function such as *y = ax + b*, differentiated for its own sake, as yielding no new relation: the coefficient is already available (*SL* §615). This supplies the positive reason for his restriction, but not a mathematical prohibition. The derivative of an affine function is well-defined and has a place in the general theory; failure to discover a previously unknown coefficient is not failure to differentiate.

For the **differentials**, Hegel states the relational determination more sharply: “**it is solely in their relation to each other that they have any meaning, a meaning merely as moments**” (*SL* §570). *dx* and *dy* are not independent minute quanta, but neither are they empty nullities within the relation. The point is not that two quantities have individually become zero and nevertheless yield a quotient. Hegel explicitly rejects that account when discussing Euler: a zero has no determinateness from which the required relation could be recovered (*SL* §§584–585). The quantities are sublated **as independently determinate quanta**, while quantitative determination remains in the relation (*SL* §§570–574).

His reference to **Becoming** answers the objection that vanishing would be an impossible intermediate *state* between being and nothing (*SL* §573). The earlier unity was not such a state; neither should this movement be frozen into one. But the reference does not identify a differential with the opening category or derive a particular differential coefficient from it. The new subject matter and its determinate relations still have to be established.

A present-day example helps mark both the insight and its limit. For *f(x) = x²*, the difference quotient with non-zero increment *h* is *2x + h*, and its limit as *h* tends to zero is *2x*. This does not evaluate *0/0*: it determines a limit from values for which division is defined. The result gives the **slope of the tangent at a point**, not the curve's entire character. Distinct curves can have the same slope at a point, and even the whole derivative function leaves an additive constant undetermined. Hegel's relational reading illuminates why the result is not an independently surviving infinitesimal length; it does not replace the mathematical argument establishing the limit or its geometrical meaning (§07).

The historical assessment is correspondingly differentiated. Newton receives exceptional praise for the account of ratios **with which** magnitudes vanish, rather than ratios of final indivisibles (*SL* §576). Hegel then criticizes the return to indefinite diminution and the use of increments, and later contests particular Newtonian demonstrations (*SL* §§577–581, 590–592). His attack extends to proofs for which Newton was elevated above Kepler: he charges them with arranging an appearance of proof around an already known result (*SL* §606). Leibniz's appeal to neglecting higher infinitesimals is criticized because empirical insignificance cannot justify an exact result; Euler's treatment approaches the required relational determination but lapses into speaking of zeros (*SL* §§582–585). These are Hegel's assessments of the presentations he examines, not a comprehensive ranking of their authors' mathematics.

**Lagrange is not merely an objector to Newton.** Hegel recognizes the precision, abstraction, and generality of his functional calculus, although criticizing its reliance on series and quantitative smallness (*SL* §595). More importantly, he praises Lagrange for separating two tasks: obtaining the derived function algebraically, and proving which geometrical relation it determines. In the tangent problem, this separation is “**the genuinely scientific course**” (*SL* §629). The algebraic operation alone does not identify its result with a tangent; the application supplies a second argument. The treatment of integration likewise requires proof of the relation between an original and a derived function in the concrete mathematical object, not merely an instruction to reverse differentiation (*SL* §§638–651).

This is where the discussion speaks most directly to scientific method. The origin of a useful procedure in particular problems is not its justification; conversely, an algebraic derivation does not automatically establish the physical or geometrical meaning assigned to its terms. Hegel distinguishes these questions and credits an actual advance in geometry. He also bounds his own exposition: he has not reviewed the whole range of applications or completed the induction that all conform to his principle (*SL* §650). The third Remark further treats qualitative determination through dimensions and the distinction between continuous figures and the elements determining their magnitudes; its scope is not exhausted by the earlier power-function example (*SL* §§652–668).

His judgments about mechanics require the same scrutiny. Hegel denies that mathematics alone can establish physical laws grounded in the qualitative nature of their moments, while expressly allowing qualitative premises to be taken from experience (*SL* §607). A derivation's validity under those premises is distinct from their physical warrant. His objection to treating terms of an analytical expansion as independently existing motions preserves that distinction; his rejection of velocity at an instant does not settle it. He argues that velocity requires a traversed interval and cannot be fixed at its endpoint (*SL* §§634–635). Under the modern definition, however, instantaneous velocity is the limit of average velocities over nonzero intervals, not distance traversed in a zero interval. For *s(t) = at²*, that limit is *2at*. The calculation neither makes the motion uniform over a finite interval nor assigns a separate physical force to a term of the expansion. Whether it describes a body still requires an interpretation of *s* and *t* and evidence for the assumed law; the calculus alone supplies neither.

## VIII. Three Misreadings, Answered Directly

**The first is that a logical interpretation either replaces mathematical foundations or leaves them wholly untouched.** Neither description fits Hegel. He expressly grants that mathematics need not generate the content of its objects through the Concept and has secured precision within its sphere (*SL* §541). His objection is that the historical calculus allegedly violates its own requirements by treating its infinitesimals both unlike and like finite magnitudes. “**Success does not by itself justify the mode of procedure**”; mathematical knowledge requires proof (*SL* §542). He therefore demands more than a philosophical redescription of successful calculation and claims that only the Concept can justify the disputed operations (*SL* §572).

That last claim must itself meet the criterion, including Hegel's direct examination of limits. He recognizes in the limit the qualitative determination of a relation, but objects that deriving the limiting coefficient again sets the increments to zero while retaining their ratio. He also argues that a difference required to be smaller than any given difference is no longer quantitative (*SL* §§596–598). His alternative is to obtain the derived function algebraically and then establish its further meaning in application (*SL* §600). The disagreement is therefore not confined to an incautious use of the word *infinitesimal*.

The modern limit proof answers the objection by distinguishing the order of its demands. For the example *f(x) = x²*, the difference between the nonzero-increment quotient and its proposed limit *2x* is *h*. For each prescribed positive tolerance, every nonzero *h* whose absolute value is smaller than that tolerance makes the error smaller as well. No single nonzero difference must be smaller than every positive tolerance; the permitted bound on *h* depends on the tolerance. Nor is *h = 0* assigned in the quotient. The limiting value is determined by this relation among the defined values, not by turning a negligible error into an absent one. This answers Hegel's stated objection to approximation without abandoning quantitative differences. **Our reconstruction retains the relational insight but does not claim that Hegel already accepted modern foundations or that his interpretation settles their adequacy.** His demand to distinguish formal operations from their application remains substantive, including where a modern theory supplies the proof he sought.

**The second is that degree is already quantity transformed into quality.** Degree is still the same quantitative determinateness as extensive amount, now in a simple form. The qualitative Something emerging through their identity has a quantum; this is not yet the qualitative determination of quantitative ratio, still less the unity of quality and quantity in Measure. Nor should the ordinary fraction, the power relation, and the differential be flattened into one finished result. Hegel distinguishes their degrees of adequacy and the further work still required.

**The third is that all mathematical infinity is the spurious progress.** The criticism targets the unresolved demand for a beyond reproduced by each step. It does not establish a verdict against completed infinite sets, exact infinite sums, or mathematical limits. Cantor's transfinite theory is later than Hegel and does not define an infinite set as a successive construction waiting for its last member. Whether a particular mathematical infinite warrants comparison with Hegel's true infinite is a further argument, not something decided by its name. The narrower result remains: repeating the finite does not, merely by repetition, resolve its opposed beyond.

## IX. What the Method Did Here — Observations

The first observation is that **the qualitative return has a determinate mediation**. The quantum's limit is indifferent; its determination refers beyond itself; that beyond proves itself quantitative; externality is thus posited as a moment of the quantum's own determination. The result is ratio. Neither a familiar pattern of double negation nor a mathematical example can stand in for these steps. Their result preserves quantity in a changed role, rather than exchanging calculation for an ineffable quality.

The same point limits a reduction of every opposition to degree. Hegel argues that treating opposed determinations merely as quanta leaves them external to one another rather than deriving their connection (*SL* §517). The objection is to that sufficiency claim: assigning numbers does not by itself explain the relation of what is numbered. It does not follow that quantitative relations are incapable of contributing to such explanation. The mathematical Remarks themselves investigate that contribution.

The second observation is that **comprehension must preserve determinate content through its transformation**. Hegel calls the purification of a quantitative ratio “**analogous to grasping an empirical reality in terms of its Notion**”: its characteristic features are grasped “**in their essentiality**” and taken into the Concept's unity (*SL* §580). The analogy concerns the change from indifferent coexistence to an internally determined relation. It is not a promise to preserve every empirical detail without selection, nor a deduction of a science's particular law. Indeed, the distinction between algebraic derivation and proof of application shows why identifying the relevant determinations remains work to be done.

The third observation is that **method includes judging the reach of its own forms**. Hegel's arithmetic ordering expressly stops short of immanent development; his account of calculus credits mathematical advances while criticizing their explanations; his own induction over applications remains bounded. Applying his requirement that a position be met on its own ground (§27), we have examined his own replies to Kant and to the theory of limits, distinguishing their premises from what the opposed arguments establish. The retained achievement is neither a timeless mathematical foundation nor a merely decorative analogy: it is the demand to exhibit how a result depends on its determinations and to prove the passage from a formal operation to the object it claims to comprehend.

## X. What Comes Next

The quantum has yielded **quantitative ratio** (*das quantitative Verhältnis*), but ratio's own forms and limitations remain. The next installment develops:

- The **direct ratio**, whose **exponent** is a determinate quotient while its sides can vary.
- The **inverse ratio** and the **ratio of powers**, through which the relation's quantitative and qualitative determinations are further developed.
- The passage into **Measure** (*Maß*), the unity of quality and quantity, whose own development — not a completed result here — eventually leads toward Essence.

The question of physical quantization deferred in §15 also remains a question. Discrete energy eigenvalues and the nodal transformation of a quality under quantitative variation are not interchangeable descriptions. Connecting them requires specifying the physical system, the relevant quality and quantity, and the actual relation between their changes. Neither the existence of Planck's constant nor the refusal to deduce its numerical value supplies that mediation. The comparison with Measure is therefore a candidate for concrete investigation, not an application already established by the quantum's logical derivation.

## Coda

The quantum seemed to be simply *this much*. Its development has shown that this determinateness cannot remain an indifferent boundary standing by itself: it refers beyond itself, and that beyond is itself quantitative. The true result is not another magnitude farther away, but a unity in which related quanta have their determination. **Quality returns without quantity disappearing.** Hegel's reading of the calculus makes this relational achievement vivid, but also brings the method under its own demand for proof: the logical interpretation, the mathematical operation, and its application are connected questions, not interchangeable answers. *The movement toward Measure begins where the how-much has disclosed a relation constitutive of what it is; whether a particular science exhibits that form must be established in the science, not decided by the resemblance of its vocabulary.*
