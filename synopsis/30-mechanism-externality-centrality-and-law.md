# Mechanism: Externality, Centrality, and Law

*The twenty-first installment of Section III — the fourth in the **Doctrine of the Concept**, and the first in **Objectivity** (*Objektivität*). §29 ended with a mediation that no longer stood between independently given extremes: its result was an immediate, self-standing whole. Yet **immediate objectivity is not the Idea**. The Concept's unity is present in the objects without yet being freely explicit as their own differentiating principle. **Mechanism** (*Mechanismus*) begins from that discrepancy: each object is a totality, while the determinations connecting it with other objects appear external to what it is. The **mechanical process** makes this external determination explicit — first through communication, distribution, and reaction, then through the unequal capacity of objects to sustain what is communicated. Its result is not merely another collision but **centrality** (*Zentralität*) and **law**: self-subsistence mediated through relation, and a universality that maintains itself in its particularization. **Absolute mechanism** articulates this achievement as a system in which absolute centre, relative centre, and non-self-subsistent objects each mediate the other two, not as a privileged body acting upon passive satellites. But the law's differentiation is still not the objects' own specific opposition; their apparent indifference survives within the relation that sustains them. The demand that their relation belong to their determinate nature opens **chemism**. Along the way, mechanism is distinguished from mathematics as such, a bounded model from a claim to exhaustive explanation, and Hegel's logical argument from his disputable physical applications. The Logic supplies the categorial form of this development; it deduces no force law, planetary system, or empirical boundary of a model.*

## I. Why Objectivity Begins with Externality

The last installment ended with an apparent reversal. The syllogism had overcome the separation of its mediating unity from its terms; why should the result now be a world of objects external to one another?

Because the result is **immediate**. The mediation has not been forgotten, but it has withdrawn into a unity whose differences are no longer separately exhibited as the steps of its production. Each differentiated moment has the standing of the whole: it is itself an object, not an incomplete term waiting for a middle outside it. But precisely because the unity has this immediate form, the relation of these totalities does not yet appear as their own self-differentiation. It is either an **inner unity**, not developed in their outward relation, or an **outer unity**, arranging objects that seem complete without that arrangement (*SL* §§1538–1544).

This is **mechanism**, and its first definition is not a list of forces. It is a relation in which what the objects are seems indifferent to how they are combined. A mixture, an arrangement, a succession can alter while the related objects remain what they are. The relation certainly occurs; what is lacking is its determination as constitutive of their nature.

The distinction matters for everything that follows. Hegel is not beginning with an empirical inventory of solid bodies and then projecting that inventory into logic. The logical **object** (*Objekt*) is the Concept's totality in the form of immediacy. Physical bodies can exemplify this form, but so can elements of thought held in external association. Nor is objectivity simply whatever stands over against a human subject. Hegel explicitly distinguishes that use of the word from its stronger sense: what has a standing not dependent on subjective caprice (*SL* §§1536–1537).

The argument continues the Concept's **development**, not Being's passing-over. Nevertheless, that development must exhibit its own initially inadequate objective forms. To have reached the sphere of freedom is not to have already made every relation free; here the Concept must develop the objective shape in which its self-determination can be explicit. The finished Idea cannot be brought in as a guarantee that this work has already been done.

The primary sequence followed below is *SL* §§1543–1577: **the mechanical object**, **the mechanical process**, **absolute mechanism**. The *Encyclopaedia* compresses the same field into formal, differentiated, and absolute mechanism (*Enc.* §§195–199); these are not three headings that map one-to-one onto the larger work's divisions. English quotations follow the online Miller and Wallace transcriptions, not a printed edition checked for this installment. Our prose retains **Concept** and **singular**, where Miller commonly has *Notion* and *individual*.

## II. The Mechanical Object — a Totality Indifferent to Its Determination

The object is not a return to §23's **Thing**, nor to §26's substance under another name. Hegel distinguishes the successive immediacies explicitly: determinate being, existence, actuality and substantiality, and now objectivity have different derivations (*SL* §1534). In the Thing, properties and their bearer could be held apart; in substance, accidents were distinguished from substantial power. The object contains its particularity within its totality. Its differences are themselves totalities, rather than parts defined only through their incompleteness apart from a whole (*SL* §§1544–1545).

But **totality is not yet self-determination**. At first the object's manifold determinateness is gathered into it without an immanent principle explaining why these determinations belong together in this way. Its form of unity remains compatible with a different mixture or arrangement. This is the precise sense in which it is an **aggregate**: not that we have discovered ultimate particles, but that the object's own unity does not yet determine the connection of its constituents. Hegel expressly refuses to identify these objects with atoms, which lack the totality required here (*SL* §§1546–1547).

The same indifference appears between objects. To explain why one has this determination, we point to another; but that other is equally indifferent to the determination it supplies. The explanation can be extended indefinitely without reaching a principle that determines itself. Hegel calls this standpoint **determinism** (*SL* §§1548–1549). His target is the explanatory regress of mutually external determinants, not the modern mathematical definition of a dynamics in which a specified state uniquely fixes its evolution. The two uses of the word must not silently exchange their conclusions.

The objection is therefore stronger, and more restricted, than *there is always another cause*. The determination referred elsewhere is the same determination that was to be understood here. If its bearer is everywhere indifferent to it, relocating it supplies no reason for its determinate character. At this level, explanation repeats its content under a different address. This is not a denial that identifying a source of motion or heat can answer a definite scientific question. It is a criticism of making such relocation the sufficient ground of the determination itself.

The contradiction now has both sides in view. The objects are supposed to be **completely external and independent**, yet the determination through which they are related is **identical** in them. Their externality excludes the unity that their determining relation requires. Conversely, that unity does not simply dissolve the objects; they maintain their independence within it. The unity of these opposed determinations is not a peaceful description but the **mechanical process** (*SL* §1550).

This also answers a question left by §26. There Hegel had already used *mechanism* for the externality of finite causality. Here he expressly explains why the category is not a simple relapse into that earlier relation. An active object is already mediated in its activity: being a cause is not its original, exclusive standing, since it is also an effect. What causality learned is retained. But the objects remain indifferent both to their causal roles and to their aggregation into a larger object (*SL* §§1551–1552). Mechanism is now developed on the achieved ground of the Concept.

## III. The Formal Process — Communication, Reaction, Product

The first movement is **communication**. One determinateness continues into another object and thereby acquires the form of a universal. It is not consumed by becoming its opposite. Hegel's most immediately intelligible example is spiritual communication: a content can be shared without ceasing to be present in the one who communicates it. In the material sphere he names motion, heat, magnetism, and electricity (*SL* §§1553–1554). These are his historical illustrations of the categorial form, not physical laws derived by the Logic; in particular, communicating motion is not a claim that each body receives an undiminished copy of a conserved quantity.

The continuity is possible because the first object's particular possession of the determination is not its impenetrable essence. The determination can be present elsewhere. Yet if communication were the whole process, the objects' distinctness would have disappeared. Their self-subsistence must therefore also be manifested.

That manifestation is **reaction**, but reaction does not initially mean simply cancelling what was received. The communicated universal is particularized in the different objects: each has its specific share. What first appeared as the exclusive determination of one object now appears as a particular instance of a determination that extends through several. **Communication and distribution are the same process read from its two sides** — the singular determination is universalized, and that universal is particularized (*SL* §1555).

Reaction then has its negative moment. Each object asserts itself against its being determined by another; the transient action subsides, and the objects return to an indifferent relation-to-self. Hegel speaks of the equality of action and reaction and of the process coming to rest (*SL* §1556). The categorial sequence concerns the restoration of self-subsistence after external determination; it is not a proof of Newton's third law, nor a prediction that every interacting physical system must eventually stop moving.

The result is the **product**. In one respect it is the same as the starting object: a totality whose determination is external to its self-relation. In another respect it is decisively different. What was merely presupposed is now **posited**: the object's determination has been exhibited as the result of another's mediation. Hegel's strong formulation is that “only as a product” is the mechanical object an object (*SL* §1558).

The difference between *same* and *new* must not be erased. The product repeats the **logical form** of the initial object; it need not repeat its initial empirical state. A new mixture or arrangement can result, and from the standpoint of the starting object that result is contingent. The process has no end already governing it as an end: Hegel expressly distinguishes this from teleology (*SL* §§1557–1559).

This is the first gain. External determination is no longer only something we say about the object. The process displays it, and displays the object's return to itself through it. Yet a process that merely leaves one indifferent object beside another has not explained the determinate relation between their different capacities to act and resist.

## IV. The Real Process — Unequal Independence, Power, and the Centre

The formal process treats its objects as mutually external but equivalent in standing. Its result makes a more determinate opposition available. An object can be dependent in its relation and yet maintain an independence against the others; these moments can now be differently distributed among the objects themselves. Their difference is no longer mere numerical diversity. Hegel speaks of differences of mass or intensity as possible representations, but warns against fixing the logical difference in that abstraction (*SL* §§1560–1561).

The **real mechanical process** therefore asks how differently constituted objects sustain a communicated determination. A stronger object does not simply overpower anything whatsoever. Communication requires a common sphere: without one there is no process of this kind, just as an infinite judgment fails to relate subject and predicate within a determinate field. Resistance itself presupposes participation in what is being resisted (*SL* §1562; compare §28's infinite judgment).

This is an important restriction on a tempting picture of domination. The relation is not intelligible merely by labelling one side strong and the other weak. One must exhibit what they share, how it is communicated, and how the recipient particularizes it. Hegel's physical and psychological illustrations here are not experimental demonstrations; the logical point does not require adopting their empirical judgments.

**Violence** (*Gewalt*) enters when the object cannot sustain the received universal in its own singular unity. The communicated determination is not integrated as the object's own self-relation; instead, its existing unity is disrupted. Hegel distinguishes **power** from its violent form on precisely this ground (*SL* §1563). The distinction recalls §26's power made external, but now its measure is explicitly the object's capacity to be itself within a universal. It also sharpens the contrast with §27's free universal, which maintains itself in its particularization without doing violence to it.

The ensuing discussion of **fate** is a deliberately wider application. Universality appears as blind fate where the subject confronts its own universal nature as an alien power. Hegel reserves fate in the proper sense for self-consciousness. For fate to have power over it, self-consciousness “must have committed a deed”: it must have given itself a determination conflicting with its essential universality. The connection with blame is explicit: “The nation without deeds is without blame” (*SL* §1564). The deed makes the subject particular against its universal essence and thereby exposes it to that essence as an alien power. Hegel distinguishes this proper fate from the contingency that befalls merely animate beings. Our restriction is that this account does not by itself establish an actual person's deed, its estrangement from the universal, or that person's responsibility. It therefore supplies no entitlement to infer from someone's suffering alone that the suffering was deserved.

What does this unequal process produce? Not simply the victory of an object labelled strongest. Mere resistance, understood as exclusion of all determination by another, has proved inadequate to the object's actual mediation. The positive result must be a self-relation **through** that mediation: a unity that maintains itself in its determinations rather than only shutting them out. Hegel calls this objective singular unity the **centre**. Its universal side is a determination persisting through changing particular relations — **law** (*SL* §§1565–1566).

The *Encyclopaedia* states the inference compactly. The object is independent only by closing with itself through its dependence; its independence therefore becomes a negative unity that includes relation to the external. This is **centrality** (*Zentralität*). But the other object is central too, and each thus has its centrality also in the other (*Enc.* §196). A centre is not an isolated point dignified with a new name. It is the beginning of self-subsistence sustained through an objective relation.

Wallace calls this stage **“Mechanism with Affinity”**; the German is *differenter Mechanismus*, **differentiated mechanism**. His wording must not make us import the fully developed specific affinity of chemism into this intermediate stage. The relation of centres has still to be articulated, and its remaining externality has still to be overcome.

## V. Absolute Mechanism — the System of Centres

In **absolute mechanism**, the common unity is no longer only transmitted from one object to another. It is their **objective universality**, immanent in the relation through which they maintain themselves. The centre gives this unity an objective singular form. Yet an absolute centre standing alone over against an undifferentiated collection would leave mediation incomplete: it would still be a middle without adequately determined extremes (*SL* §§1567–1570).

The objects must therefore also acquire centrality. A **relative centre** is self-related with respect to the objects dependent on it, while itself related to the more comprehensive centre. The relation has three terms: **absolute centre**, **relative centre**, **non-self-subsistent objects**. Absolute does not here mean the Absolute Idea, nor relative an arbitrary choice of observer. These are the different functions within this objective system.

The three functions form not one syllogism but a **system of syllogisms** (*SL* §§1570–1572; *Enc.* §§197–198):

1. The **relative centre** mediates between the comprehensive centre and the objects belonging to its particular sphere. It joins participation in the whole with a determinate local unity.
2. The **non-self-subsistent objects** mediate between the two centres. They constitute the relative centre's external existence, through which it both differs from and remains connected with the comprehensive centre.
3. The **absolute centre** mediates between the relative centre and those objects. It is the common unity through which their internal cohesion and external relation belong to one system.

The order of presentation differs between the two works; the point common to them is that **each term serves as middle**, not that one privileged middle does everything. Nor are the three mediations equally adequate when isolated. The *Science of Logic* calls the mediation through externally standing objects formal; its truth lies in the whole, not in its being elevated into a self-sufficient explanation (*SL* §1571).

This resumes §29's demand on a middle in objective form. To say that everything depends on everything else would only rename the problem. The three mediations have different work to do: sustaining a particular unity, giving it an external existence, and connecting its inner and outer sides through the universal. Hegel calls the completed articulation **free mechanism** (*SL* §1572). The freedom is the system's immanent mediation, not conscious choice by its objects and not yet the freedom of an end explicitly determining its own realization.

Hegel illustrates the structure with the solar system and, in the practical sphere, with the state. The latter example makes the mediation easier to see without identifying its terms with bodies. Particular needs connect persons with social institutions; the activity of persons realizes those institutions and satisfies needs; the institutions provide the enduring universal conditions through which persons and their satisfaction have actuality (*Enc.* §198). These are claims about distinct mediating functions, not a proof that any existing government adequately embodies them. Nor does the astronomical illustration derive a system of orbits or make government a kind of gravity. The shared claim is categorial; the empirical and political adequacy of its applications requires its own inquiry.

## VI. Law — Not Merely Regularity

The passage from **order** to **law** is the positive achievement of absolute mechanism. Order arranges objects whose nature seems indifferent to that arrangement. Law expresses a differentiation belonging to the objective unity itself. It is not an instruction issued by a separate legislator; it is the universal's own determinate articulation within the system (*SL* §§1572–1575).

This is why Hegel distinguishes a **rule** from a **law** here. A process can repeat uniformly while its determinate content remains externally given. Uniformity alone exhibits a rule; it does not yet exhibit the system's own principle of differentiation and movement. His contrast is emphatic: “Only free mechanism has a law” (*SL* §1575).

The distinction is local and technical. It is not a terminological decree that scientists must stop calling experimentally established regularities laws until they have completed Hegel's Logic. Nor does it retract the **law of appearance** developed in §24. There the stable content of appearance had yet to account for its own negative form and the necessity of its related sides. Here law is developed on the ground of the Concept as the immanent differentiation of an objective totality. The later determination preserves the earlier problem while specifying what its solution must contain.

The methodological demand can be stated without pretending to have solved it for any actual science. An invariant relation is not comprehended solely by noting that it recurs. One must show how the differences it relates belong to one determining unity, and how that unity maintains itself in their movement. That is the work Hegel attributes to law in free mechanism. He does not deduce a numerical law of gravitation in these paragraphs, and applying the word *law* to a proposed account does not establish that the account has performed this work.

The result is still **mechanism**. The law is immanent, but its differentiation remains inward or ideal in contrast with the objects' external existence. This is not *ideal* in the sense of imaginary. It is a real unity whose determinate differences have not yet been made the objects' own specific natures. The system has gained a principle of self-movement, but the objects still seem to be independently what they are before entering its relation.

## VII. Why Mechanism Develops into Chemism

The last inadequacy is therefore not that mechanism has no law, no internal relations, or no self-movement. Those would be objections to its initial form, made after its development had answered them. Its limit must be stated **on the ground it has reached**.

The objects have just been shown to possess their essential self-subsistence only through centrality and its law. Yet they retain the appearance of an indeterminate independence outside that relation. What sustains them is thus treated as indifferent to what they are. Hegel's conclusion is that the law's difference must belong to the objects' own determinate existence: not merely a unity in which they participate, but a distinction through which each is specifically related to its other (*SL* §1576; *Enc.* §199).

Two consequences follow together. The centre ceases to stand simply as the common destination of otherwise indifferent objects; its negative unity is articulated into **objectively opposed determinations**. And each object's relation becomes a relation to a **specifically opposed object**, rather than a striving towards a centre as such. Their unity now has the form of a tension between differentiated totalities (*SL* §1577).

This is **chemism**. The distinction is stated at the next chapter's opening: for the mechanical object, determinateness is indifferent to its totality; for the chemical object, its determinateness and its specific relation to another belong to its nature (*SL* §1579). The contrast is not between objects that have relations and objects that do not. Both do. It is between a relation still compatible with the appearance of their independently complete natures and a relation explicitly constitutive of their specific character.

The inference has a definite warrant and a definite scope. Within Hegel's development, an independence sustained only by relation cannot remain **indifference to that relation**; the object's own determination must express the mediation already shown to sustain it. This is not the claim that relation abolishes independence. It specifies a different independence, one that includes rather than excludes its constitutive relation. Nor does the inference prove that an arbitrary collection of physical bodies must undergo a chemical reaction. No reagent, bond, temperature, or reaction pathway is supplied by it.

Our reconstruction makes this demand intelligible; it does not independently prove that Hegel's ensuing account exhausts every possible form of relation-bearing objectivity. The strength of the move lies in requiring the object to acknowledge in its own nature the mediation on which it already depends. Its further development must show what that new determination can accomplish and where it, too, is insufficient.

## VIII. Mechanism, Mathematics, and Scientific Explanation

**Mechanism is not mathematics as such.** The category concerns the standing of objects and their relations, not whether the account uses equations. A verbal theory can be mechanical; a mathematical theory can make the relation constitutive of the objects it studies. Whether a particular theory does so is a question about its content and explanatory claims, not a conclusion from the presence or absence of symbols. Neither solving a differential equation nor fitting a curve decides the categorial question by itself.

The distinction is especially important after §29. A regression's fitted relation, its warranted use in prediction, and an explanation of why it holds are different but connected achievements. A fit can support bounded prediction without identifying the relation's ground; a theory can explain which differences should matter and suggest where the fit will fail. Conversely, apparent agreement can test or correct the theory. Neither direction is automatic, and statistical models have assumptions of their own. This is our methodological application, not a statistical doctrine contained in Hegel's chapter.

The present development adds a sharper question: **are the relata taken as already constituted before their relation, or does explaining them require that relation to enter their definition?** Even a successful prediction can leave that question unanswered. But an unanswered question about constitution does not make the prediction false. The achievement must be retained at the scope at which it is warranted.

**A restricted model is not refuted by being restricted.** Our criterion here is explicit: a model earns a bounded explanatory or predictive claim through justified assumptions and appropriate evidence, including an account of relevant error. That is not yet Hegel's criterion for the complete comprehension of objectivity. The connection is a task, not a partition into two sealed enterprises: examining why an approximation works can itself expose how the objects and relations must be understood.

A local approximation can be derived from a more comprehensive model; different regimes can have controlled overlaps. Equally, a theory's stated premises do not by themselves disclose every limit of its empirical application. The actual conditions and the consequences of neglected features still require investigation. These are distinctions within scientific warrant, not defects logic can declare without doing that work.

There is unusually direct support here for **preserving mechanism**, but it accompanies substantive restrictions. In *Enc.* §195's Addition, Hegel restricts mechanical law in nature to the most abstract relations of inert matter and denies that light, heat, magnetism, or electricity can be explained by mere mechanical processes such as pressure and impact. His rejection of mechanism as an absolute explanatory standpoint thus includes judgments about the boundaries of particular sciences. Yet the same Addition insists on “the right and import of a general logical category”. Its role is not confined to mechanics. Mechanical memory and acquired operations have a legitimate place within intelligent activity; external association need not disappear whenever understanding is present. What is rejected in that example is making the subordinate operation the sufficient account of intelligence itself.

This is not a lenient concession added to an otherwise destructive method. The higher account must include what the lower form actually does. At the same time, preservation is not a blanket vindication of every theory once called mechanical. Nor does the categorial argument by itself establish the boundaries Hegel assigns to mechanical explanation in particular sciences; their validity requires assessment of those sciences' explanatory content.

For a bounded test, consider a further physical judgment inside the *Science of Logic* chapter. In discussing centrality, Hegel calls indefinite rectilinear motion in the absence of external resistance “an empty abstraction”, and interprets friction and resistance through union with a centre (*SL* §1568). The passage must not be silently converted into the harmless observation that actual bodies are related to other bodies.

**On Newtonian mechanics' own ground**, the first law is a conditional statement about motion in an inertial frame under zero resultant force; it is not the metaphysical assertion that an actual body is absolutely unrelated to the universe. It supplies the baseline of constant velocity against which acceleration under a resultant force is distinguished. Recognizing forces, including friction, does not make that conditional scientifically empty. The logical argument for mediated self-subsistence therefore does not by itself establish such emptiness or the physical explanation of friction proposed in this paragraph. The standard of this objection is the content of the physical claim, not a demand that logic supply an experiment. The law's formulation and its distinction from friction are set out, for example, in [*University Physics*, §5.2](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/5-2-newtons-first-law).

**On Hegel's ground**, the further question is whether the abstraction can be comprehended as a subordinate determination of the related whole rather than made its complete truth. The chapter's own development, together with its explicit retention of mechanism in the *Encyclopaedia*, supplies the basis for asking that question. Our conclusion is neither that a useful law defeats the Concept nor that the Concept defeats a conditional law. The categorial insufficiency of absolute isolation and the scientific warrant of a restricted abstraction must be adjudicated at their actual scopes. This particular application has not shown that the latter is lost when the former is overcome.

## IX. Three Misreadings, Answered Directly

**“Mechanism means classical mechanics, and dialectic replaces equations with something less exact.”** No. Hegel's initial category concerns externality, and its own development reaches immanent law. Equations neither establish nor exclude that immanence. The question is what an account explains about its objects, not whether calculation is permitted.

**“A centre is a supreme object that acts while everything else merely receives.”** No. That would leave the mediating structure incomplete. Absolute mechanism requires relative centres and externally existing objects, with each term mediating the other two. Even this articulated system remains inadequate, but its inadequacy cannot be diagnosed by reducing it to the one-way relation it has developed beyond.

**“Since mechanism develops into chemism, mechanical inquiry is a mistake to be discarded.”** No. The result transforms the claim to self-sufficient explanation; it does not abolish the work of a subordinate category. Hegel expressly defends that work. Equally, retention does not preserve false conclusions as true: it is the achieved determination and its warranted use, not every assertion made under its name, that must survive.

## X. What the Method Did Here — Observations

**It made achieved mediation answer for its immediate form.** The beginning was not an unexplained collection. It was the syllogism's result, whose mediation had become immediate unity. Mechanism exposed what that result still lacked: its unity was not yet explicit as the objects' own determinate relation.

**It distinguished self-enclosure from independence.** An object indifferent to all its determinations has not thereby become free. Its indifference is precisely what leaves its determinate character to others. The positive result is not the abolition of self-relation but self-relation through mediation — centrality.

**It developed the universal in the process itself.** Communication does not merely transfer an unchanged private possession; the same process makes a singular determination universal and particularizes it among the objects. The universal, particular, and singular are not labels attached after the event. They specify what the event must exhibit and what its result retains.

**It refused to make repetition into grounding.** An endless sequence of determinants cannot supply self-determination merely by being longer. A repeated uniformity cannot become immanent law solely by recurring more often. In both cases, the question concerns the form of the connection, not a deficit to be remedied by indefinite accumulation.

**It criticized the developed form, not a defeated beginning.** Absolute mechanism has already overcome sheer aggregation. Chemism is demanded because the immanent law still leaves the objects' specific natures apparently indifferent to their sustaining relation, not because the system somehow lacks every inner connection.

**It gave preservation a determinate content.** External association, mechanical process, and mathematical treatment are not one thing. What a subsequent account retains must be specified, rather than invoked through a general assurance that nothing is lost. The Addition to *Enc.* §195 positively vindicates mechanism's subordinate work; our criticism of the *Science of Logic*'s physical application must be equally exact about the conditional achievement it defends.

## XI. What Comes Next

**§31 — Chemism.** The object whose specific nature is its relation to another; the tension between its one-sided existence and the totality implicit in that relation; the process that produces a neutral unity, and the question whether that unity contains the principle of its own renewed differentiation.

**Beyond.** Teleology will ask how the Concept is explicitly related to objectivity as an end. That relation has not been reached merely by naming a centre, discovering a regularity, or producing a compound. The Idea remains the further development in which the unity of Concept and objectivity must be made explicit.

## Coda

The first object seemed able to stand on its own because its relations did not concern what it was. The process showed the reverse: its determinate being came through those relations, and what looked like independence was sustained by what had been held outside it.

But the result is not a world in which nothing stands. **The centre is an achieved self-relation; law is an achieved immanent unity.** The defect lies in holding that achievement alongside an unchanged picture of the objects whose unity it is. Their relation has become essential, while their specific natures are still treated as though they could remain indifferent to it.

Mechanism therefore reaches beyond itself through what it has succeeded in explaining. It has made independence mediated. The next task is to let that mediation determine what the related objects themselves are.
