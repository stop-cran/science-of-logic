# Actuality: The Absolute, Possibility, Contingency, and Necessity

*The sixteenth installment of Section III — the sixth in the **Doctrine of Essence**, opening its **third and final section** with its **first two chapters**, **The Absolute** and **Actuality** (*Wirklichkeit*), after §24 developed the **mediated unity** of **inner** and **outer**. Actuality is not a grander word for whatever happens to exist: it is essence manifesting itself, distinguished from **Being**, **determinate being**, **reality**, and **Existence**, although its first, **formal** shape again has the poverty of immediate existence. The section begins with **the Absolute**: its **negative exposition** retains the preceding movement of Being and Essence, its **positive exposition** displays the finite as its expression, but neither is adequate while reflection supplies the starting determination from outside. Merely identical absoluteness itself proves **determinate** — an **attribute** (*Attribut*); its unessential form becomes **mode** (*Modus*), and externality **posited as externality** becomes the absolute's own reflective movement, not a foreign loss. This is the mediation into actuality. Hegel's subsequent reckoning with **Spinoza** and **Leibniz** must be assessed separately. Two objections concern the modal intellect's **posteriority** to the attribute and the **otherness** he finds in every determinate attribute. Our reply defends an objective, affirmative reading of Spinoza's attributes without treating intellect's modal status as a sufficient answer or confusing Hegel's diagnosis of **defects** with his intended substance-to-Concept **refutation**. Leibniz supplies internal activity and reasons for harmony, although these do not satisfy Hegel's demand for the absolute's **self-exposition**. The provenance of ***omnis determinatio est negatio*** and the affirmative-attribute objection distinguish Spinoza's doctrine from Hegel's **productive negativity**, rather than certifying the latter as an implication Spinoza overlooked. Modality then develops through **formal**, **real**, and **absolute** necessity: formal possibility and actuality pass into **contingency** (*Zufälligkeit*), their return to themselves in one another yields **formal necessity**, and that unity determines itself as content. **Real possibility** (*reale Möglichkeit*) is the **totality of conditions**, recollecting §22 in a changed form; its completion is **real necessity**, still **relative** because its conditions are contingent. **Absolute necessity** (*absolute Notwendigkeit*) makes presupposing a moment of its own movement, yet remains **blind** because its actualities do not manifest their unity in one another. This is an objective and, in the *Encyclopedia*, explicitly theological claim, not merely a lack of our knowledge. The result is **substance**, not yet the completed passage to **freedom**. Modern modal logic retains its inferential achievements; the distinction between categorial derivation and scientific application supplies no immunity from criticism. Three misreadings answered: actuality as automatic approval of the status quo, necessity as simple abolition of contingency, and the absolute as an inert substrate — without turning their correction into automatic acquittal of Hegel's arguments.*

---

## I. Where We Are: What the Word *Actual* Carries

§24 ended by developing the unity of **inner** and **outer**. The formula *what is merely inner is thereby merely outer* first expressed their immediate conversion as opposed forms, not yet the full identity of form and content. Their further mediation showed that the content is not an indifferent substrate behind those forms, and that each form is the totality only through its other. The result is **Actuality** (*Wirklichkeit*), the unity of essence and Existence, with which the Doctrine of Essence enters its third and final section. Hegel recapitulates the transition: "**Actuality is the unity of essence and Existence**" — in it "formless essence and unstable Appearance … have their truth" — the essential relation's consummation being "**the relation of inner and outer in which the content of both is only one identical substrate and equally only one identity of form**", whereby the difference of form is sublated and "**it is posited that they are one absolute totality**" (*SL* §1158); and then, in one clause, "**This unity of inner and outer is absolute actuality**" (*SL* §1159).

The word must not be heard as simply *real rather than imagined*. The kinship of *Wirklichkeit* with ***wirken*** — to work, act, be efficacious — and *Werk*, a work, gives a useful reminder, not an etymological proof of the category. Hegel's own determination is **manifestation**: the actual is not an inward essence to which an outward appearance happens to be added. "**The actual is therefore manifestation**"; it "**manifests itself**", and "**in its externality it is itself and is itself in that alone**", as a "**self-distinguishing and self-determining movement**" (*SL* §1188). Efficacy matters here, but mere persistence or success is not by itself proof of rational actuality.

The earlier being-words are consequently retained and distinguished. **Being** (§10) was sheer immediacy; **determinate being** (*Dasein*, §11–§13) was being with a quality; **reality** (*Realität*) was the affirmative side of that determinacy. **Existence** (*Existenz*, §22–§23) was the mediated immediacy that has stepped forth from ground, carrying its mediation as sublated. **Actuality** is the **posited unity** of reflection and immediacy: what exists as the manifestation of what it essentially is. Hegel explicitly says "**Being is not yet actual**" and "**Actuality also stands higher than Existence**", which is "**not yet the posited unity of reflection and immediacy**" (*SL* §1188). This is an order of categorial development, not yet a classification of concrete things into worthy and unworthy existences. Within actuality itself, **formal actuality** will be no more than immediate being or existence (*SL* §1192). The **emphatic** use of actuality in the famous political formula therefore needs its own clarification (§VIII), not a shortcut from the word's derivation.

The greater *Logic* opens this section with **The Absolute**, then develops **Actuality** and **The Absolute Relation**. The *Encyclopedia* begins directly with actuality and places its principal Spinoza discussion under substance. The greater *Logic* makes explicit a difficulty in the result just reached: the unity of essence and Existence, first held simply as unity, appears to absorb its distinctions without exhibiting its own articulation. Hence the absolute must become its **own exposition** (*Auslegung*), not the material of an exposition performed upon it. Hegel announces the sequence: first "**the absolute as such**" (*SL* §1159); then "**Actuality, possibility and necessity constitute the formal moments of the absolute, or its reflection**" (*SL* §1160); finally "**the unity of the absolute and its reflection is the absolute relation**, or rather the absolute as relation to itself — **substance**" (*SL* §1161). The historical systems illuminate this problem, but their refutation is a further claim, not something the logical sequence establishes merely by resembling them.

## II. The Absolute and the Exposition That Must Become Its Own

The absolute gathers the preceding movement of Being and Essence into a unity in which none of its determinations retains independent standing. It is "**not merely being, nor even essence**", but "**the absolute unity of both**" (*SL* §1163). Taken first as simple identity, it appears both as the **negation of all predicates** and as their **position**: nothing determinate stands outside it, yet no determination seems adequate to it. When reflection merely alternates these answers, finding a predicate and then cancelling it, Hegel calls the procedure a "**formal and unsystematic dialectic**" (*SL* §1162). Its deficiency is not that it mentions negation, but that the unity of negating and positing has not been exhibited.

The **negative exposition** must therefore be read with its positive achievement intact. Essence, Existence, the world-in-itself, whole, parts, and force have proved unable to stand as independently ultimate determinations. But Hegel explicitly identifies this exposition "**in its true presentation**" with "**the preceding whole of the logical movement of the sphere of being and essence**": its content has not been collected from outside and submerged by an alien reflection, but "**has determined itself internally through its inner necessity**" (*SL* §1165). The result preserves a determinate history. It cannot be reduced to an arbitrary *via negativa* that purchases unity by throwing content away.

The danger of **abstract identity** is nevertheless real. In the *Phenomenology*'s Preface, addressing a contemporary use of the absolute rather than Spinoza, Hegel attacks the procedure that substitutes a monochrome formula for articulated knowledge: "**to give out its Absolute as the night in which, as we say, all cows are black**" is "**the very naïveté of emptiness of knowledge**" (Preface §16, Baillie translation). That is a useful comparison, not the whole argument of this chapter. Nor should Hegel's charge of **acosmism** (*Akosmismus*) be silently softened: the *Encyclopedia* says that Spinoza's system deprives finitude of its due and holds that the world has "**no positive being**" (*Enc.* §151 Addition). Whether this is a fair account of Spinoza's dependent modes is a question for §III; it is not settled by the force of the image.

The **positive exposition** gives finite determinacy a different standing. What disappears as independently self-subsistent is retained as an **expression of the absolute**: it shines through the finite, which has its subsistence in it (*SL* §1166). Yet the finite is still treated as a medium through which we see the absolute; the medium vanishes, and reflection has selected its starting-point from outside. Hegel therefore says that this exposition, however absolute in its result, is "**not so as regards its starting point which is a determination external to the absolute**" (*SL* §1167). Positive exposition has retained manifestation, but has not yet shown why the absolute itself takes this determinate form.

The next step turns that criticism back upon the supposed starting-point. An absolute defined as **merely identical** is already determined **against difference**. It is not the wholly indeterminate beyond that external reflection imagined it had found: its very identity is a form-determination. Moreover, as the unity of inner and outer it already includes the reflection apparently standing outside it. Thus the demand for **self-exposition** arises from the content, not simply from our preference for an active absolute. Hegel writes that "**the exposition of the absolute is, in fact, its own act, which begins from itself and arrives at itself**" (*SL* §1168). This announces what the following mediation must exhibit. §24's conversion of merely inner and merely outer helps identify the defect; it does not allow us to skip attribute and mode or declare the deficient absolute to have achieved nothing.

## III. Attribute and Mode — the Reckoning with Spinoza and Leibniz

Hegel first develops the categories and only then adds his historical Remark. **Attribute** (*Attribut*) is the absolute in a **determinateness**: the whole content, not a detached part, but the whole under a form not yet adequate to that content. The merely identical absolute is itself such a determination; it is "**not the absolute absolute but the absolute in a determinateness, or it is attribute**" (*SL* §1168). The contrast is explicit: "**The attribute is the merely relative absolute**" (*SL* §1170). Its content is the whole absolute, while its distinct form still counts as an unessential shine. Merely submerging this difference restores abstract identity; it does not derive the difference or overcome external reflection (*SL* §§1171–1172).

**Mode** (*Modus*) is this externality developed as externality — the absolute's determinate appearing, initially understood as a loss of itself in alteration. The decisive step is not to deny externality, but to **posit it as externality**. A shine posited as shine has no independent subsistence against the absolute; its vanishing is negative self-relation, a return into that from which it seemed to fall away. Nor has the movement started with wholly foreign material: the initially indifferent absolute itself proved to be an attribute, a determinate form. Hegel's sequence thus turns the apparent loss into the absolute's own reflective movement (*SL* §§1173–1175). "**The true meaning of mode is that it is the absolute's own reflective movement**" (*SL* §1176). Its exposition is its content; manifestation is no longer an inner essence showing itself in something independently other. That is the transition into **actuality** (*SL* §§1177–1178).

Only now should the historical claim be weighed. **Spinoza** defines attribute through what intellect perceives as constituting the essence of substance, and mode through the affections of substance, what is in another and conceived through it (*Ethics* I, defs. 4–5). Hegel's first objection is that intellect, precisely because it is a **mode**, is posterior to attribute; defining attribute through intellect therefore appears to make the prior determination depend upon a posterior one, an other standing immediately over against substance (*SL* §1181). This is not simply a claim that intellect exists outside substance. His second objection is that each attribute is supposed to be conceived through itself, yet, as **determinate**, contains **otherness** and cannot be understood from itself alone (*SL* §1183). He also objects that "**it is not shown by what necessity the infinite plurality reduces itself to opposition**", namely thought and extension, and concludes: "**These two attributes are therefore adopted empirically**" (*SL* §1182). His demand is for an internally developed form, not merely the assertion that all determinations belong to one substance.

Our reply defends an **objective reading** of the attribute-definition: intellect apprehends what constitutes substance's essence, rather than conferring that character upon it. The ground is not merely that **perception is not production**. Spinoza requires agreement between a true idea and its object (axiom 6); the proof of *Ethics* I, prop. 30 invokes that agreement in relating actual intellect, finite or infinite, to God's attributes and modes. Prop. 31 expressly makes intellect a mode of thought, conceived through that attribute. We therefore grant the intellect's posteriority: it is a premise of Hegel's objection, not a concession that defeats him. What we contest is the inference from intellect's role in the **definition** to the attribute's dependence upon it for its nature or conceivability. Prop. 10's proof cites definition 4 whole, including its intellect clause, and then definition 3. On the reading defended here, the inference rests on the attribute's constituting substance's essence and substance's being conceived through itself; the reference to intellect identifies the apprehension of that essence, not a mode on whose prior conception the attribute depends. This answers the alleged dependence, not only an alleged production. It neither derives the definition itself nor settles every interpretation of its reference to intellect. Hegel's demand that the definitions' own necessity be developed remains a further question of adequacy. **The absence of Hegel's derivation and the absence of any derivation are different claims.**

The same distinction matters for the **modes**. Hegel says that "**this mode remains mere mode**", immediately given, and that "**its nullity is not cognised as reflection-into-self**"; substance, attribute, and mode are "**only enumerated one after the other, without any inner sequence of development**" (*SL* §1183). These three are the object of the enumeration charge — not a purported absence of every sequence among individual modes. Spinoza argues for the necessary following of infinitely many things from divine nature, develops immediate and mediated infinite modes, and relates finite determinations through finite causes (*Ethics* I, props. 16, 21–23, 28). Dependent being is not thereby illusion. Hegel's stronger demand is that the coming-forth and return of determinacy be the absolute's **self-negating form**. His language of nullity and his acosmism charge belong to this assessment; we must neither accept them without argument nor improve him into a critic who never made them.

The dispute also bears on ***omnis determinatio est negatio***. **Letter 50** to Jarig Jelles gives the formula *determinatio negatio est* in an argument about **figure**: delimitation pertains to a body's non-being rather than to its affirmative being. The textual history matters. Robert Stern's study, **“‘Determination is Negation’: The Adventures of a Doctrine from Spinoza to Hegel to the British Idealists”** (2016, §2 and n. 7), reports both the edited Dutch text in the *Nagelate Schriften* and the Latin translation in the *Opera Posthuma*; it is not a Latin autograph that we are quoting. Stern's quotation of **Jacobi** has *determinatio est negatio*, without *omnis*, whereas his n. 29 only conjectures a role for Jacobi in adding the universalizing word. That does not settle its origin. The bounded conclusion is enough: the familiar slogan is not the wording of the cited letter. In this chapter Hegel himself writes "**Determinateness is negation**", calling it "**the absolute principle of Spinoza's philosophy**" and "**this true and simple insight**" (*SL* §1179). The admiration is real; the philosophical appropriation still needs examination.

The difference cannot be settled by dismissing a geometrical example as merely geometrical. Spinoza's own *Ethics* I, prop. 8, scholium 1 contrasts finite existence's partial negation with infinite existence's affirmation: there is a general logical content to investigate. Hegel's *Lectures on the History of Philosophy*, in their discussion of Spinoza's mathematical method, concede the compulsion of the proofs and the correctness of the procedure, yet call that method the fundamental defect of the whole position and altogether unsuited for philosophy. The concession does not retract the severity: Hegel demands that definitions and method arise from the content, and faults the resulting propositions for expressing only one side of negation. **Privation** does not by itself yield **productive, self-relating negativity**. Hegel states his criticism expressly: "**Spinoza stops short at negation as determinateness or quality; he does not advance to a cognition of negation as absolute, that is, self-negating, negation**" (*SL* §1179). That advance is Hegel's development, not a doctrine we may install in Spinoza by calling it implicit.

The defence of **affirmative attributes** must also address the second objection. Hegel's contention that determinacy contains otherness is not an unsupported preference: it draws upon his prior development of determinateness and difference (§§11–13, §21). Spinoza himself allows negation here: the explanation of definition 6 says that, of what is infinite only in its kind, "infinite attributes may be denied", whereas whatever expresses essence and "involves no negation" belongs to the essence of the absolutely infinite. We cannot therefore treat every negative contrast as an import from Hegel. Definition 2, however, confines finite limitation to another of the **same nature**: thought does not limit body, nor body thought. The contested step is from denying other attributes to requiring them for an attribute's own conception. Axiom 5 denies that things with nothing in common are understood through one another; prop. 10 and its scholium defend the attributes' independent conceivability without dividing substance or making one attribute produce another. On the reading defended here, excluding other attributes does not supply the positive content by which an attribute is understood. This resists Hegel's inference, not every negation within an attribute. **Macherey**, as quoted and discussed by Stern (§5, n. 28, citing *Hegel or Spinoza*, 2011, p. 129), treats their not limiting one another as "**the condition of their substantial character**"; Stern considers this argument conditionally rather than claiming to have settled the interpretation. That point tells **against** assimilating the attributes' distinction to finite privative limitation. It does not invalidate Hegel's preceding categorial development; it contests whether that result establishes an inconsistency in attributes conceived on Spinoza's terms. **These two allegations remain contested: neither intellect's posteriority nor the attributes' mutual deniability by itself establishes dependence of conception.** The separate demands for the necessity of the thought–extension opposition and for an internally developed sequence of substance, attribute, and mode are not settled by this reply. Hegel himself distinguishes diagnosing defects from accomplishing a refutation (*SL* §1287). Whether the intended substance-to-Concept argument meets Spinoza on his own ground remains the further question of §§26–27.

**Leibniz** enters the same Remark as the contrasting achievement. The **monad** has internal activity and represents the whole universe: "**The monad is only one, a negative reflected into itself; it is the totality of the content of the world**"; "**It is an entelechy, and the manifestation is its own act**" (*SL* §1185). Hegel calls the conception of alterations as the monads' own manifestations "**extremely important**" (*SL* §1186). Leibniz's own account is not a blank where an explanation should be: the *Monadology* derives natural change from an internal principle (§11), develops perception and appetition (§§14–18), makes created monads dependent upon God (§47), explains their ideal influence through divine mediation (§51), and gives reasons of perfection for the choice and harmony of a world (§§53–60).

Hegel nevertheless objects that the monads' limitations and concord are not developed as the absolute's own immanent differentiation: their relation "**falls outside them and is likewise pre-established by another being**" (*SL* §1185). The familiar **windowlessness** (*Monadology* §7) denies influx from other created things, not internal change or all relation to the universe. Hegel's objection therefore concerns the relation between the account of each internally active individual and the account of their unity, not a discovery that Leibniz forgot to offer either account. His verdict is that their distinction and relation to their absolute remain products of dogmatic reflection rather than achieving "**an inner coherence**" (*SL* §1186). We retain the positive explanatory content while asking whether its own requirements establish the alleged defect, rather than treating the difference of criteria as sufficient. The transition to actuality rests on the exposition–attribute–mode argument above, not on declaring two historical systems defeated.

## IV. Formal Actuality, Formal Possibility, and the Contingent

Actuality, first taken, is **immediate**. The unity of inner and outer is there without yet displaying its mediation in a determinate content. Hegel calls it **formal actuality**: "**Actuality is formal in so far as, being primary actuality, it is only immediate, unreflected actuality**"; as such it is "**nothing more than a being or Existence in general**" (*SL* §1192). The distinction won in the preceding chapter is not lost, but its moments still have to be developed.

Reflect this immediate actual into itself and its self-identity, abstracted from its concrete actuality, is **formal possibility** (*Möglichkeit*). "**This possibility is actuality reflected into itself**" (*SL* §1193). Its positive determination is non-self-contradiction: "**In the sense of this formal possibility everything is possible that is not self-contradictory**"; the resulting realm is "**the relationless, indeterminate receptacle for everything whatever**" (*SL* §1195). Its negative determination matters just as much: it is **only a possibility**, a moment insufficient without actuality (*SL* §1194).

Hegel's critique first targets the poverty of the bare predication. "**A is possible means only that A is A**": it has not yet exhibited what makes the content possible (*SL* §1196). The Addition to *Enc.* §143, in Wallace's translation, illustrates this with the moon's possible fall upon the earth, obtained by isolating it from its determinate relations and comparing it with a thrown stone. The defect is not that a description has passed a consistency test, but that this is offered as sufficient warrant for a concrete possibility. **A limited achievement is not yet an adequate account of the fact.**

The second criticism concerns **external reflection**. A given content can be made formally self-identical by abstracting from its determinations. In that procedure possibility is a residue of actuality, not a populated reservoir from which actuality must be selected. Hegel says that the modal moments are initially "**not yet realised**", so that their difference belongs "**at first to external reflection**" (*SL* §1189). This describes the level's limitation, not a general thesis that possibility is always a fiction of a spectator. The further development must exhibit the actual's own modal articulation.

Third, **concrete content** brings the criterion into collision with Hegel's account of determinacy. Its manifold determinations are not merely diverse: he develops their relation into opposition and contradiction, and therefore finds everything as much impossible as possible (*SL* §1195). This depends on the categorial argument examined in §21, not on a rule of formal logic that any two different predicates contradict one another. The formal criterion remains distinguishable from Hegel's criticism of its adequacy as the ultimate account of concrete possibility.

There is also a contradiction in the **modal form itself**. Possibility claims to be the essential inwardness of actuality, yet declares itself merely a moment, deficient without actuality. Moreover, the possibility of *A* leaves the possibility of *not-A* equally standing; the form relates these opposed possibilities while withholding their actuality (*SL* §§1197–1199). Hegel takes the self-sublation of this opposed form to yield immediacy, **actuality** (*SL* §§1200–1201). This is not the inference that an imagined object exists because the thought of it exists. Conversely, immediate actuality, precisely because it is only one formal moment and not the whole mediation, is merely possible. Each determination thus passes into the other without yet grounding a determinate content.

Their unity is **contingency** (*Zufälligkeit*): "**an actual that at the same time is determined as merely possible, whose other or opposite equally is**" (*SL* §1202). Hegel insists upon two sides that must not be reduced to one. As immediate actuality the contingent **has no ground**; as a posited actuality whose other is possible, it **has its ground in another**. Hence "**The contingent, then, has no ground because it is contingent; and, equally, it has a ground because it is contingent**" (*SL* §1204). Neither its actuality nor its dependence can simply be argued away.

The methodological achievement is that **contingency is derived, not deplored**. It is a necessary determination of actuality, not a residue awaiting removal by a more ingenious deduction. Hegel names it "**this absolute unrest of the becoming of these two determinations**" (*SL* §1206). This categorial derivation does not deduce why a particular pebble happens to lie here; nor does it by itself settle whether a physical process admits alternative outcomes under exactly the same complete conditions. Those are different questions, and the latter will meet the stronger claim of real necessity.

The unrest has a further result. Since each determination passes into its other, **in that other it returns to itself**. Hegel calls "**this identity of both, of one in the other**" **necessity** (*SL* §1206). This is a positive advance beyond mere alternation, not a fraudulent proof disguised as a tautology. Its limit is that the moments and their unity are still **formal**: no determinate content has yet been developed through them. The necessary is consequently immediate and groundless as well as mediated through possibility (*SL* §1207); formal necessity does not simply discard contingency. "**The necessity which has resulted is formal because its moments are formal**" (*SL* §1208).

## V. Real Possibility — §22's Conditions, Recollected as Modality

The transition to content must itself be followed, not performed by restoring empirical details that we earlier removed. In formal necessity the two modal determinations have proved one unity, indifferent to their difference. Hegel takes this **identity of form** to determine itself as **content**, whose manifold form initially stands over against it as indifferent (*SL* §1208). The actual is now not merely one formal moment but an actuality with content. This is the local argument into **real actuality**; its adequacy depends on that form–content mediation, not on an unexplained demand that the next category be richer.

**Real actuality** (*reale Wirklichkeit*) is accordingly the thing of many properties, the existent world, an actuality capable of acting upon another (*SL* §1209). Its possibility cannot be assessed by bare self-consistency. "**If one brings into account the determinations, circumstances and conditions of something in order to ascertain its possibility, one is no longer at the stage of formal possibility, but is considering its real possibility**" (*SL* §1211). **Real possibility** (*reale Möglichkeit*) is the actual multiplicity in which what the fact requires is present, initially as scattered circumstances rather than as the accomplished fact.

And with that, §22 returns — a **recollection**, not a repetition. Its culminating sentence, *"when all the conditions of a fact are present, it enters into Existence"*, followed the development through which ground and condition proved moments of one fact, not an external collection whose size finally made it sufficient. Here condition-totality is reached as **real possibility**, and its form is different. Hegel restates the earlier sentence in the element of actuality — "**When all the conditions of something are completely present, it enters into actuality; the completeness of the conditions is the totality as in the content**" — and explicitly marks the difference: "**In the sphere of conditioned ground, the conditions have outside them the form** … Here, on the other hand, immediate actuality is not determined by a presupposing reflection to be condition, but it is posited that **this actuality itself is possibility**" (*SL* §1214). The name is his too: "**real possibility constitutes the totality of conditions**" (*SL* §1213). The recurrence does not identify this relative modal stage with the absolutely unconditioned of §22.

The self-sublation changes **both sides**. The dispersed actualities cease to count as independently self-sufficient and become moments of another actuality; the merely inward possibility ceases to be merely inward and becomes the accomplished fact. Thus the conditions are not an inert heap to which a mysterious modal force must be added. The immediate actuality is itself the possibility of the fact, and its negation as independently standing is the realization of that possibility (*SL* §§1213–1216). The form of the passage belongs to the relation being developed.

The result is **real necessity**: completed real possibility can no longer be otherwise. Hegel states the strong conclusion without reserve: "**under the particular conditions and circumstances something else cannot follow**" (*SL* §1217). We must not replace it with the weaker claim that conditions merely permit an outcome. But neither may an empirically incomplete list be called complete because the desired result follows in our model. Establishing the concrete conditions, their completeness, and the relevant law remains the work of the particular inquiry. Whether every complete physical account must determine one outcome is a further assessable claim, not something protected from criticism by the word *categorial*.

Real necessity is nevertheless **relative**. It begins from conditions it has not itself posited, each of which is an actuality whose independence can again be questioned: "**it has a presupposition from which it begins, it has its starting point in the contingent**" (*SL* §1219). Hence "**real necessity is in itself also contingency**" (*SL* §1221). The connection is necessary given the conditions; their being there is not explained merely by deriving what follows from them. A modern conditional derivation can have genuine inferential validity without establishing either the truth or the necessity of its premises. That distinction preserves its achievement while locating the further question, rather than saying that a proof is only as necessary as its premises.

The remaining limit is therefore precise. Formal necessity had no developed determinate content; real necessity has such content but receives it as a presupposition. It returns from the otherness of possibility and actuality, "**but not from itself to itself**" (*SL* §1221). Its unity with contingency is so far only implicit. To reach **absolute actuality**, the presupposing itself must be shown as a moment of the same movement, not met by appending one more independently given condition.

## VI. Absolute Necessity — and Why Hegel Calls It Blind

**Absolute necessity** (*absolute Notwendigkeit*) is that necessity: the one whose presupposing is its **own act**. This is not established by declaring that a finite outcome has used up its conditions and thereby produced them. §22 showed ground and condition to be moments of one mediation; the corresponding unity must now be established in the determinations of modality. Real necessity has proved to contain contingency within itself (*SL* §1221). As determinate necessity it is actual; yet this actuality, held simply as the unity of itself and possibility, is an empty determination and thereby contingent (*SL* §§1222–1224). Its becoming is not only an external passing between opposed determinations: each unites with itself in the other. The negative positing of actuality and possibility is also the positing of their unity as immediate, so that what necessity presupposes is a moment of this same movement (*SL* §1225). Contingency is not abolished; necessity determines itself as contingency in repelling itself from itself and returning into itself. This is the **self-positing** at issue, not a transfer of §22's result that dispenses with the modal derivation. Hegel's own formulations run the same way: "**This actuality, which is itself as such necessary, for it contains necessity as its in-itself, is absolute actuality — actuality which can no longer be otherwise, for its in-itself is not possibility but necessity itself**" (*SL* §1223); "**It is therefore necessity itself which determines itself as contingency** — in its being repels itself from itself and in this very repulsion has only returned into itself" (*SL* §1226); and, of the presupposing, "**contingency is absolute necessity, it is itself the presupposing of that first, absolute actuality**" (*SL* §1230).

Why, then, is this necessity **blind** (*blind*)? Its differences stand as **free actualities**, each presenting itself as independently grounded and leaving no trace of its relation to the other. Their unity is not manifest **in their relation**. Hegel says exactly that: "**Absolute necessity is therefore blind**", because its sides are "**free actualities, neither of which is reflected in the other**, nor will let any trace of its relation to the other show in it" (*SL* §1229). This is not merely our ignorance of a connection that the category already exhibits. Their determinate being is also their perishability; in their negation the necessity concealed in their apparent independence reveals itself (*SL* §1230). The claim is objective and severe, not merely a promise that a nicer category will arrive.

Nor is its scope exclusively technical. The Addition to *Enc.* §147 calls necessity blind when an **end** is not explicitly present in its process, and develops the contrast through **Providence**, **theodicy**, ancient **destiny**, and Christian **consolation**. It says "**Necessity is blind only so long as it is not understood**", but also distinguishes gods merely known from God as **self-knowing**. Knowledge and the religious interpretation therefore belong to Hegel's text; they must not be denied in order to protect him from fatalism. Equally, they cannot replace the logical mediation. Freedom requires that the unity become manifest as self-relation **in the other**, through substance, causality, reciprocity, and the Concept (*Enc.* §§157–159), not merely that an observer understand an otherwise unchanged constraint. §08's account of a unity still blind because not manifest in the relation gives the appropriate preliminary formulation.

The immediate result here is **substance**, not yet that completed unveiling. "**This identity of being with itself in its negation is now substance**"; and "**The blind transition of necessity is rather the absolute's own exposition, the movement of the absolute within itself which, in its alienation, rather reveals itself**" (*SL* §1231). The identity in the perishing determinations belongs to their own movement. But substantiality will itself prove deficient (§26): calling the transition self-exposition does not already make necessity fully transparent or free.

## VII. The Categorial Site of the Modern Modal Disputes

The chapter develops **categories**, not particular laws, constants, or probability distributions. That boundary limits what follows from its argument; it does not give its claims scientific immunity. In particular, **completed conditions entail a determinate outcome** is stronger than the uncontroversial claim that outcomes depend on conditions. A contemporary application must show what its conditions include, what kind of possibility is meant, and what warrants the asserted consequence. Similar vocabulary cannot perform those tasks.

**Modal logic and possible worlds.** Truth at some accessible world is not simply the consistency of an isolated description. A model includes worlds, valuations, and an accessibility relation; restrictions on frames correspond to modal axioms, and soundness and completeness results connect proof with validity. These are genuine achievements in determining which conclusions follow under specified assumptions, not merely pictures of claims established elsewhere (see the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, **“Modal Logic”**, sections on possible-worlds semantics and frame conditions). The applicability of a model to a physical or historical situation is a further question, but its conditional inferential achievement does not vanish for that reason.

A bounded comparison with Hegel remains useful. Bare consistency does not establish **real possibility**; a determinate inquiry must establish the circumstances and connections relevant to its object. But frame restrictions need not be arbitrary imports, and not every epistemic, deontic, or metaphysical modality asserts that a Hegelian **totality of conditions is assembled**. Even a claim of physical possibility may concern alternatives compatible with partial conditions rather than the completed real possibility that Hegel identifies with necessity. The comparison should expose the difference of tasks, not certify Hegel's critique as a refutation of modern semantics.

**Chance.** Hegel gives contingency objective categorial standing rather than treating it simply as a defect in our knowledge. This supports the methodological refusal to call every contingent feature a failure of inquiry. It does **not** by itself establish irreducible physical indeterminism. A probabilistic law can determine a distribution without uniquely determining an individual outcome; identifying that relation with Hegel's *SL* §1217 would require an additional argument. The status of particular physical alternatives needs scientific and philosophical assessment. Conversely, Hegel's claim that necessity includes contingency remains more than the banal observation that we do not know every condition.

**Law and its ground.** §24 distinguished a law's stated connection from the demonstration of its necessity. Here the modal question is what follows under determinate conditions and what accounts for the conditions themselves. Not every scientific explanation is a necessitating deduction, but one that is such a deduction still need not explain its starting-point. **Absolute necessity** is not a final conditioned link with an impressive name; Hegel proposes that presupposing and positing belong to one self-mediation. This is a claim about actuality, not merely about our dissatisfaction with explanations. Its theological reach should not be suppressed: *Enc.* §50 criticizes traditional proofs of God for leaving their finite starting-point affirmatively standing, while defending thought's elevation beyond it. The logical reconstruction is thus not the addition of an external first cause, but neither is it a neutral procedure with no metaphysical commitment.

**Consolation.** A bare assurance that everything happens for a reason supplies no determinate explanation. It should not, however, be identified without argument with formal necessity, still less with the whole of Hegel's account. *Enc.* §147's Addition explicitly argues from destiny and resignation to the preservation of subjectivity in Christian consolation. That theological interpretation is Hegel's, and its justification is not supplied merely by distinguishing the three levels of modal necessity. Refusing an empty assurance and assessing that interpretation are different tasks.

## VIII. Three Misreadings, Answered Directly

**The first is the status-quo reading of the double proposition.** Hegel repeats the *Philosophy of Right*'s formula in *Enc.* §6: "**What is reasonable is actual and What is actual is reasonable**" (Wallace translation). It does not say that whatever happens to exist is rational. Replying to his critics, Hegel points to the greater *Logic*, where actuality was "**accurately distinguished … from the cognate categories of existence and the other modifications of being**" (*Enc.* §6 Remark). §08 already recorded both the distinction and the need for a determinate argument in applying it.

But it is equally wrong to say that the contingent simply falls **outside actuality**. The modal chapter calls it **an actual** and derives contingency as a determination of actuality itself. In *Enc.* §6 Hegel explicitly distinguishes the **emphatic name** of actuality from the casual use that includes every error, evil, and transitory existence. The full unity of essence and existence is not exhausted by actuality's immediate formal shape. The distinction prevents an automatic endorsement of whatever persists; it does not decide by itself which concrete institution adequately realizes its concept.

A tyranny may have determinate conditions of existence, even a relatively necessary connection with them, without thereby being rational. But to show that a particular order fails the emphatic standard requires an account of its concept and realization, not the declaration that we disapprove of it. Hegel's same Remark also rejects the **abstract ought** that treats its own ideals as superior to actuality merely because they remain unrealized. The double proposition therefore cannot be reduced either to unconditional acquiescence or to a ready-made warrant for every criticism. The logical distinction sets a problem for concrete judgment; it does not discharge it.

**The second is that Hegel simply abolishes chance.** Contingency is produced as a category and retained within relative necessity, not merely tolerated until our ignorance is overcome. Here there is a genuine contrast with Spinoza, who denies contingency in nature and locates the name in a defect of knowledge (*Ethics* I, prop. 29 and prop. 33, scholium 1). Hegel's limits on deducing nature's particulars, examined in §27, reinforce the refusal to promise a deduction of every event from the pure Logic. Yet none of this cancels his claim that completed real possibility cannot yield another outcome. **Objective contingency, limits on philosophical deduction, and alternatives under identical complete conditions are not the same proposition.** Blind necessity is also a real determination in his account, though not the last word; its further development cannot be replaced by denying that he ever attributes it to actuality.

**The third is that the Absolute is an inert One behind its manifestations.** The chapter's positive result is **self-exposition**, not a substrate held apart from its determinations. §23 developed the thing through its properties; §24 developed the mediated unity of inner and outer; here attribute and mode must articulate that unity. But correcting the picture of Hegel is not a reason to impose it on Spinoza: immanent causation, affirmative attributes, and necessary modal connections are part of Spinoza's own account. Nor is Hegel's criticism a simple rejection of him. The *Lectures on the History of Philosophy* make Spinozism an essential commencement of philosophy; *SL* §§1287–1288 identifies its alleged error as the claim to be the highest standpoint and requires its preservation in the higher. That is the **standard** for a successful sublation, not a certificate that every historical criticism in the Remark meets it.

## IX. What the Method Did Here — Observations

The first observation is that **self-exposition is both the chapter's result and its demand upon historical systems**. The logical movement does not merely alternate predicates of an indifferent absolute: it finds determinacy in the supposed absence of determinacy, develops attribute into mode, and makes the mode's externality a moment of return. The historical assessment must then ask whether Spinoza and Leibniz's own achievements require precisely that development. Their use of different methods is not sufficient evidence that they contradict themselves.

This is where Hegel's own distinction between **defect** and **immanent refutation**, explicitly stated in *SL* §§1287–1288 and examined in §27, matters. The Remark diagnoses defects; Hegel reserves the intended refutation for substance's development into the Concept. Yet §1288 also says that a defect based on unshared assumptions is a defect only for someone starting from those demands. Our criticism tests that narrower claim: intellect's posteriority does not by itself establish the attribute's dependence upon it, nor does Spinoza's own allowance of denial among attributes by itself make each depend for its conception on what is denied. We have supplied grounds for the reading that resists those inferences, not a neutral interpretation requiring no defence. Nor are Spinoza's positive derivations erased by their failure to be Hegelian. **These two defect allegations remain contested; the intended refutation is a further argument, not a conclusion Hegel claims already to have earned here.** The separate demands concerning the thought–extension opposition and the substance–attribute–mode sequence have been reported, not resolved by this reply. Both Hegel's categorial mediation and its bearing on Spinoza's standpoint remain to be assessed in the further argument. Preservation means identifying what survives and what is transformed, not announcing that everything established has been retained.

The second observation is that **modality is developed as actuality's own determination**, in explicit contrast with Kant. The Postulates of Empirical Thought make the modal categories express the object's **relation to the faculty of cognition**, without adding determinations to its concept (A219/B266). This is not private psychology: possibility concerns the formal conditions of experience, actuality its material conditions, and necessity the connection with the actual according to universal conditions of experience. Kant's qualification that the modal principles are not **objectively synthetic** means that they do not enlarge the object's concept, not that they lack objective validity for experience (A233–234/B286–287).

Hegel addresses this position in *Enc.* §143 Remark: abstract possibility may indeed have the poverty of a subjective modality, but actuality and necessity cannot be treated as mere modes for something else. In the greater *Logic*, the advance to real possibility supplies his positive case: the actual circumstances themselves constitute possibility, and their mediation yields necessity. **Omitting a knower from the description does not by itself refute Kant's restriction on warranted cognition.** The philosophical issue is whether this development establishes the objective standing Hegel claims for it, not whether Kant overlooked conditions or confined modality to a person's attitude.

The distinction recurs in several forms. **§20** distinguished logical reflection from an act of ours; **§23** developed the thing's own determinateness while separating that result from the further question of cognitive warrant; **§24** develops appearance's own reflection; **§25** examines modality. These are connected investigations of the relation's objective standing, not interchangeable proofs that Kant's restriction has been refuted. Each result requires its particular mediation.

The third observation is that **real possibility recollects the condition-totality of §22 in a different form**. The recurrence is explicit, but so is the distinction: in conditioned ground the connecting form stands outside the conditions; here immediate actuality is itself posited as possibility (*SL* §1214). Completed real possibility yields real necessity, and that necessity still contains contingency. It is therefore not simply §22's absolutely unconditioned renamed. Recollection retains a determination while requiring its connection and limits to be established anew; it does not license mapping successive triads onto one universal rhythm.

## X. What Comes Next

Absolute necessity has become a necessary being whose determinations are its own, released as free actualities that are nothing outside it. That is substance, and the Doctrine of Essence turns to its last chapter:

- **§26 — Substance and the Absolute Relation**: **substance and accident** (*Substanz und Akzidenzen*), the whole manifest in the coming-to-be and passing-away of its accidents; then **causality** (*Kausalität*), with its examination of finite explanation and the infinite regress of causes; and **reciprocity** (*Wechselwirkung*), in which the mutual determination of substances must itself be comprehended. The unveiling has further steps; substance is not already its completion.
- **Beyond**: the Doctrine of Essence passes into the **Concept** (*Begriff*). Necessity is not discarded but developed into **freedom**, self-relation in the other, rather than supplemented by an observer's knowledge. The **subjective Concept** — universal, particular, singular; judgment; syllogism — leads through **objectivity** — mechanism, chemism, teleology — to the **Idea**.

## Coda

The absolute has not become actual merely because an expositor attached activity to an inert One. Its apparent indifference proved a determinate form; the externality of its mode, followed through, became its own reflective movement. Nor did possibility become necessity merely because we collected enough examples. Formal actuality and possibility exposed their dependence upon one another; their return yielded a unity that developed as content, and real possibility made the actual conditions themselves moments of the fact. Contingency survived that advance, and necessity had to confront the presuppositions it seemed merely to receive. The resulting unity remains **blind** where its actualities show no trace of their relation in one another; substance begins, but does not complete, its unveiling. *The methodological gain is a demand that neither an absolute nor a necessity borrow its articulation unnoticed from elsewhere. The corresponding obligation is ours as well: to show the mediation, preserve what the opposed account actually explains, and not mistake the demand for a proof that it has been met.*
