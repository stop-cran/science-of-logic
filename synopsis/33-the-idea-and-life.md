# The Idea and Life

*The twenty-fourth installment of Section III — the **first chapter of the Idea** (*Idee*), **Life** (*Leben*), the Idea in its immediate form. The living individual is not a mind housed in matter or a machine assigned an end from outside: its Concept is the immanent unity of its objectivity, whose differentiated **members** belong to the whole that sustains them. The **life process** relates this unity to an external world through need, which begins in a felt contradiction that Hegel calls pain, and through assimilation; that relation makes life active, but also finite and dependent. In the **genus** (*Gattung*) process, the individual relates itself to another of its kind and gives way to the universal that it is only implicitly. Hegel's path into Life and on to Cognition is a logical sequence, not a chronology of biological evolution or an explanation of abiogenesis. Life supplies the positive case, deferred in §28 and prepared in §27, from which an organism's own standard is reconstructed; Hegel's restriction of that standard to life, its concrete biological application, and the empirical boundary between living and non-living remain answerable to argument.*

---

## I. Why the Idea Begins with Life

§32 ended with the **Idea**, the unity of Concept and objectivity. That result is not an ideal standard waiting beyond actuality (*SL* §§1634–1635). The Idea is the claim that objectivity is true only through its unity with the Concept; its first form, however, is immediate. The Concept pervades objectivity but is not yet explicitly free within it. Hegel names this first form **Life** (*SL* §§1638–1642).

This transition gives the final sphere of the Logic a determinate beginning. Hegel's ground is that the Idea, the true in and for itself, is the subject matter of logic and has come on the scene through the Concept's own necessity; considered first in its immediacy, it is Life. Cognition, as the Concept's relation to an objectivity it knows, does presuppose an immediate Idea; but that is a further consideration, not the ground. Logic does not thereby become an applied logic or take in the psychology and anthropology often interpolated into it: it admits only a presupposition that is itself Idea, and the immediate Idea is Life. Hegel also distinguishes logical Life from natural life, whose conditions lie in other natural formations, and from life in relation to Spirit. Logic considers Life as the Idea's immediate realization, not a catalogue of natural organisms (*SL* §§1645–1647).

We must not use this distinction to confine biology to description while reserving the intelligibility of life for philosophy. Biology itself investigates living organization and its history; the comparison below takes that achievement as its starting point. Biological explanations do not by themselves establish Hegel's stronger claim about the unity of Concept and objectivity, but neither may that claim dismiss what they explain. Calling Life the immediate Idea does not identify a biochemical process, organism, or species: the category must be followed through the relations Hegel says constitute it.

## II. The Living Individual: Members of an Immanent Whole

Life first appears as the **living individual**. Its objectivity is not an indifferent substrate to which a concept is subsequently attached. Hegel's claim is that the Concept is the living object's substance: its differentiated externality is permeated by a unity that remains one through that multiplicity (*SL* §§1648–1655).

The body is therefore not a heap of parts whose relation is supplied only by an observer. The familiar sentence from the *Encyclopaedia* states the point: “The single members of the body are what they are only by and in relation to their unity” (*Enc.* §216 Addition). The *Science of Logic* contrasts a living **organism** (*Organismus*) — a “manifold, not of parts but of members” — with an object considered as a merely mechanical or chemical product (*SL* §1656). A member can be distinguished and, in some respects, separated; but its standing as this living member depends on the organism's unity. When considered apart from that unity, it falls back under relations of ordinary objectivity. Hegel does not deny material difference. He disputes that the living whole can be adequately understood as an aggregate of independently complete items.

The organism's unity is not an inert container. Hegel differentiates the living objectivity through the Concept's own moments: universality as **sensibility** (*Sensibilität*), particularity as **irritability** (*Irritabilität*), and singularity as **reproduction** (*Reproduktion*) (*SL* §§1658–1661). Sensibility is the living unity's receptivity, which takes all externality into itself; irritability, its self-limiting relation to an externality it presupposes; reproduction, the concrete return through which the individual maintains and renews itself (*SL* §§1659–1661; *Enc.* §218 Addition). The three are not separable stages: “Each of the individual moments is essentially the totality of all” (*SL* §1661).

Here the question left open in §28 can be taken up in its proper scope. Hegel calls the organism “the means and instrument of the end, perfect in its purposiveness”, and for that very reason “this means and instrument is itself the realised end” (*SL* §1656); in its self-producing process the product “is itself the producing agent” (*SL* §1657). Our reconstruction reads this as a standard internal to the organism: its activity and differentiated members constitute and maintain it, rather than serving a use assigned from outside. This is stronger than saying that an observer finds a pattern convenient. Yet it is not a free-standing scientific test. To judge a particular capacity as fulfilling or failing its role requires evidence about the organism and the activity in question; the logical form alone does not establish a physiology, a diagnosis, or an account of every deviation. The distinction between a bounded functional assessment and Hegel's stronger ontological claim remains the one §28 drew.

## III. The Life Process: Need, Assimilation, and Resistance

The living individual is not self-sufficient. It presupposes an external world that stands indifferently alongside it, yet that has, in the face of the living subject, only the value of something negative and lacking self-subsistence (*SL* §1663). Its relation to that world begins with **need** (*Bedürfnis*): the individual determines itself, posits itself as denied, and so relates itself to an other, yet maintains itself in that loss and is therefore the **urge** (*Trieb*) to posit the other as its own. Hegel calls this “absolute contradiction”, and the living being “has the feeling of this contradiction, which is pain” (*SL* §1664). “From pain begin the need and the urge that constitute the transition” by which the individual, explicitly the negation of itself, becomes also explicitly its own identity (*SL* §1665). Contradiction, said to be unthinkable, is in the **pain** (*Schmerz*) of a living being “even an actual existence”; pain exhibits the power to sustain contradiction that §21 ascribed to life (*SL* §1664; cf. §21, VI).

The living being does not merely encounter an object and leave both sides unchanged. Through its activity it achieves **assimilation** (*Assimilation*); mechanical and chemical processes are involved, but their product is taken into the living individual's own reproduction. Unlike chemical neutralization, the result is not simply a third product in which the initial sides lose their former standing. The living subject maintains itself through the change and makes the external process a moment of its own return (*SL* §§1666–1668; *Enc.* §219 and Addition).

That claim also clarifies the return of **resistance**. In external purposiveness, Hegel denies the object a power of resistance as an absolutely self-subsistent other to the Concept: the object is the Concept **in itself**, while the end is that Concept explicitly determining itself (*SL* §1610; §32, IV). Already in reproduction, however, Hegel credits Life with “the power of resistance” (*SL* §1661). There is no simple reversal: the living being is not an indifferent object waiting to be shaped by an external end. An external object that enters into relationship with a living being “does not act on it as a cause, but excites it”; because the living being is an urge, externality can enter it only in so far as the living being finds it conformable (*SL* §1665). This is the account that §26 deferred when Hegel declared the cause-and-effect relation inadmissible for living things and referred it to the Idea (§26, V; *SL* §1256). Whether that account of living response survives physiological scrutiny is a question for evidence, not for the logical form alone.

The positive result is real but finite. Life makes objectivity an immanent means of its own activity; it does not eliminate the need for an external world, and its process still presupposes what it assimilates. Yet in this coming together with its objectivity the individual, having sublated its particularity, has “raised itself to universality”: through the external life process it has posited itself as “real universal life”, that is, as genus (*SL* §1668).

## IV. The Genus Process: The Individual and Its Universal

The life process thus leads from the individual to the **genus** (*Gattung*). Having posited itself as real universal life, the individual is the genus, the identity of itself with its previously indifferent otherness. This Idea of the individual is “essentially the particularisation of itself”, and its diremption is “the duplication of the individual”: the presupposing of an objectivity identical with it, “a relationship of the living being to itself as to another living being” (*SL* §1670). The other is therefore no longer merely an indifferent object but another living individual of the same genus; in the *Encyclopaedia* this relation is the “Affinity of the Sexes” (*Enc.* §220). Since the genus-relation is the identity of the individual's self-feeling in what is at the same time another self-subsistent individual, it is a contradiction, and the living being is again an urge (*SL* §1672).

This relation is not yet the genus explicitly knowing itself. The individual's identity with the other individual, which is its universality, is as yet only “internal or subjective”; each is the genus in itself but not “explicitly or for itself”, and what exists for it is only another living individual (*SL* §§1672–1673). The genus process therefore has two sides. In one, the genus obtains actuality through the propagation of the living species, and the Idea falls back into actuality: this reflection is “only repetition and the infinite progress”, in which the Idea “does not emerge from the finitude of its immediacy”. In the other, the “higher side”, the individuals “sublate in one another their indifferent immediate existence and in this negative unity expire”, and the genus is thereby “coming together with itself”: the Idea has “sublated this immediacy” (*SL* §§1675–1676).

Hegel states the transition in a single sentence: “In copulation the immediacy of the living individuality perishes; the death of this life is the procession of spirit” (*SL* §1676; cf. *Enc.* §222). The Idea, implicit as genus, has become explicit by sublating the particularity that constituted the living species, and “has thereby given itself a reality that is itself simple universality”. As such it “relates itself to itself as Idea”: the **Idea of Cognition** (*SL* §1676). This is a logical formulation, not an empirical theory that an individual's death causes consciousness or that cognition follows from life as a dated event (cf. §09, IV). It is Hegel's warrant for the transition, and whether it suffices remains to be judged.

The point at issue is a change in the form of universality. In Life, it is embodied but still immediate; in Cognition, the Concept will have itself for its object.

## V. Biological Organization and Its History

Life makes the positive case, deferred in §28 and prepared in §27, that must precede any assessment of Hegel's boundary between organic and inorganic nature: an organism is not merely a connected system, but a unity whose differentiated activity is supposed to maintain and reproduce that unity. In both the *Encyclopaedia* and the *Logic*, Hegel makes this case through the relation of members to the whole and through assimilation, and develops it as a transition from the Idea's immediate objectivity to the genus process (*Enc.* §§216–222; *SL* §§1648–1676).

But the existence of this argument does not settle whether Hegel has established its exclusivity. Three questions must remain distinct (cf. §27, II and VIII, and the [working essay on the Concept in nature](../essays/2026-10-01-the-concept-in-nature.md)): what Hegel says the Concept is; whether Life's organization warrants the standard he assigns it; and whether his restriction of that determination to organic life (*SL* §1788) is adequate. A system's feedback or persistence alone is not enough to establish the Concept. Neither does naming an organization “living” resolve which of its differentiations belong to the whole, or show that no inorganic process can meet a relevant part of the criterion.

Biology investigates the reciprocal dependencies through which organisms maintain themselves, and the evolutionary processes through which their organization arose and changed. A member's present contribution to the whole and the historical explanation of its origin or persistence are connected but distinct questions. Randolph Nesse's [discussion of Tinbergen's four questions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6343817/) distinguishes proximate questions of mechanism and individual development from evolutionary questions of history and adaptive significance. Our comparison concerns these scientific explanations.

Natural selection can explain the spread of heritable capacities through differences in reproductive success under specified conditions, without making the resulting organization an end already directing its prehistory. A trait's persistence in a lineage does not by itself establish adaptation: inherited variants can also spread or become fixed through genetic drift. Douglas Futuyma's [account of evolutionary theory](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5566807/) distinguishes these processes and the levels at which selection operates (sections 1–2). An organism's maintenance, differential reproductive success, and the persistence of a species must therefore not be conflated. In Hegel's account of the living individual, the whole whose members sustain it is the organism; the genus process is not already a theory of natural selection or a warrant for making the species' survival the end of every adaptation.

The distinction between logical development and natural history matters also for abiogenesis. §32 used prebiotic chemistry to ask what a plausible natural route can explain beyond a reaction made to work under laboratory conditions. The further scientific question is what organization would make chemical activity a process that maintains and reproduces a living unity. The logical sequence supplies no precursor, geological condition, reaction pathway, or threshold at which chemistry becomes life. Those are matters for evidence and scientific theory.

Within biology, knowledge of present organization can guide inquiry into its emergence, while historical evidence can correct our interpretation of that organization. Our comparison asks how the dependencies established through these inquiries bear on Hegel's logical account of members and whole: whether it articulates them adequately, and whether they support or challenge the limits he assigns it. This does not identify physiological explanation with logical Life or require natural history to repeat the order of logical exposition. The relation between the histories of the disciplines is a further question. Methodological affinity must not be mistaken for historical influence, and scientific inquiry and philosophical reflection need not develop in step (cf. §05, II and IV).

## VI. Three Misreadings, Answered Directly

**1. Life is a hidden soul or vital substance added to matter.** Hegel's “soul” (*SL* §§1646, 1648) names the Concept as the immanent unity of the living individual's objectivity, not a second object inhabiting a body. The account must explain the relations that make the whole living; invoking an invisible principle would not do so.

**2. The logical sequence is a history of biological evolution.** No. Hegel distinguishes logical Life from natural life and orders the moments by their conceptual dependence. This ordering does not itself deny biological history, and leaves its explanation to empirical inquiry; Hegel's rejection of descent in the *Philosophy of Nature* (§339 Addition) is a separate claim (§05; §27, VIII).

**3. The organism's own standard is already a scientific verdict.** No. The category makes intelligible why an assessment can concern the organism's own activity rather than an observer's preference; whether a given feature sustains or frustrates that activity is a matter for evidence.

## VII. What the Method Did Here — Observations

**1. It gave the Idea a first objective form.** The result of teleology was not treated as an abstract identity or a completed product. Life makes the unity of Concept and objectivity an immediate, differentiated activity.

**2. It changed the status of the means.** Mechanical and chemical processes are not discarded; in the living process they become moments of an individual's assimilation and reproduction. Their operations remain, while their relation to the whole is transformed.

**3. It followed the whole through its dependence.** The individual is not declared self-sufficient because it maintains itself. Need reveals its dependence on an external world; the genus process reveals that it does not contain its universality as an explicit, self-conscious relation.

**4. It distinguished logical Life from natural life.** The logical account does not supply the natural conditions of an organism's existence. Our comparison asks what Hegel's account of internal purposiveness clarifies in biological explanations of organization and history, and what their results require us to reconsider. Distinguishing the logical and biological warrants does not make the inquiries unrelated.

## VIII. What Comes Next

**§34 — Cognition.** Life's immediate unity is differentiated into the knowing Concept and its object. We will follow the theoretical Idea, which takes the world as given and fills the Concept with its content, and the practical Idea, which seeks to make the world conform to its end. Their distinct achievements and their remaining opposition lead to the Absolute Idea, where the method itself becomes explicit.

**Beyond.** The Idea's release into Nature (§09, V; §10) lies beyond even the Absolute Idea and is not to be anticipated here. Life has reached the threshold of the Idea's knowing form; it has not already completed the Idea.

## Coda

Life is the Idea, but not yet the Idea for itself. Its unity is present in its members and activity; its external world and its genus still confront it as immediacies. The living individual maintains itself through what it does not simply contain, and the universal it embodies appears to it as another individual. The next transition does not erase this achievement. It asks how the Concept can know and will the unity that Life has only immediately enacted.
