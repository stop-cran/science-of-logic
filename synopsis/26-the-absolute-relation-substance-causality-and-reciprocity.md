# The Absolute Relation: Substance, Causality, and Reciprocity

*The seventeenth installment of Section III — the seventh and last in the **Doctrine of Essence** — follows **the absolute relation** through **substantiality**, **causality**, and **reciprocity**. **Substance** (*Substanz*) is not a substrate behind appearances but the **actuosity** (*Aktuosität*) of its **accidents** (*Akzidenzen*), creative and destructive in one movement. Their immediate unity remains deficient: difference must itself acquire substantial standing. **Formal causality** (*Kausalität*) distinguishes cause and effect within one power; the cause is actual **only in its effect**, and their content is identical. That identity becomes indifferent to the distinction of form in **finite causality**, which Hegel calls an **analytic proposition**. His examples and restrictions are examined, not sheltered from criticism: the causal **respect** of a thing, the alleged **remote cause**, the **transmutation** of an input by life and spirit, and the distinction between **cause**, **condition**, and **occasion**. The further treatment of **life** supplies his positive account of self-maintaining activity without proving that modern causal explanations lose their warrant. Finite causality's **infinite regress** develops into self-conditioning through **action and reaction**: the passive substance's being posited by another becomes its own becoming. **Violence** (*Gewalt*), power as external, is neither an automatic political licence nor a passage insulated from Hegel's practical philosophy; the comparison with the *Philosophy of Right* retains both his rejection of coercion taken abstractly and his contested extensions of rightful coercion. **Reciprocity** (*Wechselwirkung*) closes the progress but does not complete comprehension. Hegel grants reciprocal explanations a degree of correctness while demanding that their sides be understood as moments of a whole. The remaining substantial independence contradicts their identical mediation; **necessity** is raised to **freedom** by manifesting its inner identity. The **universal**, **singular**, and **particular** emerge as determinations of that reflection, not as an unexplained inventory, though the adequacy of the derivation still requires judgment. **Kant's** table and Third Analogy, **Hume's** account of inference, and modern interventionist and feedback models retain their distinct achievements. Three misreadings are answered: reciprocity as the last word, freedom as mere spectatorship over necessity, and the abolition of causality. Logical derivation, concrete application, and scientific warrant remain connected but distinct.*

## I. Where We Are: Necessity That Has Become Relation

§25 closed with absolute necessity: the necessity whose presupposing is its own act, whose determinations are released as free actualities that are nothing outside it. Hegel's first sentence of the new chapter refuses two articles on purpose. Absolute necessity "is not so much **the** necessary, still less **a** necessary, but **necessity**" — not an item that must be, nor the collection of such items, but necessity as such, "being, simply and solely as reflection." And necessity as such is a **relation**, for the reason that it "is a distinguishing whose moments are themselves its whole totality" (*SL* §1232).

That clause states the task, not its completed execution. §24 had already reached sides each mediated through the other as the whole relation. Here the sides are the totality of **necessity** itself: each is itself and its opposite, and there is "only one subsistence" (*SL* §1232). This does not abolish their distinction at the outset. Through substantiality and causality, the chapter must develop active and passive substances before reciprocity makes their identical mediation explicit. The absolute relation is not an external connection added to independently complete things; the chapter must exhibit how their independence belongs to the relation itself.

Hegel draws the consequence immediately: "The sides of the absolute relation are therefore **not attributes**." An attribute contained the whole absolute under a form still "**presupposed and picked up by external reflection**." §25 reconstructed the demand that exposition become the absolute's **own act**, while distinguishing that logical argument from the historical criticism of **Spinoza**. It contested two alleged dependencies: the attribute's dependence on a posterior intellect, and its dependence for conception on what is denied of it. That defence of an objective, affirmative reading does not settle the other adequacy demands or the intended substance-to-Concept refutation. Here Hegel identifies the expositor with absolute necessity, "identical with itself as self-determining" (*SL* §1233). Its sides are totalities because their apparent independence is itself posited as appearance: each is itself and its opposite. That is the stated mediation, not an automatic certificate that the historical opponent has been met on his own ground.

The chapter's plan is given in a single paragraph, and it is worth having in view from the start. The relation "in its immediate Notion" is the relation of **substance and accidents**. When substance determines itself to being-for-self over against an **other** — when, that is, the relation becomes **real** — we have the relation of **causality**. And when this, as self-relating, passes into **reciprocity**, the absolute relation is **posited** in accordance with the determinations it contains; and that posited unity, whose determinations are themselves the whole and equally determinations, "is then **the Notion**" (*SL* §1234).

Three sequences should be distinguished rather than reduced to one schema. §24 developed whole-and-parts, force, and inner-and-outer; §25 developed formal, real, and absolute necessity; §26 develops substantiality, causality, and reciprocity. The recurrence of three terms is not their proof. In §24, the immediate relation of whole and parts had to become the conditioned mediation of force, and then the developed unity of inner and outer. The modal and absolute relations must likewise be followed in their determinate content, not assigned a result by their place in a three-step pattern.

## II. Substance and Accident: the Actuosity of the Whole

**Substance** (*Substanz*) is absolute necessity taken as being — "being that is **because** it is, being as absolute self-mediation." Hegel calls it "the being in all being", and then, in one sentence, rules out the two pictures the word ordinarily conjures: it is "**neither** the unreflected immediate, **nor** an abstract being standing **behind** Existence and Appearance". The second refusal is the one to hold onto. §23 developed the thing through its properties; §24 developed the mediations of law, force, and manifestation rather than installing a second realm behind appearances. Here too an independently underlying substrate is refused. Substance is "immediate actuality itself and this as absolute reflectedness-into-self" (*SL* §1235).

Substance's **activity** is exhibited in the movement of **accidentality**. The **accidents** (*Akzidenzen*) are its own determinations in their arising and passing away, with no subsisting of their own apart from it. Hegel's account contains a methodological remark of the first importance. This movement "exhibits in each of its moments the illusory showing in one another of the **categories of being** and of the **reflective determinations of essence**" (*SL* §1236). He then runs the demonstration in miniature: the immediate something has a content; its determinateness makes it **pass over** into another; but the same quality is also a determinateness of reflection, indifferent **diversity**, which energizes itself into **opposition** and withdraws into the **ground**; the ground sublates itself, is a reflected in-itself and so **possibility**, and this in-itself becomes the **necessary actual**. Earlier determinations are recollected within accidentality, not simply repeated with their earlier standing unchanged.

The name Hegel gives to this movement is worth keeping in German: the **actuosity** (*Aktuosität*) of substance, "a tranquil coming forth of itself." And the qualification attached to it points to the difficulty developed in §III: this actuosity "is **not active against** something, but only against itself as a simple unresisting element." (*SL* §1237) Substance is already creative and destructive power, not a whole that merely displays itself. What remains to be developed is its **reflection into itself** through determinations it posits and distinguishes from itself, rather than difference that immediately vanishes in its unity.

Two shapes of substance's own showing are then distinguished, and the first is a diagnosis. On one side stands "the simple identity of being" — and Hegel names it without ceremony as "the **formless substance of ordinary thinking**", which clings to an indeterminate identity as an absolute, "an identity which has no truth". The reversal here is sharp and easy to miss: the substrate-picture is not merely rejected as false; it is placed. It is a **form determination that falls into accidentality** — the underlying stuff turns out to be an accident of the substance it was supposed to underlie. On the other side stands "the flux of accidents," which is substance as **absolute power** (*absolute Macht*): manifesting itself as **creative** power through the actuality into which it translates the possible, and as **destructive** power through the possibility to which it reduces the actual. The two are one: "the creation is destructive and the destruction is creative" (*SL* §§1238–1239).

One further result of this section will do heavy work later, and it is stated flatly. **The accidents have no power over one another.** They are the somethings, the existing things with manifold properties, the wholes consisting of parts, the forces requiring solicitation — that is, the whole preceding sphere, re-catalogued as accidentality. "In so far as such an accidental **seems** to exercise power over another, it is the **power of substance** which embraces both within itself" (*SL* §1240). One accident expels another only because its own subsisting is the totality of form and content in which both perish. Whatever else finite things do to one another, at this level it is not they who are doing it.

## III. Why Substantiality Is Not Yet Enough

The relation of substantiality is deficient, and Hegel states the deficiency in three ways that are worth separating, because the third is the one that decides.

First: "On account of this immediate identity and presence of substance in the accidents, **no real difference is as yet present**" (*SL* §1241). The flux is already articulated into determinations, but its difference has no substantial standing against the unity in which the accidents vanish.

Second, and more precisely: the relation is **one-sided in its negativity**. Substantiality "relates itself to itself **not as a negative**"; it is the immediate unity of power with itself, "in the **form** only of **its identity**, not of its **negative essence**". Hence "only one of the moments, namely, the negative or the difference, vanishes altogether, the other, the moment of identity, does not" (*SL* §1241). Identity persists while difference merely disappears; the negative has not yet been posited as substance's own self-relation.

Third: substance at this stage "is, in fact, only **as** the *inner* of the accidents, and these are only *in the substance*." Hegel's own transition follows immediately, and it is worth taking in his order rather than ours: accidentality, which *in itself* is substance, "is for this very reason also **posited** as such" — determined as self-relating negativity towards itself, and so "**substance that exists for itself and has power**." That is the stated ground on which "the relation of substantiality **passes over** into the relation of causality" (*SL* §§1241–1242).

§24's distinction between immediate and mediated identity illuminates the defect, but does not supply a substitute proof. **Our comparison** concerns a common insufficiency: a unity left merely inner has not made its manifestation its own articulated relation. Yet §24 also showed why the immediate conversion of inner and outer was not enough to yield actuality. Here the specific advance is that accidentality, already implicitly substance, is **posited as self-relating negativity** (*SL* §1242). The transition depends on that content, not on treating every earlier formula as a law automatically binding every later category.

This also develops Hegel's reckoning with Spinoza, but §25's qualification must remain in force. His enumeration charge concerns the sequence **substance–attribute–mode**, not an absence of every derivation among the modes; Spinoza already affirms immanent causation and necessary causal connections. Hegel's further demand is for determinacy's **self-negating coming-forth and return**. The *Encyclopedia* puts his criticism in an image — substance accepted without a previous mediation by dialectic is "as it were a **dark shapeless abyss** which engulfs all definite content as radically null, and produces from itself nothing that has a positive subsistence of its own" (*Enc.* §151 Addition). That is Hegel's judgment, not a substitute for assessing Spinoza's positive account. Within the present logical development, the defect is the immediacy of substantiality; its remedy is not an externally added activity, but the development of power as **positing determinations and distinguishing them from itself** — the relation of cause and effect.

## IV. Formal Causality: Nothing in the Effect That Is Not in the Cause

Substance is power "reflected into itself and not merely transitory," (*SL* §1243) power that posits determinations **and distinguishes them from itself**. What it posits as negative, as merely posited, is **effect**; substance as it is for itself is **cause**. Taken by itself, without regard to content, this is the **formal** relation of causality.

Two guards go up at once, and both are against the picture that comes unbidden. The first: cause is **originative** (*ursprünglich*) in relation to effect, but substance in its determining "does **not** begin from accidentality as if this were already an **other** to start with". Cause and its determining are "one **actuosity**". Causality is therefore not two independently constituted things with a link between them; it is one power in its own self-differentiation, and the "two" terms are its two form-determinations. The second guard: the priority of the cause is not a priority of standing. "It is therefore **as cause** that substance first has actuality. But this actuality… it has **only in its effect**." The cause is not a reservoir that may or may not discharge. It **must act**: only in acting is it a cause at all, and it "is therefore truly actual and self-identical **only in its effect**" (*SL* §§1245–1246).

From this follows the sentence on which everything downstream depends, and it is worth quoting in both directions: "**effect contains nothing whatever that cause does not contain. Conversely, cause contains nothing which is not in its effect.**" And the corollaries are as unsentimental as the principle: "in so far as cause has not yet acted, or if it has ceased to act, then it is **not cause**, and effect in so far as its cause has vanished, is **no longer effect** but an **indifferent actuality**" (*SL* §1247).

Two threads meet here. §22 anticipated the identity of content in **formal causality** while distinguishing ground from cause; §24 developed force's identity with its expression through its conditioning relation. Here the identity belongs to **actualities**: substance is actual as cause only in its effect. The *Encyclopedia* adds that the cause's dependence in the effect is itself sublated into reflection-into-self, so that "the cause consequently is in its full truth **causa sui**" (*Enc.* §153). Against **Jacobi's** charge of formalism, Hegel identifies self-causation with this mediation through the effect, not the production of something wholly separate. This is not simply Spinoza's opening definition of that whose essence involves existence, nor does the difference of formulation refute that definition. Hegel himself calls Spinoza's notions of cause of itself and substance "profound and correct", while objecting to their adoption as immediate definitions at the outset of the science (*SL* §1180).

But formal causality cannot hold this identity. If cause and effect have exactly the same content, then nothing in the content distinguishes them, and the distinction of form has no support. Hegel says the cause is **extinguished** (*erlischt*) in its effect — and the effect with it, "for it is only the determinateness of the cause" (*SL* §1248). What is left over is an immediacy **indifferent** to the relation of cause and effect, to which that relation "attaches to it externally" (*SL* §1248). Perfect identity of content has produced a content on which causality now sits like a label. This is the passage to **determinate**, or finite, causality.

## V. Finite Causality: the Tautology, the Regress, and the Inadmissible Application

Once the identity of cause and effect is indifferent to their difference of form, that identity is **content**, and content becomes something the causal relation merely **has**. Causality now works on a **given** content; its substance is **finite** substance; and Hegel delivers the verdict this whole stretch is famous for: "Through this identity of content, this causality is an **analytic proposition**. It is the **same fact** which presents itself once as cause and again as effect" (*SL* §1252).

He does not soften it. Since the form-determinations here are an external reflection, "it is, in point of fact, the **tautological consideration of a subjective understanding** to determine a phenomenon as effect and from this to ascend to its cause in order to comprehend and explain it; it is merely a **repetition of one and the same content**". The examples are his own, and they are deliberately flat. Rain is the cause of wetness — but "the rain wets" is an analytic proposition, and the same water is rain in the form of a self-subsisting fact and wetness in the form of something posited. The cause of *this* colour is a pigment, one and the same actuality twice determined. The cause of an act is the agent's inner disposition, "the same content and worth as the outer existence which it acquires through the deed." A body's motion is caused by a propulsive force — but "it is the same quantum of motion that is present before and after the impulse" (*SL* §1253).

The affinity with §22's **formal ground** is precise: a supposedly explanatory ascent can return the same content under a different form. Here the identity belongs to actual cause and effect, not merely to ground and grounded. But Hegel's applications also make claims about rain, action, and motion. Showing that a cause-name only repeats an effect does not show that every causal investigation does so. Whether an explanation identifies a previously unknown dependence or supplies a tested law must be examined, not excluded by assigning it to finite causality.

Three refinements follow, each of which is worth more than its brevity suggests.

**Only a respect of a thing is its causality.** The painter has countless properties besides those that made this picture; the propelling body is stone or wood, green or yellow. "Only those of his properties represented in the **effect** are present in him **as cause**; he is not cause as regards the rest of his properties" (*SL* §1254). A thing acts in a determinate respect, not through every property indiscriminately. This corrects an undifferentiated picture of acting things; it does not refute mechanics, which can itself distinguish causally relevant properties.

**The alleged remote cause.** Multiplying intermediate terms conceals the identity of content. Hegel's example is a man whose talent developed in circumstances arising from the loss of his father, killed by a bullet in battle. The shot might then be called the cause of the man's skill. Hegel first says it "is not by itself this cause, but only the combination of it with other effective determinations", then sharpens the verdict: "**Or rather, it is not cause at all but only a single moment which belonged to the circumstances of the possibility**" (*SL* §1255). This recalls §22's condition-totality and §25's **real possibility**. His objection addresses the conversion of one contributing circumstance into the origin of the whole content. It does not, without further argument, establish that an upstream factor cannot make a genuine causal difference when other conditions are specified (§VIII).

**Hegel rejects the application to life and spirit.** He declares it inadmissible to call food the cause of blood, certain dishes or chill and damp the causes of fever, the Ionic climate the cause of Homer's works, or Caesar's ambition the cause of Rome's republican downfall. What acts on a living being "is independently determined, changed and **transmuted** by it, because the living thing does not let the cause come to its effect, that is, it **sublates it as cause**." Spirit does this in a higher sense still. The argument combines the **identity-of-content** requirement with a positive account of the recipient's own activity; it is not independent of Hegel's philosophy of life. These relationships "belong to the **Idea** where they will first come up for consideration" (*SL* §1256). There he distinguishes a living subject's self-maintenance from an external mechanical or chemical production, while retaining mechanical and chemical processes as moments of its assimilation (*SL* §§1656–1657, 1665–1667). This explains his restriction. It does not yet show that an explanation of how an input changes a living system must treat that system as a merely passive product.

To this is joined a restriction of a different order, and its qualifier is easy to lose and important to keep: "in so far as the relation of cause and effect is **admitted, although improperly**, the effect cannot be **greater** than the cause; for the effect is nothing more than the manifestation of the cause." This is not a second inadmissible application but a constraint holding *inside* a use Hegel has just pronounced improper. Hence his impatience with the historians' habit of deriving huge events from slender anecdotes: such a so-called cause is "nothing more than an **occasion**, an external stimulus" (*Veranlassung*), of which "the **inner spirit** of the event had no need, or could have used a countless host of other such". And then the reversal, which is the sharpest thing in the passage: "such a petty and contingent circumstance is the occasion of the event **only because the latter has determined it to be such**." A great event springing from a small circumstance exhibits, Hegel says, "the conversion which **spirit** imposes on the external" — and for that very reason the external is not the cause in the process; the conversion "**sublates** the relationship of causality" (*SL* §1256).

The logical argument does **not derive a conservation law**, but §1253's impulse example is nonetheless a physical application: Hegel asserts identity of the quantum of motion before and after the impulse. To assess that assertion one must specify the bodies, the physical magnitude, and the conditions of its conservation; the identity of cause and effect alone supplies none of these. Two further questions must be kept distinct. In §1255 Hegel retains the shot as a condition among effective determinations: the disagreement concerns its standing as a cause, not whether the circumstance contributes at all. §1256's historical claim that the event needed no such stimulus, or could have used others, additionally needs an account of the event and its alternatives. **Our criticism** concerns the warrant for that exclusion and counterfactual judgment. Under the difference-making criterion developed in §VIII, a factor need not contain an outcome's entire content to have a causal role, and the availability of substitutes need not remove that role. This is a defended alternative criterion, not a concession silently attributed to Hegel or proof about a particular historical event.

The rest of finite causality works out its externality. The causal determinations **inhere** in a substrate: the moving stone has colour and shape "which do not enter into its causality." In the rain example, another force has raised the water; Hegel calls its distance from the earth alien to its original identity, its **heaviness**. Its fall removes that determination, "but also, therefore," sublates its causality (*SL* §1260). The causal activity restores the substrate's immediate existence instead of returning into itself **as cause**. That is the example's logical role; Hegel's account of heaviness is a physical premise, not a result deduced by repeating that rain and wetness are water.

And because the cause is finite — "determined as **one** moment of form over against the effect," so that it has its negation outside it and, for that very reason, has its determinateness within it as positedness — every cause demands a further cause, and every effect a further effect. This is the **infinite regress**, in both directions at once. Hegel's verdict on the two ways finite reflection meets it is one of the best short statements of the bad infinite in the whole book: reflection either "stops short at this immediate" and distributes the form-unity externally, or it "transfers the form unity into the **infinite**, and through the endless progress expresses its **impotence** to attain and hold fast this unity" (*SL* §1261).

The proposed resolution is not a first term, nor does an indefinitely extended chain simply turn into a circle. At first, what a cause **is as effect** differs from the effect it **has**; the same separation holds for the effect as cause (*SL* §1263). Yet producing an effect extinguishes the cause and makes the recipient causal: each determination's disappearance is also its renewed positing. Hegel makes this conversion the causal relation's **own** activity, no longer an external transition between indifferent substrates. The implicitly identical substrate is now posited as causality's presupposed condition: "**Causality therefore presupposes its own self or conditions itself**" (*SL* §1264). Action and reaction must develop this self-conditioning; the argument is not an empirical proof that every causal series loops back.

## VI. Action and Reaction: Passive Substance, Violence, and the Bending-Round

"Causality is a **presupposing** act. Cause is **conditioned**". With that, the relation acquires its second substance. Over against **active substance** — negatively self-related, mediating itself with itself through its negation — stands **passive substance**: "Passive is that which is immediate or in itself, but is **not also for itself**". Now "causality no longer has a substrate in which it **inheres**"; rather, "what is originative is **causality alone**". Hegel identifies the substrate as "the passive substance which has presupposed itself" (*SL* §1265).

The action of the active substance is therefore **double**, and both halves matter. It sublates the otherness of the passive substance and returns into itself — that is its **effect**. But because as presupposing it determines itself as its other, it posits the effect **in the other**; and through this second moment "the passive substance is **preserved**" (*SL* §1266). Only *some* determinations in it are sublated. Nothing here consumes its opposite.

It is at this point that Hegel writes the paragraph on **violence** (*Gewalt*), and it needs handling rather than hurrying past. "Violence is the **manifestation of power**, or power as **external**." Therefore "not only is it possible to do violence to that which suffers it, but also violence *must* be done to it" — because what is merely passive is "the self-subsistent that is only something **posited**," an actuality that is only a possibility, whose apparent immediacy is "the **illusory immediacy** which active causality strips off from it" (*SL* §1267).

The logical subject is the **passive substance**, whose supposed independence is negated: it "only **receives its due**" by losing "that immediacy, the substantiality which is **alien to it**" (*SL* §1268). This does not identify a person with passive substance or establish a right to harm one. But the practical comparison cannot be made harmless by an exact one-to-one transfer either. In the *Philosophy of Right*, Hegel distinguishes bodily coercibility from the will's capacity to withdraw from its external attachment (§91); he then insists that freedom is actual only in determinate existence, so force annulling that existence is wrong when taken abstractly (§92). A second coercion annulling a first can therefore be rightful and necessary (§§93–94). The §93 Remark also extends this argument to education and state-founding, construing the merely natural will as force against freedom's Idea. **Our judgment** is that this extension requires its own warrant: calling a will natural or an intervention liberating does not show that the intervention realizes freedom rather than suppresses it. Neither the logical necessity of negating passivity nor the biological restriction of §1256 decides that question. The comparison exposes the work needed to connect a category with a practical judgment; it neither derives a political licence from the *Logic* nor shields Hegel's practical claims from criticism.

The hinge of the section follows. Being posited in its own determination, the passive substance is not thereby cancelled; "rather that it only **unites with its own self** and therefore in being determined is, in fact, **originative**." Hence: "**Its being posited by an other, and its own becoming are one and the same thing**" (*SL* §1268). Its passivity is itself negated, and it becomes cause. This supplies a moment of the coming argument for freedom, not its whole content: reaction and reciprocity must still overcome the opposed substances' apparent independence.

From it follows **reaction**. The passive substance, converted into cause, sublates within itself the effect it received — and this reaction is directed **against the first active cause**, whose causal substantiality is sublated with the effect in which alone it was actual. The contrast with determinate causality is exact: there, the acted-upon substance also became a cause, but it "posited its effect again in **another** substance, giving rise to the progress to infinity". Here the cause "is **self-related** in the effect, because it is its other **as condition**, as something presupposed". And so, Hegel writes, "the action, which in finite causality runs on into the spuriously infinite progress, is **bent round** and becomes an action that returns into itself, an **infinite reciprocal action**" (*SL* §§1270–1271). The *Encyclopedia* uses the same image: "the rectilinear movement out from causes to effects, and from effects to causes, is **bent round and back into Itself**" (*Enc.* §154).

## VII. Reciprocity — the Truth of Causality, and Its Own Insufficiency

Before naming what reciprocity achieves, Hegel names what it displaces. "In finite causality it is **substances** that are actively related to each other. **Mechanism** consists in this **externality of causality**" (*SL* §1272): the cause's reflection into itself in its effect also repels that effect into another substance. In reciprocity this mechanism is **sublated**; originativeness becomes self-mediating through its negation. The name anticipates the later treatment of **objectivity**, but that treatment will develop the relation of objects within the Doctrine of the Concept, not merely repeat this paragraph.

**Reciprocity** (*Wechselwirkung*) presents itself first as a mutual causality of presupposed, self-conditioned substances, each of them active and passive toward the other. But the presentation immediately consumes itself. "Since the two, then, are both passive and active, **any distinction between them has already been sublated**; the difference is only a **completely transparent semblance**". What conditions the causal activity is no longer another substance at all but the cause's own passivity, "produced by its own activity". Hence the formula: "**Reciprocity is, therefore, only causality itself**; cause not only *has* an effect, but in the effect it stands, **as cause**, in relation to itself" (*SL* §1273).

Hence the verdict: reciprocity "is therefore **still only an empty mode of representing** this" (*SL* §1273), externally bringing together what is already one mediation. The *Encyclopedia* specifies the aim governing the criticism: "**supposing that our aim is a thoroughly comprehensive idea**," we should not stop at reciprocity. Otherwise it leaves matters "utterly incomprehensible", a "mere dry fact". Making Spartan manners the cause of the constitution and the constitution the cause of the manners "may no doubt be in a way correct"; what it has not comprehended is either side as a moment founded in the **concept** of the whole (*Enc.* §156 Addition). The Addition also discusses the functions and organs of living bodies. Its concession matters: a reciprocal explanation may establish something correct without satisfying this stronger demand for comprehension.

Reciprocity thus has a positive result: cause and effect are no longer independently fixed roles, and the progress has returned into itself. The *Encyclopedia* calls the two sides "**not causes, but factors of causation**" (*Enc.* §154). Merely alternating the roles cannot explain what each side is through their unity. But the scientific usefulness of a determinate reciprocal model and the adequacy of reciprocity as the final logical form are different questions. The passage to the Concept must establish the latter limit from the relation itself.

Hegel identifies the contradiction in **necessity and causality**, which have vanished in their earlier form within reciprocity: they joined identity of connection to the substantial independence, and hence contingency, of the sides (*SL* §1275). Necessity both is immediate being and has a ground, so is mediation; causality makes the conversion between originativeness and positedness explicit, while their identity initially remains inner. Now each side's becoming the other is its own reflection into itself. Their substantial independence cannot remain alongside that identical mediation: "**necessity unveils itself**." Thus Hegel's preceding announcement that "**necessity is raised to freedom**" receives its explanation (*SL* §§1274–1275). Necessity is not simply erased; its still inner identity is **manifested** in the sides' own movement. Conversely, "**contingency becomes freedom**": what had the shape of indifferent actualities is posited as one reflection, not collected into a unity by an external observer.

The last paragraph derives the moments of the **Concept** from this reflection. The formerly **passive** substance, a simple whole self-identical in its positedness, becomes the **universal**; the formerly **causal** substance, self-identical as negative determinateness, becomes the **singular** (*das Einzelne*, Miller's "individual"). The mediation between them is essential. The universal contains determinateness as **sublated**, hence the negative as negative; it therefore contains the same self-related negativity as singularity. The singular, as negation of negation, is correspondingly the same self-identity as the universal. Their simple identity is **particularity**, immediately uniting singular determinateness and universal reflection-into-self. "These three totalities are, therefore, **one and the same reflection**"; their distinction is transparent within it. "**This is the Notion, the realm of subjectivity or of freedom**" (*SL* §1276). This is an argument for the triad's genesis, not merely the assignment of three familiar names. Its adequacy remains a question of whether those determinations and their unity have actually been earned.

## VIII. The Categorial Site of the Modern Disputes About Causation

The comparisons here are **ours**. A logical derivation does not establish a particular causal dependence; neither does that limit exempt Hegel's examples or restrictions from scientific criticism. To locate a modern account in a logical category, one must examine its actual commitments rather than infer them from its vocabulary. The following comparisons distinguish a philosophical demand for grounding from the conditional knowledge an inquiry can establish.

**Hume and the missing connection.** Hume's *Enquiry*, VII, Part II, does not stop at failing to observe a necessary tie. Repetition produces a customary transition of the imagination, the felt connection from which he derives the idea of necessity; he offers accounts of cause through regular conjunction and through the mind's inference, while preserving causal reasoning's scientific and practical use. Hegel's identity of cause and effect does not concede or complete this account. It proposes a different ground: substance's self-differentiation, rather than an explanation of our idea from impressions and habit. Nor does §1253's charge of repeated content establish Hume's thesis about distinct events. As §01 insists, identifying a stronger demand does not refute an account that disputes that demand. Hegel's causal derivation and its warrant must be assessed independently.

**Causal inference, intervention, and explanatory limits.** Interventionist accounts distinguish observing a correlation from changing one variable under conditions that exclude competing routes of influence. They can distinguish **direct**, **indirect**, and **total** effects, including cancellation along different paths; an upstream variable need not be demoted to a mere condition. Our source here is James Woodward's account of these approaches in the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, ["Causation and Manipulability"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/causation-mani/), §§4–7, not a claim to have independently inspected every work it discusses. Such accounts do not require the effect's content to be identical with the cause's. A model's variables, mechanisms, and background assumptions constrain its conclusions, but this does not make its results tautological or show that it has exactly Hegel's finite causal form. The methodological comparison is narrower: a successful conditional explanation does not thereby explain all its own conditions. That further question must preserve what the model has established, not replace it with an endless-regress verdict.

**Life, history, and contributing causes.** The same source supplies a useful biological example: two gene complexes can compensate for one another, so testing their contributions requires both varying one and holding the other inactive. This is an illustration of experimental reasoning, not a named empirical discovery. It preserves the recipient's organization as part of the explanation rather than treating an input as the whole source of the outcome. Hegel's account of **transmutation** therefore does not by itself invalidate this causal question. Conversely, such a model does not establish the living whole's self-maintenance in Hegel's stronger sense. Redundancy also shows why available substitutes need not remove a factor's causal role; applying that point to a particular historical occasion requires separate evidence. **Our retained criticism** must distinguish reclassification from explanatory loss. Hegel's account already retains effective conditions; withholding the name of cause does not by itself erase their contribution. If reclassification as conditions or occasions also discards an established distinction between causal routes, however, a genuine explanatory achievement is lost. Preserving that achievement within a more comprehensive account meets the requirement Hegel sets for refutation (*SL* §§1287–1288). This is our conditional application of that criterion, not proof that a change of label violates it, nor Hegel's acceptance of interventionism.

**Reciprocity, feedback, and comprehension.** A bare assertion that *A* affects *B* and *B* affects *A* leaves their determinate connection unexplained. A model specifying their interaction need not. §08's elementary predator–prey example yields conditional results about coexistence and cycles; those are more than the name of a loop. They neither derive Hegel's category nor prove that the populations form a self-determining subject. His further demand is to comprehend the sides through the whole whose moments they are (*Enc.* §156 Addition). The same caution governs comparisons with **downward causation**: one cannot settle whether a whole and its components supply rival causal explanations merely by calling the components accidents. Their relation, the relevant causal criterion, and what each explanation establishes must first be shown. Substantiality poses a question about dependence; it is not a ready-made solution to modern disputes about emergence.

## IX. Three Misreadings, Answered Directly

**The first makes reciprocity Hegel's last word.** The slogan that everything affects everything converts a **threshold** into a destination. Reciprocity is the truth of causality, but retaining its sides as independently constituted substances contradicts their identical mediation. This is the specific inadequacy that leads to the Concept, not a denial that reciprocal models can yield determinate knowledge. §§03 and 08 already point beyond reciprocity; the close reading must earn that advance. A richer category is not imported to repair the relation from outside: its new content must be the developed resolution of this relation's own difficulty.

**The second reduces freedom to "the insight into necessity" alone.** The formula is **Engels**'s, from *Anti-Dühring*, offered explicitly as a report of Hegel — *die Einsicht in die Notwendigkeit* — and Engels immediately gives it a practical edge that the slogan drops: freedom is knowledge of laws "and in the possibility this gives of systematically making them work towards definite ends" (*Anti-Dühring*, Part I, ch. XI). The target here is not that practical paragraph but its flattening into the claim that nothing changes except that we now *know* we are determined. The *Encyclopedia* does say "**necessity is blind only so long as it is not understood**" (*Enc.* §147 Addition), and §25 retained its cognitive and theological setting: the contrast of blind necessity with an explicit end, Providence, and self-knowing subjectivity. Knowledge cannot simply be excluded from Hegel's account. Indeed, §158 Addition says that "man is most independent when he **knows himself to be determined** by the absolute idea throughout"; §159 describes thinking as meeting oneself in the other, a liberation whose individual form is the **I** (*Enc.* §§158–159). Knowledge of self-determination must therefore be distinguished from merely observing an alien constraint: the former belongs to the freedom Hegel is explaining. It does not dispense with the categorial mediation. The greater *Logic* says "**Necessity does not become freedom by vanishing, but only because its still inner identity is manifested**" (*SL* §1275). The apparently independent actualities must manifest their unity in one another; the passage through substantiality, causality, and reciprocity makes freedom **self-relation in the other**, not spectatorship over an unchanged constraint. This preserves the cognitive emphasis without substituting it for the objective development that §25 began. The *Encyclopedia* draws the corollary: "what a mistake it is to regard freedom and necessity as **mutually exclusive**" (*Enc.* §158 Addition) — freedom presupposes necessity and carries it within itself as a sublated moment.

**The third makes sublation the abolition of causal inquiry.** Hegel retains causality as a moment and supplies a further account of life rather than simply declaring living activity unintelligible. But his restrictions cannot be reduced to harmless reminders that every explanation is incomplete: §1256 expressly refuses causal standing to specified physiological and historical relations. Whether that refusal fits a modern account depends on the account's content and causal criterion. A theory of contributing causes is not refuted by the failure of an input to contain the whole effect; equally, its success does not answer every question about the recipient's self-determination. Preservation of scientific knowledge and criticism of its explanatory form have to be demonstrated together.

## X. What the Method Did Here — Observations

The first observation is that **the critique does not exempt the sphere's own highest product**. The earlier installments likewise developed determinations while examining their received formulations; they were not merely polemics against external positions. Reciprocity continues this double task. Its positive achievement must survive the criticism of its finality, just as the earlier categories' achievements must survive theirs.

The sequence also reworks **Kant's** table of relation: inherence and subsistence, causality and dependence, community or reciprocity (*Critique of Pure Reason*, A80/B106). Kant does not merely supply three unrelated forms. He derives the categories from functions of judgment, describes the third in each class as combining the first two through a distinct act of understanding (B110–111), and argues in the **Third Analogy** that reciprocal influence is required for cognizing objective coexistence (A211/B256 ff.). Hegel instead develops the relational determinations through their own insufficiencies and makes reciprocity yield the Concept. That difference of method is substantive, but the existence of his alternative does not itself disprove Kant's grounding or restriction to possible experience (§01). The question is whether the new derivation establishes the necessity it claims.

The second observation is that **the spurious infinite returns in a new logical element.** The developments **within the Doctrine of Being** — finitude, quantum, ratio, and measure — must each be assessed on their own premises. §19 examines Hegel's intended passage into Essence without certifying every proposed resolution: the equilibrium inference remains disputed, as does §17's restriction on the inverse ratio. The regress of causes here is thus the **same figure in a new element**, not a recurrence that establishes or refutes those earlier arguments by itself. Its specific problem is a form-determination that has its negation outside it and therefore, for that very reason, within it. Its proposed resolution is the **bending round** of the progress into self-return — Hegel's own image, used in both *Logics* — recalling the straight line of §13 and that installment's **circle**, now with cause and effect as its two arcs. The return of the figure does not replace the causal argument that has to establish this self-return.

The third observation concerns **recollection within a transformed relation**. Accidentality exhibits earlier determinations of Being and Essence in its own movement (*SL* §1236): determinateness, diversity, opposition, ground, possibility, necessary actuality. This is not a fresh proof of every preceding transition. It shows how determinations that had occupied separate stages now function within substantial activity. The distinction keeps the method's economy from becoming a licence to certify an argument merely because its vocabulary has appeared before.

The fourth observation is that **an offered derivation and its adequacy are distinct**. The Subjective Logic does not simply announce universal, particular, and singular as an inventory; §1276 develops them from reciprocity. That answers the literal charge that no derivation is attempted. It does not by itself answer the stronger suspicion that the derivation has arranged its destination. The local test is whether self-related negativity and self-identity warrant these distinct but identical totalities without an imported classificatory premise. Their subsequent work in concept, judgment, and syllogism is a further test, not a replacement for this one. §27 must retain that distinction rather than defer the whole question to later usefulness.

## XI. What Comes Next

The Doctrine of Essence closes with an actuality whose highest relation has shown itself to be a threshold. Mediation has not disappeared: the independence of the related substances is sublated into a totality whose distinctions are its own. The next Doctrine must develop this **self-determination**, rather than merely rename reciprocal dependence.

- **§27 — The Concept**: the third Doctrine opens with the **subjective Concept** — **universal**, **particular**, and **singular** taken up as they have just been produced, no longer as classificatory conveniences but as the moments of one self-differentiating whole. With them, the reckoning the school logic has been owed since §01: whether these inherited words can be made to carry a doctrine that school logic would not recognize. And with them a sustained engagement with Kant, in which the **original synthetic unity of apperception** is called one of the profoundest insights of the *Critique* and convicted in the same breath of not fulfilling the promise of its beginning.
- **Beyond**: **judgment** (*Urteil*, read by Hegel as **original division**, *Ur-Teil*), in which the Concept divides itself; the **syllogism** (*Schluss*); **objectivity**, with **mechanism**, **chemism**, and **teleology**; and the **Idea**, where life, cognition, and finally the **absolute Idea** make the method itself the subject matter. The later treatment of mechanism must be read in that new setting, not identified without mediation with finite causality's externality.

## Coda

The chapter moves from the power in which accidents disappear to a relation whose sides have substantial standing, and then through that standing's own mediation. Cause is actual in its effect; the passive becomes active; reciprocity makes each side's apparent dependence its own return. Hegel's result is not a larger network but **self-determination**, the proposed truth of necessity. This reconstructs the movement without ratifying every physical application, historical dismissal, or claim to have refuted an opponent on that opponent's ground. *The methodological demand is double: preserve what an explanation actually establishes, and ask whether its own form accounts for the connection it employs. The advance to the Concept must meet that demand, not merely announce that connectedness is insufficient.*
