# Being-in-itself, Being-for-other, and the Limit

*The third installment of Section III — a close reading of the inner structure of the Something. Its two moments — **being-in-itself** (*Ansichsein*) and **being-for-other** (*Sein-für-Anderes*) — develop the self-preservation in otherness reached in §11. Their unity and the differentiation of what is present in Something yield **Determination** (*Bestimmung*) and **Constitution** (*Beschaffenheit*); their mutual passage makes alteration explicit in Something itself. From this emerges the **Limit** (*Grenze*), through which Something and Other both are and cease; the contradiction of Something with its own immanent limit opens the path to **Finitude**.*

---

## I. Where the Previous Installment Left Us

§11 closed with self-preservation in otherness, not with the reduction of Something to whatever is not the Other. The Other, taken in its own determination, alters itself; yet what it becomes is again Other. It thus unites with itself, and its result is a self-identical Something with otherness retained as a distinct moment. Something relates to its otherness without being simply that otherness (*SL* §§218–219).

This result gives two moments: relation to otherness, in which Something preserves itself, and self-relation in opposition to that relation. They are **distinguished within the Something itself**, not an external connection plus an independent interior added to compensate for it. As §11 showed, the initially indifferent pair of Something and Other has developed into relations within one unity (*SL* §221).

These two sides Hegel names **being-in-itself** (*Ansichsein*) and **being-for-other** (*Sein-für-Anderes*). The task of this installment is to develop them — what they are, what they are not, why their reciprocal play forces the categories of **Determination** and **Constitution**, and how their interaction produces the **Limit**, from which the entire arc to Finitude and Infinity will follow.

## II. Being-in-itself and Being-for-other

Take the Something as reached in §11. Its quality is no longer merely immediate determinacy: the distinction of Reality and Negation has been sublated into simple self-relation. The development through otherness has now distinguished two moments within this unity.

**Being-in-itself** (*Ansichsein*) is the Something's preserved being, self-related **in distinction from** its relation to the Other. It is self-equality in opposition to otherness, not an inventory of properties that would remain if every relation were withdrawn (*SL* §221). The distinction from otherness belongs to this self-relation; it is not proof of independence from all mediation.

**Being-for-other** (*Sein-für-Anderes*) is the same Something **in** its relation to otherness. Otherness belongs to it and is distinguished from it; Something is not simply lost in that relation (*SL* §§219–220).

Three clarifications are essential before going further, because each of them blocks a standard misreading.

**First, the German particles do not mean what their English glosses sometimes suggest.** *An sich* — "in itself" — does not mean "considered as it really is, behind appearance"; it does not mean "secretly," "essentially," or "as it would be in some privileged metaphysical inspection." Here it names self-relation in opposition to the relation to otherness. Likewise, *für Anderes* — "for other" — does not mean "as it appears to the Other," in the sense of how the Other perceives it. It means: *as it stands in the relation to the Other*. There is no observer in this picture. There is no appearance/reality distinction. The terms name distinct moments of the same Something, not a related thing and a relationless remainder. The richer Essence-level meanings of *Ansich* belong to the Doctrine of Essence and have not yet been earned. But they are not all of a kind, and the difference matters for everything that follows. *Appearance versus essence* is genuinely earned there (§23, §24). *The in-itself behind the for-us* — a second world withheld from what shows — is never earned at all: §24 stages precisely that world in order to **collapse** it, and concludes that there is one world, "present as it appears and as it essentially is." So the fault in importing that picture here is not merely that it is early. It is that it is false.

**Second, the two sides do not correspond to two parts of the Something.** The Something does not have an "inside" and an "outside" the way a thing has a hidden interior and a visible surface; it is not made up of an *Ansichsein*-piece glued to a *Sein-für-Anderes*-piece. It is *one* Something. The distinction is not an arbitrary choice between our viewpoints: relation to otherness and self-equality opposed to that relation have emerged within its own self-preservation.

**Third, the two sides are inseparable.** As §11 showed, being-in-itself is not an immediate being left over after relation has been subtracted: it is self-relation mediated through the negation of otherness. Being-for-other is likewise not pure Nothing or a contentless relation: it belongs to the Something that preserves itself in it. Each moment contains a reference to the other (*SL* §§221–224). Isolating either as the whole would lose the result from which both have arisen.

What we have, then, is a structure already familiar in pattern: a Something that is *one* and yet contains within itself an opposition between two sides. *Dasein* had Reality and Negation as distinguished moments of its Quality (§11). Something has *being-in-itself* and *being-for-other* as moments of *its* unity. The recurrence is transformed, not a law prescribing the next move: §10 distinguishes the Absolute Idea's free release from the transition of an inadequate logical category; §19 will show the *mode* of movement changing at the threshold of Essence; and §27 will exhibit the universal particularizing itself as "creative power," expressly **not** a transition. What the recurrence licenses here is an expectation to be tested at each step, not a schema to apply in advance.

## III. Anticipating Misreadings Again

Hegel already addresses the philosophical weight of these terms in the present passage. Their identity is formally given here, developed more expressly through inner and outer in Essence, and most precisely through Concept and actuality in the Idea (*SL* §226). These later forms must not be read back into the present one. Nor should a popular picture of hidden essences be substituted for Kant's own critical distinction. Four misreadings need separating.

The first is the **noumenon/phenomenon misreading**: that being-in-itself is the Something "as it really is, behind appearances," while being-for-other is "the Something as it appears." Here the terms are *moments of the determinate*, not levels of access to it; no observer or distinction of essence and appearance has been derived. Kant's position is more guarded than that popular picture. His negative noumenon is the thing thought apart from our sensible mode of intuition, a limiting concept rather than a positively known hidden world; a positive noumenon would require an intellectual intuition we do not possess (*Critique of Pure Reason*, Transcendental Analytic, "Phenomena and Noumena"; §01). Hegel criticizes the thing-in-itself **here**, not only later: abstracting from all being-for-other leaves no determinations by which the question of what the thing is could be answered (*SL* §227). His positive continuation is that the *Logic* expounds the true in-itself as the concrete, internally determined Concept, not as an empty abstraction. This states his alternative; it does not, merely by showing determinations to be present in Something, answer Kant's question of what warrants cognition apart from the conditions of possible experience. The later discussions (§23, §24) develop Hegel's rejection of a final separation without making this local identity an automatic refutation on Kant's own ground.

The same local remark also distinguishes what is still **in itself**, or implicit, from what the development has **posited**. Hegel charges metaphysics, including critical philosophy, with missing this distinction (*SL* §228). The distinction concerns the argument's achieved content, not whether we have noticed a property. In Being the movement takes the form of transition; positing in the stricter sense belongs to Essence, where, for example, ground posits what is grounded. We shall need the local contrast between implicit and posited below, without importing that later mode of movement.

The second is the **private-self misreading**: that being-in-itself is what the Something is "to itself," in some quasi-subjective sense, while being-for-other is what it is "to the other." There is no self-consciousness here. The Something does not yet *have* a self in any reflective sense; it has only the minimal self-relation derived in §11 (the sublation of the distinction within Quality, through which its unity is posited as simple, mediated self-relation). The "for" in *für Anderes* is not a relational predicate from one consciousness to another; it is the structural position of the Something *as standing in the relation to its Other*, with no perceiver on either side.

The third is the **hidden-essence misreading**: that being-in-itself is some deep nature that the Something *has* independently, which then *manifests itself* in its being-for-other. This is essence-talk, which the Doctrine of Being has not yet earned. At this level, being-in-itself is not a deeper layer; it is the Something's self-relation in opposition to its relation to otherness. There is no descent from a hidden inner to a visible outer — both sides are equally on the surface, in the precise sense that *Dasein* has no inside-vs-outside structure of that kind.

The fourth is the **relational-eliminativist misreading**: that since the Something is constitutively in relation to its Other, the talk of *being-in-itself* is empty — there is no "in itself" to speak of, and the Hegelian move is just a confused holdover from substance-thinking. This is the mirror image of the hidden-essence misreading, and it is just as wrong. The whole point of distinguishing the two sides is that *neither* side, taken alone, can sustain the Something. A purely relational Something is no Something; a purely isolated Something is no Something. The structure requires both, distinguished within the unity.

Avoid all four imports, and the two sides come back to their actual scope: *Ansichsein* as preserved self-relation opposed to the relation to otherness, *Sein-für-Anderes* as that relation itself — distinguished moments within one Something.

## IV. From the Two Sides to Determination and Constitution

The next step is not simply to acknowledge the two sides more explicitly. Their reciprocal reference must develop into a further determination of the Something itself.

Ask: what is the Something *in itself*? It is withdrawn from otherness, but that withdrawal is itself a negative relation: being-in-itself is the non-being of being-for-other (*SL* §223). The moment supposed to exclude relation therefore contains relation within its own determination. This is not because the name "this" means nothing but "not-the-other"; it follows from the mediated self-relation reached in §11.

Conversely, being-for-other negates the simple self-relation of Something, but it is not pure Nothing. Its negative determinate being points to being-in-itself as its own being reflected into itself (*SL* §224). The reference back is internal to the moment, not a demand that we supply an independent content from outside.

Because both moments belong to one and the same Something, what it is in itself is also **present in it**, and what it is as being-for-other likewise belongs to this unity (*SL* §§225–226). Hegel distinguishes *an sich* — **in itself**, as self-relation — from *an ihm* — **present in it**, as determinateness belonging to Something. "Present in" does not mean manifest to an observer. Nor does their formal identity mean that every determinateness is already an achieved fulfilment of the in-itself. It means that the in-itself is not a relationless remainder: its other moment is present within the same Something.

This is the passage to **Determination** (*Bestimmung*). Being-for-other, taken back into the unity of Something, is determinateness reflected into itself; in the simple form of being it is once again a quality, but now a quality mediated through these moments (*SL* §§229–230). Determination is thus not the in-itself merely renamed. It is **affirmative determinateness maintained in relation to the Other**: Something preserves its self-equality by making this determination hold good in its being-for-other (*SL* §231).

The word carries both a constitutive and a vocational sense: what Something is in itself, and what it is *to be*. Fulfilment is conditional. Something fulfils its determination insofar as the further determinateness developed through its relations becomes a congruous filling of its in-itself (*SL* §231). Hegel's example is that "the determination of man is thinking reason" (*SL* §232). Thought is not merely an absent ideal: a human being actually thinks, and this thinking has content. Yet the determination, together with the filling incorporated into it, still confronts natural and sensuous determinateness not incorporated into it; in this respect it remains something that ought to be. The vocational sense is already explicit, but the developed relation of **Restriction** and **Ought** has still to be reached. Neither the actual presence of thought nor the incomplete fulfilment may be dropped.

What is present in Something therefore differentiates: there is determinateness incorporated as the filling of its determination, and determinateness that remains being-for-other, held outside that determination. The latter is **Constitution** (*Beschaffenheit*) — Something's external determinate being, which belongs to it but is distinguished from its in-itself (*SL* §233). This separation is possible because, in Quality, differences retain immediate, qualitatively independent standing even as sublated moments. Constitution is not a wholly unrelated addition; but neither has the identity of the moments already abolished their distinction.

In its Constitution Something is open to external influences. A particular influence or make-up may appear contingent; **having a constitution**, however, belongs to Something's quality (*SL* §234). Initially alteration is assigned to this side alone: the Constitution changes while Something preserves its Determination (*SL* §235). This provisional indifference is important. The argument must show why the separation cannot hold, rather than replace it at once with a declaration that everything interpenetrates.

Hegel gives two directions of passage, connected through **what is present in Something** (*SL* §236). **Determination passes into Constitution:** because the in-itself is present in Something, it is burdened with being-for-other. The determinateness united with it retains otherness as a qualitatively distinct moment; that otherness therefore enters the in-itself itself. Determination cannot remain an untouched standard over against alterable Constitution: it acquires the character of Constitution.

**Constitution passes into Determination:** isolate being-for-other as Constitution, and it is otherness taken on its own. As §11 showed, the Other in itself is the Other of itself; its self-othering is self-relation. Constitution thus proves to be self-related determinate being, an in-itself with determinateness — a Determination. This is not merely the converse assertion that something external must affect something internal. It repeats, in this more developed content, the self-relation reached through otherness.

Their separation can consequently no longer be held in its initial form. Even insofar as they are kept distinct, determining from outside is itself determined by Something's immanent Determination; Constitution is not simply imposed on a passive recipient. Conversely, Constitution belongs to what Something is in itself: **Something alters with its Constitution** (*SL* §236). The contrast between what it *is* and what it merely *has* cannot secure an invariant core against this result.

The gain is precise. Alteration is no longer only implicit in Something's Concept, nor confined to a for-other side that leaves its in-itself untouched. **Alteration and negation are now posited in Something itself** (*SL* §237). No threshold of sufficient divergence has been introduced. It is the attempted separation of Determination and Constitution that has undone itself, and that result supplies the next transition.

## V. The Limit

Determination and Constitution have passed over into each other. Their difference is sublated, yielding Something in general again; but the difference from which it results includes qualitative otherness, so the result is *two* somethings. Their negation is no longer obtained "only from comparing them" but is "now immanent in the somethings" (*SL* §238). Each preserves itself through the sublation of its otherness, while the Other also retains qualitatively distinct being. The affirmative Something is therefore no longer immediate: it relates to the Other "through its own nature… because otherness is posited in it as its own moment" (*SL* §239).

From that, and not from any postulate, the next determination follows. There is, Hegel writes, "a *single* determinateness of both," which on one side is identical with the somethings' being-within-self as negation of the negation, and on the other side "conjoins and equally disjoins them through their own nature, each negating the other" — and he names it in the same breath: "this determinateness *is limit*" (*SL* §239). This is the **Limit** (*Grenze*).

The development of this Limit must now be followed, not replaced by a picture of two regions meeting at an edge. The contradiction is not proved merely by saying that the same border can be described from two sides. Hegel develops how the negation of the Other becomes Something's own non-being, while remaining the mediation of its being (*SL* §§240–243).

**First, the Limit negates the Other — but the Other is itself Something.** Initially Something limits its Other, and the limit seems to be only that Other's non-being. Yet the Other equally limits the first Something as *its* Other. The same limit is therefore the non-being of each Something, not an exclusion from which either one remains exempt (*SL* §241).

**Second, Something is through that same Limit.** The ceasing of the Other in Something is its own affirmative being: through the limit it is what it is and has its quality. Limit is thus present in Something itself as the mediation both of its being and of its non-being (*SL* §§242–243). The affirmative moment cannot be removed in favour of sheer negation, nor the negative moment turned into something that concerns only the Other.

**Third, these moments initially fall apart again in the form of qualitative immediacy.** Something has its determinate being "outside (or, as it is also put, on the inside) of its limit"; the limit is the non-being in which Something and Other cease (*SL* §244). This is the intelligible basis of the spatial picture, not simply a reader's mistake. It preserves the difference of Something from its limit. But it cannot be the final account, because Something has just proved to be what it is *through* that limit.

Does their shared limit erase the difference between Something and Other? On the contrary, it is abstraction from the limit that erases it. "Something as it is outside the limit, the unlimited something, is only a determinate being in general"; each then has the same determination as its Other (*SL* §246). What remains is not pure Being, but Something in general, without the distinction at issue. Distinctness is present only "with the determinateness as limit, in which both are what they are, distinguished from each other" (*SL* §246). There is no private positive core that first distinguishes them and then receives a common boundary.

Now the contradiction can be stated in its developed form. Something has its determinate being only in its limit; yet determinate being and limit are also the negative of one another, as the preceding separation exhibited. **Something therefore separates from itself in the very determination through which it is itself**: it points beyond itself to its non-being and passes over into it (*SL* §246). This is more than mutual exclusion between two self-contained qualities. The limit is immanent, and the unrest belongs to Something's own self-relation.

Hegel uses point, line, plane and solid in this very passage to illustrate these determinations; the spatial example need not wait for Quantity. The line first appears as line outside its limit, the point (*SL* §245). But the point is also where it begins, and Hegel calls such limits "the principle of that which they limit"; he then reads their unrest as the point's dialectic into the line and the line's into the plane (*SL* §246). He expressly reserves the application itself to the consideration of space, giving only an indication here. The illustration is qualitative, not a derivation of geometry from the mere image of a moving point; it must not replace the mediation just followed.

## VI. From the Limit to Finitude

The sublation of the difference between Determination and Constitution led to Somethings whose negation is immanent; the Limit then articulated their unity and distinction. Its further development has not merely added an external threat to their being. Something's relation to its own limit is now its contradiction with itself, directing it beyond itself (*SL* §247).

The difference from the merely limited is decisive. Something with a quality and limit initially stands as affirmative and stable. The developed opposition of its determinate being and its immanent limit is now itself its self-relation; in that relation it is a becoming rather than a stable being with negation placed beyond it (*SL* §248). Its non-being is not merely where another begins: it constitutes Something's own nature.

This is **Finitude** (*Endlichkeit*).

A finite Something does not merely alter; it ceases to be, and this ceasing belongs to its being rather than remaining a possibility that might never occur (*SL* §249). **Finitude is the immanent limit posited as Something's own contradiction**, not simply a limit newly recognized as constitutive. Constitutivity was already established. The further result is that Something's negative self-relation sends it beyond its own being: it has its end in itself.

The next installment will develop this in full: the structure of finitude proper, **Restriction** and the **Ought** (*Schranke* and *Sollen*) as further articulations of finitude's internal contradiction, the way finitude immanently passes over into infinity, and the difference between *spurious* infinity (the endless repetition of finite-after-finite) and *true* infinity (the infinity that includes finitude as a moment of itself rather than excluding it). That arc — from finitude through spurious infinity to true infinity — closes the first major movement of Quality. It is also the point at which, for the first time in the *Logic*, a determination is shown to be **infinite** — and the contemporary reader is most often startled by what "infinite" means at this stage of the exposition. The next installment will address that directly.

## VII. Method Observations at This Step

Three observations about the present step; how far each reaches must be tested at later steps.

The first is that **the moments are re-determined, not multiplied by counting**. The unity of in-itself and being-for-other returns as a quality, Determination; what is present in Something differentiates into congruous filling and externally held Constitution. Their provisional indifference gives way through two distinct passages, yielding alteration posited in Something itself. These are changes of content, not new labels attached to unchanged sides. §27 records the warning against counting articulated moments: number "since it has **the unit** for its principle, converts them as **counted** into completely **isolated and mutually indifferent** determinations." What matters here is precisely the transformation that such indifference would erase.

The second is that **the resulting determination must undergo its own development**. The mutual passage of Determination and Constitution does not by itself prove Finitude. It leads to the immanent negation articulated as Limit; only the Limit's further mediation shows Something separating from itself through its own determinateness. Earlier resolutions likewise did not simply arrest the movement: Becoming yielded *Dasein*, whose Quality developed into Something and Other. But recurrence is not a licence to omit the particular argument or a law governing the whole *Logic*. §10 distinguishes the Absolute Idea's release into Nature, an act of "absolute liberty", from the passage of a further deficient logical category into its successor, without suppressing the still-logical Idea's urge to sublate its confinement; and §27 exhibits the universal specifying itself as "creative power," expressly **not** a transition. The expectation of further movement must remain answerable to the determination and mode of movement actually reached.

The third is that **the move from Limit to Finitude makes the Something's own end an immanent determination of its being**. Self-relation has not waited for this step: §11 already reached Something and then the two moments of its self-preservation in otherness. The Limit is likewise not merely a boundary supplied by an external comparison. Here the further determination is that the immanent limit belongs to the Something as its own contradiction: its non-being constitutes its nature, and its self-relation sends it beyond itself (*SL* §§247–249). This is not the first turn inward, nor a transfer to the Doctrine of Essence. It is a further development within Being, whose consequences the next installment will trace.

## VIII. What Comes Next

The next installment will develop:

- **Finitude proper** — the Something as that which has its end in itself; the *Schranke* (**Restriction** — the Limit taken as the barrier standing against the Something's own Determination) and the *Sollen* (the **Ought** — the inner ferment by which the finite presses against its restriction); the immanent contradiction of finitude that requires the move to infinity.
- **Spurious infinity** — the endless succession of finite-after-finite, the "bad infinity" of the understanding, and why it is not yet what Hegel means by *the* infinite.
- **True infinity** — the infinity that does not stand against the finite (which would make it itself a finite, since "infinity-vs-finitude" is a relation between two determinations and so a finite structure) but contains finitude as one of its own moments. This is one of the most original determinations in Hegel's *Logic* and one of the most commonly misread.

With true infinity, the first major movement of Quality closes. The path from there opens into **Being-for-self** (*Fürsichsein*) — the determination in which the Something has fully internalized its Other and stands as one — and from there to the dialectic of the **One and the Many**, which prepares the passage from Quality into Quantity.

---

## Coda

The internal articulation has deepened, but not by accumulating independent terms. Self-preservation in otherness became the unity of in-itself and being-for-other; the differentiation of what is present in Something yielded Determination and Constitution; their mutual passage made alteration explicit in Something itself. The Limit then joined and distinguished Somethings through their own negation, and its development made that negation Something's own end. Each gain depended on preserving the preceding content while changing the form in which it could stand. The next question is therefore not which pattern to repeat, but whether Finitude can sustain its own result — whether the end it carries can itself remain the last word.
