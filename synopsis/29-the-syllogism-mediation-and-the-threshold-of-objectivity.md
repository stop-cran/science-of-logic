# The Syllogism: Mediation and the Threshold of Objectivity

*The twentieth installment of Section III — the **third and concluding chapter of Subjectivity**, redeeming the closer reading promised in §09 and taking up the second half of §27's comparison with formal logic. Judgment has filled its copula until the subject's constitution supplies a **middle term**; the syllogism makes this mediating unity explicit. Hegel's thesis, "**Everything is a syllogism**" (*SL* §1448), concerns the articulation of things, not their composition out of three sentences, and it must be distinguished from the inadequacy of the **immediate** syllogism in which the thesis first appears. The development turns on one contradiction: the middle is supposed to be the determinate unity of the extremes, yet stands against them as another independently fixed determination. In the **syllogism of existence**, this gives both the contingency of selecting one property from a concrete thing and the regress of proving premises by further premises. The figures are followed as successive determinations of that difficulty: **S–P–U**, **P–S–U**, **S–U–P**, each moment occupying the middle, until their fixed qualitative distinction disappears into **U–U–U**, the mathematical syllogism. Its equality-relation is not invalidated; what is refused is its claim to be presuppositionless and its sufficiency for comprehending the Concept's differentiated unity. The other, positive result of the figures is mediation that itself rests on mediation, and this demands a richer middle. The **syllogisms of reflection** supply it successively as **allness**, **induction**, and **analogy**: collected concrete instances, their indefinitely extended series, then an instance taken as bearing its kind's nature. Each makes explicit a presupposition the preceding form needed; analogy finally asks whether the transferred property belongs to the instance through its kind or only through its contingent constitution. The **syllogisms of necessity** make that grounding their content: **categorical**, **hypothetical**, **disjunctive**, moving from an inner substantial identity, through the actualization of conditions, to a universal explicitly differentiated into its mutually exclusive and exhaustive species. The three figures recur within both reflection and necessity; their warrant lies in the changed mediation, not in the symmetry of the names. In the disjunctive syllogism the middle contains the extremes as its own determinations; the distinction of mediating and mediated is sublated, and the result is **objectivity**, an immediacy produced by mediation rather than a return to an unexamined given. The derivation is reconstructed, not certified by its name: it depends on Hegel's account of the Concept as already objective in significance, and neither manufactures an empirical object from a thought nor establishes a particular scientific classification. Nor is the result already the Idea: the first objectivity is **mechanism**, in which the Concept's unity is not yet freely explicit. Throughout, formal validity, empirical warrant, and categorial development are kept distinct without being sealed into separate territories. **Our reconstruction** confronts Hegel's depreciation on its strongest ground: the treatment of Concept-determinations as conceptless material. It distinguishes his method's preservation of subordinate content from our further defence of bounded formal inquiry; preservation alone does not establish a contradiction between method and verdict. Induction and analogy retain their scientific work without being promoted into guarantees of necessity. §27's derivation debt is partly discharged by the transitions exhibited, not settled by the recurrence of its triad. The endpoint is the threshold of Objectivity, **not yet the release into Nature**.*

## I. The Copula Has Become a Middle

§28 ended where the old logic ordinarily inserts a page break. Its last judgment was no longer merely *this action is good*, but *this action, being constituted in such a way, is good*. The constitution was not an additional predicate decoratively attached to the subject. It was the ground of the subject's correspondence to its concept — the determination through which subject and predicate were related. What had been the thin copula *is* was now a relation with content.

Hegel's concluding sentence names the result:

> "Through this impregnation of the copula the judgment has become the syllogism." (*SL* §1435)

The new chapter immediately specifies what has been recovered:

> "We have found the syllogism to be the restoration of the Notion in the judgment, and consequently the unity and truth of both." (*SL* §1436)

The Concept held its differences in unity; judgment posited them as self-subsistent extremes and left their unity as an assertion across the divide. The **syllogism** (*Schluß*) neither retracts the differentiation nor simply repeats the assertion. It posits their **determinate unity** as what mediates them. The subject is not merely said to be the predicate; the relation is exhibited through a middle.

This is already the governing question. **What must the middle be if it is genuinely to unite the extremes?** The answer cannot be settled by placing any third expression between two others. At first the middle is exactly such an independently fixed determination, and Hegel's chapter follows the consequences of that inadequacy until mediation no longer confronts its own terms as a third thing.

There are two debts to discharge. §09 compressed the movement into an itinerary from the qualitative syllogism to objectivity. §27 asked whether the Concept's three moments actually generate the subsequent logical work, rather than merely lending familiar names to an inherited inventory. §28 examined judgment; the present installment examines the **figures and kinds of syllogism**. Neither a table of correct forms nor the assertion that all forms are dialectical will pay the debt. The transitions must be watched.

## II. “Everything Is a Syllogism” — What the Claim Claims

The opening thesis is deliberately stronger than a theory of inference:

> "Everything is a syllogism, a Universal that through particularity is united with individuality; but it is certainly not a whole consisting of three propositions." (*SL* §1448)

That qualification belongs to the thesis, not to an interpreter's attempt to make it less extravagant. Hegel has just rejected the picture in which the nature of things first frames a major premise, then picks up a minor, then extracts a third proposition. Such a succession is a possible **subjective presentation** of reasoning, not the order in which a thing acquires its constitution (*SL* §§1445–1447).

The claim is that the universal is actual through a particular articulation and a singular realization, and that the singular is what it is through that articulation of its universal (*Enc.* §181). The middle is therefore not necessarily an intervening object, still less a mental device laid across an otherwise unconnected world. It is the **determinate relation** in which the extremes belong together. Hegel even warns that the spatial suggestion of *middle term* encourages us to treat the three as externally adjacent (*SL* §1438).

Here the distinction established in §27 remains decisive. The logical significance of the Concept is not confined to a consciousness that happens to think about things. Yet **Subjectivity** is still a determinate stage: its moments have not yet overcome the form in which their unity stands apart from them. Objective significance and the achieved category of **Objectivity** are not interchangeable. Otherwise the destination would have been reached before the chapter began.

The *Encyclopaedia* is explicit that even the syllogism of understanding has an objective significance: it expresses the **finitude of things**, whose properties and universality are not yet held together as their own self-mediating unity (*Enc.* §182). The inadequate form is thus not merely an incompetent reasoner's mistake. Nor, conversely, does an object's finitude make a valid inference about it invalid.

The thesis's scope should be faced rather than softened. Hegel does not offer *everything is a syllogism* merely as a convenient description of some explanations. It is a claim about the rational constitution of actuality. What this installment can show is how the syllogism's own determinations are developed toward the unity that claim requires. It cannot vindicate the claim by finding three words in an arbitrary scientific explanation and labelling them universal, particular, singular.

## III. The First Figure: A Middle That Does Not Yet Mediate Itself

The **syllogism of existence**, also called the qualitative syllogism, begins with **S–P–U**: singularity united with universality through particularity. Our *S* follows the synopsis's word **singular**; Miller and Wallace write *I*, for **individual**. The letters below name categorial functions, not three permanently assigned objects.

Particularity initially supplies the middle because it has two sides. Against the universal it is determinate and subsumed; against the singular it is universal and subsuming. Hegel's example in the *Encyclopaedia* is a rose related to colour through red (*Enc.* §183). Red is not an arbitrary interpolation: it is the respect in which this rose belongs to the universal sphere of colour.

But the two-sidedness is not yet an adequate unity. The rose is an immediate concrete thing with many determinations; the middle selects one of them; the universal predicates what follows through that selection. Thus the singular, particular, and universal still confront one another as independently fixed terms. The middle **does** mediate the inference, but its own relations to the extremes have not been mediated.

Two consequences follow, and they must not be collapsed.

**First, contingency of content.** A concrete thing supplies many possible middle terms, and the same middle can connect it with different predicates. The form as so far developed supplies no principle for deciding which determination expresses the thing's nature rather than one isolated aspect (*SL* §§1449–1451). Looking back from the syllogism of allness, Hegel illustrates the difficulty with a picture judged pleasing solely through its greenness, and a building judged beautiful solely through regularity (*SL* §1487). A property may matter without deciding the whole judgment.

Hegel's sharper formulation says not merely that different middle terms can yield opposed conclusions, but that the conclusion can be false although the premises taken by themselves and the inference are correct (*SL* §1450). His examples include a painted wall, human sense-nature, and planetary gravitation. We need not endorse the physical particulars of those examples to identify the argument, and this installment draws no physical law from them. Hegel expressly refuses to shift the defect merely onto the content: the abstract form itself makes one isolated quality the middle.

**Our reconstruction** accepts the abstract form's limitation for comprehending the concrete object without withdrawing its warranted conditional inferences. When Hegel grants correctness to premises taken **by themselves** and to their inference, that is not yet a demonstration of **soundness** in the contemporary sense — valid inference from true premises. Nor should §28's truth/correctness distinction be used to make the difficulty disappear: there a judgment can correctly report an untrue content, whereas here Hegel calls the **conclusion** false. The interpretation defended here grants the inference only under its isolated determination and denies that this warrants an unrestricted conclusion about the concrete whole. It neither shifts the entire defect to poor premises nor certifies the examples as sound deductions. Two sound arguments cannot yield contradictory conclusions about the same subject in the same respect; nothing in the reconstructed development requires that they should.

**Second, contingency of the connections.** The conclusion is mediated, but the premises — *S–P* and *P–U* — are still immediate judgments. The form demands that the extremes be united through a ground while taking its own grounding relations as given. If each premise must in turn be proved by another syllogism of the same kind, two premises become four, then eight, without altering the defect.

> "The unending process of proving the premises does not resolve this contradiction but only perpetually renews it and is the repetition of one and the same original defect." (*SL* §1454)

The objection is not that every deduction ought to re-prove every assumption before it can be used. A deduction from stipulated premises remains a determinate achievement. The objection arises when **this form is offered as a self-sufficient account of rational grounding**. It makes mediation essential and repeatedly postpones the mediation of what it itself requires.

Hegel's next step is accordingly not to add further terms without end. It is to make explicit what the first inference has already done to its terms. Its conclusion has determined the singular as universal; its minor premise already determined the singular through particularity. The singular is therefore the point in which those two determinations meet. What appeared only as an extreme must now occupy the middle.

## IV. The Figures: Each Moment Must Take the Middle

### (a) Singularity: P–S–U

The second figure makes **singularity** mediate particularity and universality. This is Hegel's second figure, traditionally counted as the third; he expressly records the difference of ordering (*SL* §1461). Reversing the printed order of the extremes — *U–S–P* rather than *P–S–U* — does not change which moment mediates. It does not authorize reversing the subject–predicate relations of the premises indiscriminately.

The move has a double basis. As regards content, the first form depended on a determination encountered in this concrete singular. As regards form, its own conclusion joined the singular with the universal. The new middle therefore exposes the **immediacy on which the previous mediation depended**, rather than introducing an unrelated specimen from a logician's collection.

Hegel tests this figure's formal correctness by its conformity to **S–P–U**: the middle must both subsume and be subsumed. Here the singular is subject in both premises. Recasting one relation so that it can perform the other function requires a **particular judgment**, and the conclusion can therefore only be particular. Hegel does not merely record a useful restriction: this judgment, he says, is both positive and negative, and consequently "**no great value can be attached**" to its conclusion (*SL* §1461). This is the account of particular judgment already encountered in §28: *some* carries *some not* with it (*SL* §1395).

**Our illustrative inference uses a different, explicitly existential reading of *some*.** From *this rose is red* and *this rose is a plant* we infer *at least one plant is red*. The illustrated rose is assumed to exist. Neither *every plant is red* nor *some plant is not red* follows: the premises could hold even if every plant were red. We retain this restricted inference without attributing its interpretation of *some* to Hegel or treating it as a refutation of his account on premises he shares.

Hegel's next step depends on his own determination. Because the particular conclusion is positive as well as negative, he takes its relation as indifferent to those determinations and hence universal. The contingency of mediation through this immediate singular is now posited: what should unite the extremes is itself externally determined, not their determinate unity. The middle therefore points beyond itself to **abstract universality** (*SL* §§1462–1463). Our example makes the limit of a singular conjunction intelligible; it does not supply this entire derivation.

### (b) Universality: S–U–P

The third figure therefore puts **universality** in the middle. But it is initially an **abstract universal**. Two terms' inclusion under a common predicate does not establish their determinate connection. That roses and trees are plants does not make a rose a tree.

Hegel's stated rationale for its formal correctness is again conformity to the first figure. The universal is predicate to both extremes; one relation must therefore be rearranged so that it also occupies the subject's place. He appeals to the negative judgment, in which he treats the subject–predicate relation as indifferent, and the conclusion is necessarily negative (*SL* §1466). **Our illustration** uses a universal exclusion: *no stone is a plant* can be converted to *no plant is a stone*; with *this rose is a plant*, it warrants *this rose is not a stone*. This exhibits the conversion and valid exclusion, not a botanical or geological classification derived from logic.

The development has now made each moment mediate, but none as an isolated determination has supplied the required totality. The particular connected through one property; the singular through a contingent instance; the universal through an identity abstracted from the extremes' determinacy. Hegel calls this third figure the truth of the **formal syllogism as a whole**, not merely of its last attempt: the middle unites the extremes only abstractly, leaving their essential determinateness outside that unity. What has become explicit is the formalism itself (*SL* §1465).

There is also a circle of dependencies. The first figure leaves *S–P* and *P–U* unmediated; the other figures take those relations as what is to be mediated, while drawing on relations the first supplied. Hegel calls the determination of the syllogism completed, but each figure still contains an immediacy mediated **outside it** (*SL* §1464). The *Encyclopaedia* specifies that this completion is "**only implicit**" (*Enc.* §189); in reflection the dependence will be posited **within the syllogism itself** (*SL* §1489). **It is not a licence for circular proof.** The three schemata do not manufacture the truth of their premises by referring us around a triangle.

This is the point at which the inherited table has been made to move. The figures are no longer merely alternative places for an unchanged middle; each brings to light a determination that the preceding mediation required but could not itself sustain. The objective demand is correspondingly stronger: *Enc.* §187 presents rationality as a **triple syllogism**, each moment serving as middle as well as extreme. Merely cycling the letters does not meet that demand; the content of each mediation must be exhibited.

### (c) What has actually been derived

The result is narrower than a derivation of every textbook mood. Hegel expressly says that he has treated the principal conditions of correctness without pursuing all combinations of affirmative, negative, universal, and particular premises (*SL* §1476). This is not a neutral division of labour: *Enc.* §187 condemns the inquiry into which moods yield correct conclusions for its "**purely mechanical nature**" and its "**intrinsic meaninglessness**". We return to that verdict below. He also rejects the traditional fourth figure as an empty distinction; his own fourth, which follows, is **not** that figure (*SL* §§1467–1469).

The debt to §27 is thus being paid by exhibiting the work of the moments, not by pretending that the entire historical syllogistic has been reconstructed here. We have followed Hegel's argument for the changing determination of the middle and shown simple inferences that make two formal restrictions intelligible. These examples neither establish an exhaustive inventory of valid moods nor discredit a calculus that supplies one.

## V. The Mathematical Syllogism: What Abstraction Preserves and What It Leaves Out

The immediate result of the terms' interchangeability is **U–U–U**:

> "The mathematical syllogism runs: if two things or determinations are equal to a third, they are equal to each other. Here the relationship of inherence or subsumption of the terms is extinguished." (*SL* §1470)

The equality is symmetrical; which term serves as the middle depends on which equalities are given. Unlike the first figure, its form does not assign one term the role of subsuming universal, another that of subsumed singular, and a third their differentiated unity. What has disappeared is that **qualitative distinction of functions**, not the possibility of a correct inference.

Hegel's criticism is precise at its strongest point. The mathematical axiom is commonly treated as an immediately self-evident first principle requiring and admitting no mediation. Yet its quantitative equality presupposes abstraction from qualitative difference. A triangle and a square can be equal **in magnitude**, not identical as triangle and square (*SL* §1472). Equal areas are not the same shape. Hegel claims more than the exposure of a presupposition: the axioms, as determinate logical thoughts, are deducible from self-determining thought, and "**To deduce them is to give their proof**" (*Enc.* §188). The proof claimed here is the categorial derivation of the equality-form, not another calculation conducted within it.

The argument does not amount to a derivation of all modern mathematical uses of equality from this passage. Its target is the supposed presuppositionlessness of the relation as Hegel has characterized it. Mathematics can explicitly specify its objects, equalities, and rules; within such a framework the inference may be entirely warranted. What the equality-form does not do merely by being applied is explain why these determinations are the appropriate ones or how they develop into a different categorial form.

This discharges a concrete part of §27's comparison. **Hegel's textual verdict**, **its local warrant**, and **our judgment of the retained achievement** are not the same thing. He credits the study of the forms of reason, including its contribution to correctness, and rejects contempt for it as barbarism, yet condemns the received syllogistic apparatus (*SL* §§1476–1477). He holds that negative and particular judgments weaken formal determinacy and that certain restricted conclusions yield "**completely worthless results**" even in respect of form (*SL* §§1477–1479). His strongest ground is not simply that fixed forms fail to develop. Arithmetic legitimately operates on conceptless material; treating the syllogism's form-determinations, which are Concepts, in that way is for him the "**harshest and most glaring of contradictions**" (*SL* §1479). The very §62 to which we appeal for determinate negation also rejects making the external course of conceptless quantity the course of the Concept. Together with *Enc.* §187's condemnation of the inquiry's mechanical character, this ground must be answered, not reduced to a preference for a different task.

**Our judgment distinguishes the standards.** Under the existential reading stated in §IV, *at least one plant is red* excludes *no plant is red*, but does not by itself identify which plant; the singular premise supplies the witness. A valid negative conclusion likewise excludes a connection. These are determinate differences in what may be asserted, although neither by itself comprehends a kind through its own principle. We accept that latter limit but contest the depreciation of the former achievement. This semantic standard is ours, not an immanent refutation of Hegel's positive-and-negative reading of particular judgment.

**The comparison with Hegel's method has a limit of its own.** His *SL* §1479 grants only that the formal syllogism determines what follows or does not follow **from this middle**; its adequacy as proof remains under criticism. **Determinate negation** requires the result to contain the content from which it results (*SL* §62), and the higher standpoint must contain the subordinate one (*SL* §§1287–1288; §27, III). But retaining a subordinate determination does not require retaining the school presentation of it or valuing an exhaustive table as rational cognition. Hegel himself examines the conditions of formal correctness in §§1461 and 1466, and their restrictions enter the subsequent development (§§1462, 1467–1469). That shows formal determination at work within his argument, not his endorsement of an autonomous calculus. We have therefore not shown that his higher treatment erases the conceded content: preservation alone does not demonstrate the claimed contradiction between his method and his verdict.

**Our further defence concerns the legitimacy of a bounded abstraction.** Holding a form fixed for a stated investigation need not assert that it is intrinsically fixed or that its determinations are merely conceptless material. Hegel's own examination of formal correctness shows that such conditional work can enter the development; it does not by itself vindicate the whole mechanical inquiry. For that further assessment we state our criterion: an inquiry has a legitimate inferential task where it distinguishes warranted assertions from unwarranted ones under explicit premises and rules. Testing a proposed mood can expose an invalid inference. Checking a finite inventory's claim to include every form allowed by its stated rules can expose an omitted case; this is a question about coverage, not merely a count of combinations. Neither task makes the fixed forms the adequate account of the Concept. On that ground we reject the dismissal of the bounded inquiry as meaningless, without claiming that Hegel's preservation requirement alone compels this valuation. Its results still do not derive its categories or establish their adequacy. **Both the formal achievement and the categorial development require their own demonstration.**

The mathematical figure is not, however, the only result of the preceding movement. Hegel marks the distinction:

> "But the result of the syllogism of existence is not merely this abstraction from all Notional determinateness" (*SL* §1473).

There is a **positive** result as well. Each moment has occupied the middle and the extremes; each has thereby acquired relations to the other moments. And what each figure presupposes is not simply an unexplained datum: it is another mediation. The result is therefore **mediation resting on mediation**, with the earlier externality brought within a reflected whole (*SL* §§1474–1475).

This does not make a deductive circle evidentially sufficient. It changes what the middle must be. Instead of an isolated quality, it must contain singularity and universality together. Equality preserved their interchangeability by abstracting from the differences; the next form must preserve their **determinate relation**. That is the syllogism of reflection.

## VI. Reflection: Allness, Induction, Analogy

### (a) Allness: the concrete instances return

The first reflective middle is **allness** (*Allheit*): not a property considered by itself but all the concrete singulars possessing it. It retains **S–P–U** with this richer middle (*SL* §1491). Hegel's contrast is between judging through abstract greenness and judging all actual green things with their other properties included (*SL* §§1485–1487). The step matters because the object cannot now be treated as though the one selected quality exhausted it.

But the improvement makes a new dependence visible. If *all humans are mortal* means the collected fact about every singular human, then Gaius's mortality is already a condition of the premise's being true. The supposed ground cannot be established in that way without the case it is supposed to ground (*SL* §1488).

**Not every universal premise is thereby circular.** A universal grounded in a theory or in a demonstrated relation need not be established by first checking every application. The defect belongs to **allness as the external collection of instances**. Hegel himself later distinguishes the categorical syllogism precisely by its not presupposing its conclusion for its premises (*SL* §1509); the present criticism must not erase that distinction.

Once the major premise is recognized as resting on the singulars, those singulars must themselves occupy the middle. What allness used without exhibiting is made explicit in **induction**.

### (b) Induction: the series cannot supply its own necessity

Induction returns to **U–S–P**, but the singular middle is now the attempted completeness of the instances, not a lone specimen. Hegel illustrates the enumeration with lion, elephant, and so on, taken together as quadrupeds. The full collection is supposed to be coextensive with the genus and so capable of occupying the subsuming position that the earlier singular could not fill (*SL* §§1491–1492). That changed mediation gives the recurring figure its point. It is a demand on the enumeration, not a zoological classification established by logic.

The difficulty is not that adding observations adds nothing. It is that a further observation, taken simply as another immediate singular, does not by itself transform the series into a necessary connection of kind and property. For an open empirical domain, the series can always be extended. *All so far examined* is not *all as such*, and still less *all by virtue of their nature*.

The qualification matters. Exhaustively checking a finite, independently delimited collection can establish a claim about every member of that collection. Mathematical induction is a proof-principle, not the indefinitely repeated inspection Hegel is discussing. Neither is refuted by his account of the empirical series. His target is the attempt to obtain the universal connection simply by extending immediate singularity.

Hegel's next move is sharper than the familiar complaint that the next case might differ. To accept the incomplete experience as holding beyond the observed cases already presupposes that the kind is connected with the determination, rather than merely that the observed individuals happen to coincide (*SL* §1495). The singular must stand for more than itself: it must be taken as manifesting its **universal nature**.

Thus induction's unfulfilled requirement produces **analogy**. The move does not add a missing observation. It changes the significance of the observed instance, which now mediates as an instance **of a kind**.

### (c) Analogy: in virtue of what does the instance bear its property?

Analogy returns to **S–U–P**, but its universal middle is now an individual taken in its **universal nature**, not a bare common quality. Another individual is connected with a predicate because it shares that nature with the first (*SL* §1496). What is transferred is no longer supported merely by extending a list.

Hegel's example is deliberately instructive: the earth is inhabited; the moon is taken as an earth; therefore the moon is inhabited. The ensuing analysis supplies the objection rather than endorsing the conclusion. Is the earth inhabited **as a heavenly body**, or only through determinations peculiar to this heavenly body? The common kind does not decide that question (*SL* §1499). Nothing in the logical form establishes the empirical conditions of habitation.

The *Encyclopaedia* nevertheless credits analogy with important results in the experiential sciences (*Enc.* §190). Its anticipation is that a property found in experience has its ground in the thing's nature. That is a genuine advance over an unexamined accumulation, not yet the achievement of the ground anticipated.

The distinction is now exact. **Sharing a kind** is one claim; **having a property through that kind** is another. Analogy immediately joins them in its middle, but has not exhibited their unity. It therefore demands that the relation between the original instance and its predicate itself be grounded — precisely what it proposed to provide for the second instance.

Hegel calls for the sublation of the immediate individuality of the middle (*SL* §1500). This does not mean deleting the specimen from the world or dispensing with evidence. It means that the specimen's bare possession of a property can no longer serve as the ground of its transfer. The middle must be the **objective universal** through which the property belongs to the instance.

That is the passage to necessity. A limitation has yielded a determinate requirement, not a free certificate that a scientific theory already satisfies it. Whether this property really follows from this nature remains to be established in each science. The Logic develops the form of that grounding; it supplies neither the scientific nature nor the empirical demonstration.

## VII. Necessity: The Ground Must Become Explicit in Its Terms

The syllogism of necessity begins where analogy's immediate identification of specimen and kind has been overcome. The middle is no longer some selected property, nor the external collection of instances, nor an instance standing provisionally for its kind. It is their **determining nature**.

> "This syllogism is therefore the syllogism of necessity, for its middle term is not some alien immediate content, but the reflection-into-self of the determinateness of the extremes." (*SL* §1503)

Still, necessity is not the endpoint simply because the name has arrived. The substantial unity is at first **inner**; its terms retain a form of indifference to one another. The last three syllogisms develop that discrepancy between content and form.

### (a) Categorical: one nature, still an inner bond

The **categorical syllogism** unites the subject with its predicate through its substance — substance now comprehended as universal nature, not merely as a substrate carrying accidents (*SL* §§1506–1507). Its particular middle is the genus; the universal extreme is a determinate principle of its differentiation; the singular is their concrete unity.

Here the old schema **S–P–U** has acquired a different content. The relation is no longer grounded in whatever property happens to be convenient. The middle is the subject's essential nature, and the predicate expresses its essential determination. Hegel says without qualification that the demand for proof which produced the earlier infinite regress "**does not arise**" (*SL* §1508): the connection is not an external judgment simply appended to independent terms.

This must not become a device for exempting a scientific premise from proof by calling it essential. The result is conditional on the nature and relation having been exhibited. The question is what the syllogism is **when** its connection is such a relation, not whether pronouncing the word *genus* establishes one.

Its remaining defect is that this content has not yet completely determined its **form**. The singular appears as this independently existing instance among others; it also has determinations not contained in the genus. The extremes are essentially connected yet still presented as indifferent actualities (*SL* §§1511–1512).

Their relation thus says two things at once: they subsist on their own, and their immediately independent standing is contingent in relation to the unity that makes them what they are. The unity is consequently still an **inner necessity** confronting the externality of its manifestations. To make that relation explicit is to distinguish the connected terms as **conditioning and conditioned actuality**. The categorical syllogism develops into the hypothetical (*SL* §1513).

### (b) Hypothetical: necessity that is

The hypothetical judgment said only *if A is, then B is*. The syllogism adds *A is*, and concludes *B is* (*SL* §1514). As a formal inference this is familiar. Hegel's concern, however, is not merely the substitution of a proposition into a rule.

The middle is **the being of A**, interpreted through the necessary relation: the existence of the conditions as the mediation of the conditioned. Hence the return to **U–S–P**, with singularity now the actuality of the conditioning content (*SL* §1522). Hegel names condition and conditioned as the most appropriate relation here, while also allowing ground and consequent or cause and effect (*SL* §§1516–1517). This is not an account of every truth-functional conditional as a causal law.

The new work lies in the conditions' two-sidedness. They exist immediately, yet as conditions their significance is not exhausted by their separate existence. They are the material of an actuality in which their dispersed standing is overcome. Their actualization is the **negative unity** that mediates the result: the necessary connection is not merely inward but active in what exists (*SL* §1518).

And the conclusion is not wholly different from that middle. *B is* asserts an immediate actuality, but one that is through its conditions. Hegel identifies the content of conditioning A and conditioned B, and says not merely that they are related but that "**the difference of A and B is an empty name**" (*SL* §1520). Their independent standing has been sublated; mediating and mediated are moments of the same unity.

Our restriction concerns the application of that claim, not a weakening of its wording. §26 already traced Hegel's **identity of content** in cause and effect; that must not be denied here or confused with identity in every respect. The present argument concerns the necessary relation with the conditioning content taken in its totality. It does not prove that arbitrary propositions *A* and *B* are interchangeable or that every implication is reversible.

The next demand is now determinate. The identity can no longer remain merely an inward bond between differently named actualities. It must exhibit its own differentiation and the unity of that differentiation. This is the **disjunctive syllogism** (*SL* §1521).

### (c) Disjunctive: the middle contains the extremes

The disjunctive syllogism returns to **S–U–P**, its middle now a universal whose particularization is explicit and complete (*SL* §1522). The genus is not placed beside a list obtained elsewhere; it contains its division into species. Those species are mutually exclusive as well as collectively exhaustive. Hegel's two forms are:

> "A is either B or C or D,  
> But A is B,  
> Therefore A is neither C nor D." (*SL* §1523)

and:

> "A is either B or C or D,  
> But A is neither C nor D,  
> Therefore A is B." (*SL* §1523)

Exclusivity warrants the first exclusion; exhaustiveness warrants the second determination. Neither is supplied simply by writing *or*. A taxonomy whose alternatives overlap or fail to cover the domain would not have the form here required.

The same content now appears as universal sphere, as determinate species, and as the singular determination that excludes the alternatives. The middle is therefore not only the universal in contrast with the particular and singular: it contains **all three determinations**. Hegel's A is the universal articulated into its species and determined as this one.

This is not a claim that an ordinary process of elimination produces a world. Its formal correctness can be assessed given a stipulated partition; Hegel's further argument concerns a partition which expresses the **objective universal's own determination**. The stipulated case illustrates the inferential relation, not the self-grounding of that universal. Confusing them would make the modest achievement of correct elimination carry a metaphysical result it has not earned.

The decisive sentence is therefore not one of the displayed conclusions:

> "For the middle term, which is posited in it as the totality of the Notion, contains itself the two extremes in their complete determinateness." (*SL* §1524)

The extremes no longer possess an independent determination standing outside the middle whose mediation they need. Their difference is the middle's own articulated difference. The **mediating** and the **mediated** have become moments of one totality.

For that reason Hegel says that it is "**no longer a syllogism at all**" (*SL* §1524). Our explanation of this unqualified formulation concerns the shape that has been sublated: a third mediating determination separated from independently standing extremes. It does not follow that the displayed inferences cease to support their conclusions. The syllogism completes itself by removing the externality that made that shape necessary.

## VIII. Why the Result Is Objectivity — and Why It Is Not Yet the Idea

At the start, the middle was the unity the extremes **ought** to have, represented by another term. At the end, what it mediates is contained within its own determination. Hegel's retrospective distinguishes **two axes**: the **figures** exhibit each moment in the middle; the **kinds** exhibit the middle's increasing concreteness. Existence makes the isolated moments mediate in turn; reflection gathers the extremes' determinations **externally**; necessity makes their unity at once developed, total, and simple (*SL* §1527).

This also explains the pattern across kinds without erasing the figures within each. Reflection **as a whole** has the singular middle, **P–S–U**, because its universality rests on collected singulars; necessity has **S–U–P**, the objective universal (*SL* §1501; *Enc.* §191). Yet each develops the three figures internally, with a changed content at each return. The recurrence is not itself the derivation; its claim to necessity must be tested against the work just followed.

The conclusion is not the abolition of mediation without remainder:

> "The result is therefore an immediacy which has issued from the sublating of the mediation, a being which is no less identical with the mediation" (*SL* §1529).

**Immediacy as result** means that the whole no longer depends on a middle standing outside it. It is not bare Being again, and not a return to an unexplained datum. Its relation to itself contains the mediation through which it has been determined.

This is why Hegel calls the result **objectivity**. The Concept's unity is no longer only an inward connection over against an external multiplicity; that multiplicity has exhibited the unity in its own articulation (*SL* §1528). The *Encyclopaedia* names the object as this **realisation** of the Concept, a totality which has acquired immediate unity through the sublation of mediation (*Enc.* §193).

The distinction from a subjective act of inference is indispensable. A reasoner does not make a thing exist by producing a third sentence. The argument begins from the Concept developed through Being and Essence, not from a mental representation to which existence is then added. The object is the logical determination of a self-standing concrete totality, not the prize for having arranged some premises correctly.

But it would be equally evasive to say that Hegel has changed only our vocabulary. He claims to have exhibited **the Concept's objectification**. The opening of Objectivity explicitly connects the movement with the ontological argument (*SL* §§1530–1533). Its force therefore depends on the Concept's **already objective significance**, argued in §27, and on the success of the development, not on the uncontroversial validity of a syllogistic mood.

Our assessment is correspondingly bounded. The chapter provides an intelligible derivation of the required **logical determination**: when mediation is no longer external to its terms, their unity has the form of an immediate, self-standing totality. This is more than simply declaring thought and being identical. It is not, by itself, an empirical demonstration that any proposed object exists, nor does it settle every objection to Hegel's identification of logical and objective determination. Those objections have to address that identification and its grounds; a counterexample to an invalid formal inference would not by itself reach the argument.

A final difficulty is productive rather than incidental. If the unity has been achieved, why does the next section begin with objects external to one another?

Because the result is **immediate objectivity**, not the Idea. The mediation has withdrawn into the result; the Concept is not yet freely explicit as the principle governing the object's differentiation. The totality's moments themselves have the form of self-standing objects, and their connection initially appears as an inward bond or an external relation. Hegel states this as the opening determination of **mechanism** (*SL* §1539).

The achievement and its limitation are thus inseparable. Subjectivity has overcome the middle's externality to the extremes; the result stands immediately as an object. But **the Concept's own free relation to that objectivity** has yet to be developed. The opposition will return in a richer form, notably in the relation of end and object, before the Idea is reached (*SL* §1538). The Logic has not finished simply because the first whole has become self-standing.

Nor is this the Absolute Idea's **release into Nature**. We are still within the Logic, at the beginning of Objectivity. The present realization is a determinate result of the syllogism's development; the later release must be read on its own grounds when the Idea has completed its course. Treating them as the same crossing would erase the very argument still to be made.

## IX. What This Contributes to Scientific Method

**The middle is a question about explanation, not a demand for three-line presentation.** Finding a connection that licenses an inference and finding the determination through which the object is what it is are related but distinguishable achievements. A correlation may support prediction without identifying its ground; an account of the ground can in turn alter which correlations matter. The syllogism chapter supplies a way to ask whether the middle is an isolated feature, a gathered regularity, or an exhibited determining relation. It supplies no empirical answer on its own.

This develops §09's compressed comparison of syllogism and explanation. The transferable demand is to exhibit the appropriate mediation and its warrant, not to force every science into the same explanatory template.

**Induction and analogy are retained, not left behind as mistakes.** Their limits are limits relative to a claim of necessity or self-sufficient grounding. They do not erase the difference between a carefully supported expectation and a careless guess. Hegel's favorable assessment of analogy makes that preservation visible; our extension of the point is that probabilistic support must be assessed by its own standards, not treated as failed deductive certainty merely because it is not certainty. The chapter does not provide a theory of probability or decide a live problem of inference.

**A method can become part of the investigated content.** The figures begin by being used to mediate terms; their own premises and middle then become objects of investigation. That investigation changes the form of mediation itself. This gives a precise, local instance of the content–method relation with which the synopsis began in §01. It does not assign the sciences a sealed content-domain while reserving all reflection on method to a separate philosophical authority. The sciences can investigate, justify, and transform their own methods.

**Yet development of a method is not already the whole Logic.** A science can determine the adequacy of a particular construction, revise its classifications, or explain why one procedure works better than another. Those are substantive methodological achievements. Hegel's further question concerns the categorial forms through which such grounding and revision occur, and whether their development can itself be comprehended. The questions meet; neither is a substitute for the other's work. The mathematical discussion in §V exhibits precisely that relation without requiring either calculation or philosophy to surrender its proper achievement.

## X. Three Misreadings, Answered Directly

**“Dialectic improves an inference by allowing invalid conclusions.”** No. The restrictions of the figures matter: a singular conjunction warrants no unrestricted universal conclusion, and a common predicate does not identify its subjects. Hegel's investigation concerns what the form presupposes and what kind of unity its middle supplies. Where we distinguish a correct inference from an inadequate account of the concrete object, we do not make the former incorrect or exempt the latter from proof. A dialectical claim that depends on a fallacy has not been rescued by being dialectical.

**“Once a theory's claims mutually support one another, their circularity proves them.”** No. The circle of the figures reveals interdependence and motivates a different determination of the middle. It is not a rule for certifying mutually borrowed assumptions. The movement to necessity requires the relation to be grounded in the object's nature; changing the name of a circle to a system does not perform that work. Even an independently warranted formal system need not claim the self-grounding Hegel seeks.

**“A complete disjunction proves the existence of its object.”** No. Formal elimination requires premises; it does not produce their existential warrant. Hegel's move to objectivity concerns the developed Concept's overcoming of the distinction between an inward mediating unity and its independently standing terms. Rejecting or defending that movement requires confronting this larger claim, not attributing a magical productivity to *either–or*.

## XI. What the Method Did Here — Observations

**It made the instrument answer its own demand.** The syllogism requires mediation of a relation, then discovers that its premises are still immediate. The criterion is not imported from a preferred scientific model. It is the form's own requirement, turned upon the conditions of its operation.

**It transformed the middle rather than indefinitely extending the chain.** More premises reproduce the need for mediation; more instances reproduce the gap between collected singulars and the universal relation. In each case the advance changes the kind of unity required, rather than promising that sufficient repetition will eventually become grounding.

**It distinguished the negative result from the positive.** The figures' fixed distinctions disappear into quantitative equality, but their movement has also made each moment internally related to the others. The syllogism of reflection develops the latter result. Without that distinction, the mathematical figure would look like a detour to be discarded rather than a determinate abstraction whose truth and limit both survive.

**It let form acquire content without importing an arbitrary empirical content.** The shift from a lone quality to allness, from allness to the series of singulars, from that series to the kind-bearing instance, and from that instance to its determining nature is a specification of the middle. Hegel expressly defends this enrichment against the objection that logic should abstract from content (*SL* §1497). What is acquired is not a biological discovery supplied by logic; it is the determination of what would count as the mediation under investigation.

**It completed a form by sublating its external shape.** The last syllogism does not need a separately standing middle because the middle's own differentiation contains the extremes. Mediation is preserved in the immediate result. But the next beginning is not the final reconciliation: Objectivity still has its own development to undergo.

**§27's debt is partly discharged, not waved away.** Its test was whether the moments do the work, not whether the names are new. The recurrence is more than a repeated diagram: induction's attempted totality repairs the singular middle's inability to subsume, while analogy's kind-bearing singular exposes the need for a ground that bare co-membership cannot supply. These are reasons for changed forms, not just places in a triad. But the paired positive-and-negative character of the particular conclusion remains Hegel's premise, not a result established by our existential illustration; and this synopsis does not demonstrate that every recurring pattern is exhaustively necessary. As in §28, that is a limit of our demonstration, not evidence that Hegel omitted the argument. The treatment of necessity also presupposes the Concept's objective significance (§27). None of these bounds invalidates a warranted restricted inference or lets it certify the stronger systematic claim.

## XII. What Comes Next

**§30 — Mechanism.** The Concept's first objective realization: self-standing objects whose unity appears inward or externally imposed. The next task is to follow their process and the development of centrality, not to identify the category with one historical physical theory.

**Beyond.** Chemism and teleology; then Life, where the deferred warrant for an organism's own standard must be faced; then Cognition and the Absolute Idea. The release into Nature remains the destination of that longer course, not a conclusion to be anticipated at this threshold.

## Coda

The old logic can tell us whether a conclusion follows once the terms, relations, and rules have been fixed. **We retain that achievement**, but distinguish its preservation within Hegel's development from our further defence of bounded formal inquiry. His method does not authorize simply discarding achieved content; that is not yet a demonstration that his polemic has discarded it. Our defence asks what a fixed-form investigation can establish without making its abstraction the whole of reason. Hegel's further question remains: what happens when the form that mediates those terms is itself required to account for its mediation?

Then the middle ceases to be merely a place in a diagram. It becomes the problem around which the chapter turns. A property joins the extremes but leaves its own connections immediate; an instance brings their conjunction into view but leaves it contingent; an abstract universal joins them only by leaving their determinate difference out. Reflection gathers what was separated, then discovers that gathering cannot replace the determining nature it presupposes. Necessity makes that nature the middle and finally exhibits its differentiation in the terms themselves.

The result is not a more elaborate apparatus standing between a knower and an unchanged object. It is the Concept's claim to have given its unity an objective form. That claim has a derivation to be examined and limits not to be concealed. Its first result is not yet transparent to itself.

Subjectivity ends, and the object stands there. What it is to stand on its own is the next question.
