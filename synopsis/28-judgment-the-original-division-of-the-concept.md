# Judgment: The Original Division of the Concept

*The nineteenth installment of Section III — the **second chapter of Subjectivity**, taking up the debt §27 recorded. Judgment is not the combining of two ready-made concepts but the Concept's **own partition**: "**The judgment is the self-diremption of the Notion**," and the German word says so — "**It is thus the original division [Teilung] of what is originally one; thus the word Urteil refers to what judgment is in and for itself**" (*SL* §1349), the *Enc.* adding that the etymology "**goes deeper, as it were declaring the unity of the notion to be primary, and its distinction to be the original partition**" (*Enc.* §166). The whole received picture is thereby inverted: the school logic "**looks upon the subject as self-subsistent outside, and the predicate as found somewhere in our head**", and formal logic "**makes the judgment look as if it were something merely contingent, and does not offer any proof for the advance from notion on to judgment**" — whereas the Concept "**is rather an infinite form, of boundless activity, as it were the punctum saliens of all vitality, and thereby self-differentiating**", so that a plant's unclosing of root, branches and leaves "**is, as it were, the judgment of the plant**" (*Enc.* §166). Hence "**all things are a judgment**" (*Enc.* §167) — the objectivity claim of §27 §II carried into the form itself. Two boundaries are drawn before the development begins. A **judgment is not a proposition**: "**'Aristotle died at the age of 73, in the fourth year of the 115th Olympiad,' is a mere proposition, not a judgment**" (*SL* §1350), and neither is "**'I slept well last night' or 'Present arms!'**" (*Enc.* §167). And every judgment whatever, beneath its content, asserts "**the individual is universal**" (*SL* §1363) — an identity **asserted** by the copula and not yet sustained by its terms, and the engine of everything that follows. The **copula** carries the development: at first "**only by an abstract 'is'**", it is progressively filled until, "**through a copula charged with content**", the judgment "**comes to be a syllogism**" (*Enc.* §171). On that basis the inherited list of judgment-forms is refused as a list: "**Not only does the ordinary enumeration seem purely casual, but it is also superficial, and even bewildering**"; "**The various kinds of judgment are no empirical aggregate. They are a systematic whole based on a principle; and it was one of Kant's great merits to have first emphasised the necessity of showing this**" — Kant credited and convicted in one breath again, his table resting on "**the merely formal application of this categorical rubric**" yet on "**a true perception**" — and the ladder rises by an internal criterion: "**the different species of judgment form a series of steps, the difference of which rests upon the logical significance of the predicate**" (*Enc.* §171). Three kinds parallel to **Being, Essence, Notion**, the middle doubled because Essence "**is the stage of differentiation**" — hence four classes. The **judgment of existence** yields the corpus's sharpest instrument for the sciences, the **truth/correctness** distinction: "**Correctness, generally speaking, concerns only the formal coincidence between our conception and its content … Truth, on the contrary, lies in the coincidence of the object with itself, that is, with its notion**," so that "**a person is sick … may certainly be correct. But the content is untrue**" (*Enc.* §172). Negation proves **determinate** once more — "**in saying that the rose is not red, it is assumed that it has a colour, but a different one**" (*SL* §1376) — until the **infinite judgment** destroys the form by completing it: "**the rose is not an elephant, the understanding is not a table**" are "**correct or true, as the expression goes**" and yet "**not judgments at all**" (*SL* §1382), while **crime** and **death** are its objective instances — crime "**negates right as right**". Then reflection, necessity, and the **judgment of the Concept**, where the predicate becomes evaluative of necessity ("**This house is bad, this action is good**", *SL* §1422), where bare assurance meets its equal and opposite ("**the opposite assurance that 'this action is bad', is equally justified**", *SL* §1424), and where the **apodeictic** judgment — "**the consummation of the judgment**" (*SL* §1536) — restores the mismatch by putting the **constitution** into the subject, and so passes into the syllogism. Where this meets the sciences is set out without softening: what an observation-report can and cannot be, why classification by **principle** outranks classification by sensible quality, why the normative vocabulary of biology and medicine is not an intrusion of value into fact, and the resemblance — noted as resemblance, not influence — between the infinite judgment and the later doctrine of **category mistakes**. Three misreadings answered, and one debt only **partly** discharged: the classes are genuinely re-derived, but the inherited inventory still visibly governs which forms receive treatment, and that is recorded rather than explained away.*

## I. Where We Are: A Maturity That Is a Loss

§27 ended on a sentence that should be read as a piece of method rather than as rhetoric: the Concept's highest maturity is the stage at which its downfall begins. It is worth being precise about why, because the whole of this installment depends on it and because the point is easy to mistake for a pious paradox.

The Concept's three moments — universal, particular, singular — were not three items laid side by side. Each was the whole, determined differently. The universal was **free power** that keeps itself in its other; the particular was the universal's own self-diremption; the singular was the point at which the Concept, in Hegel's phrase quoted at §27, seizes itself — "the profundity in which the Notion seizes itself" (*SL* §1339). But precisely there a difficulty arises that no amount of care in stating the triad can avert. In singularity the Concept is **this** — determinate, exclusive, set off against what it is not. And a determination that excludes is a determination that stands over against something. The Concept, at the very point of grasping itself, becomes **external to itself**.

That externality is not imposed from outside by a logician who insists on writing propositions. It is what singularity is. And the form in which the Concept exists as external to itself, with its moments standing apart as self-subsistent and joined only by a connective, is **judgment**.

So the transition into this chapter has the shape the corpus has met at every major joint since §12: not a step taken because the exposition needs to continue, but a step the content takes because its own achievement undoes it. §13's true infinite was reached when the finite's own going-beyond proved to be its own return; §21's contradiction was reached when difference driven to its extreme collapsed; §26's reciprocity emptied itself and demanded the Concept. Here, what undoes the Concept's unity is the very determinacy in which that unity became actual.

Hegel states the result flatly. The judgment's sides are "**totalities which to begin with are essentially self-subsistent**", and their unity is "**at first only a relation of self-subsistents; not as yet the concrete and pregnant unity that has returned into itself from this reality, but only a unity outside which the self-subsistent sides persist as extremes that are not sublated in it**." (*SL* §1349)

Two things are worth marking in that sentence before anything else is said. First, **the extremes are totalities** — not fragments, not halves. Subject and predicate are each the whole Concept, which is why neither can be reduced to the other and why their relation will prove so hard to state. Second, the unity is "**only a relation**" — an external one. The Concept has become a **connection between its own moments**, which is exactly what it means for it to have become external to itself.

And the retrospect is immediate. Judgment is not merely the next form; it is what the earlier transitions were all along, now arrived at their truth:

> "In the sphere of being, the object becomes and others itself, the finite perishes or goes under in the infinite; in the sphere of Existence, the object issues from its ground into Appearance and falls to the ground, the accident manifests the wealth of substance as well as its power; in being, there is transition into an other, in essence, reflected being in an other by which the necessary relation is revealed. This movement of transition and reflection has now passed over into the original partition of the Notion which, while bringing back the individual to the in-itself of its universality, equally determines the universal as something actual." (*SL* §1353)

That is §13's circle applied to the two preceding Doctrines at once. Becoming, going-under, grounding, appearing, manifesting — the whole repertoire of transitions — are here recognized as imperfect anticipations of one movement: the Concept dividing itself and holding itself in the division.

## II. *Urteil*: Original Division, Not Combination

The received account of judgment is so entrenched that stating it feels like stating a truism. There is a subject, out in the world. There is a predicate, a general term, in the mind. Judging consists in bringing the second to the first. The copula records that the bringing has been done.

Hegel's objection is not that this account is useless. It is that it is an account **of the outside of judgment**, mistaken for its nature. He grants the appearance its due: "**But regarded from the side of externality, the Notion is present in the judgment as Appearance, since its moments therein attain self-subsistence, and it is on this external side that ordinary thinking tends to fasten.**" (*SL* §1349) The externality is real — it is the whole point of the chapter that the Concept has become external to itself — but it is the **result** of a division, not the starting condition of a combination.

The German word is his witness. *Urteil* — *Ur-Teil*, original division. "**It is thus the original division [Teilung] of what is originally one; thus the word Urteil refers to what judgment is in and for itself.**" (*SL* §1349) The *Encyclopaedia* puts it in one sentence that may be the cleanest statement of the doctrine anywhere in Hegel: the etymology "**goes deeper, as it were declaring the unity of the notion to be primary, and its distinction to be the original partition. And that is what the Judgment really is.**" (*Enc.* §166)

A caution is owed here, and the corpus's own rules require it. An etymology is not an argument. If the case for judgment-as-division rested on the accident that German *Urteil* can be read as *Ur-Teil*, it would rest on nothing; and it is a further complication that this reading is not the word's actual historical derivation but a philosophical re-reading current in Hegel's period and much older than him. Hegel's case does **not** rest on it. The etymology is offered as a confirmation — "as it were declaring" is deliberately hedged — of a result already reached from the content: the Concept's singularity is its self-externalization. What the word supplies is a mnemonic, and a reminder that ordinary language sometimes preserves what a technical tradition has lost. Read as evidence it proves nothing; read as a summary of an argument made elsewhere it is exact. This corpus takes it in the second way.

From the division-reading, three received doctrines fall at once.

**That judgments combine heterogeneous concepts.** Half-right, says Hegel: the theory "**is correct, so far as it implies that it is the notion which forms the presupposition of the judgment, and which in the judgment comes up under the form of difference. But on the other hand, it is false to speak of notions differing in kind.**" (*Enc.* §166) Subject and predicate are not two concepts of different species brought into contact; they are the one Concept in its two moments. This is why the account of §27 had to come first. If the universal were the common element and the singular a bare *this*, subject and predicate really would be heterogeneous, and their union really would be an external act.

**That the predicate is ascribed by us.** "**Language like this looks upon the subject as self-subsistent outside, and the predicate as found somewhere in our head.**" (*Enc.* §166) Against it, the plain deliverance of the copula: "**By saying 'This rose is red', or 'This picture is beautiful', we declare, that it is not we who from outside attach beauty to the picture or redness to the rose, but that these are the characteristics proper to these objects.**" (*Enc.* §166) The argument is modest and strong: whatever our theory of judgment, our practice of judging asserts objectivity, and a theory that cannot accommodate what judging says it is doing has misdescribed it.

**That judgment simply turns up.** This is the sharpest charge, and it belongs to the methodological thread this corpus has followed since §01. Formal logic, Hegel says, "**makes the judgment look as if it were something merely contingent, and does not offer any proof for the advance from notion on to judgment.**" (*Enc.* §166) The textbook has a chapter on concepts and then a chapter on judgments, and between them nothing but the table of contents. Why judgment at all? Why should a being that has concepts go on to judge? The school logic has no answer because it has no resources for one: its concepts are inert. Hegel's answer is that the Concept is not inert — "**It is rather an infinite form, of boundless activity, as it were the punctum saliens of all vitality, and thereby self-differentiating.**" (*Enc.* §166) *Punctum saliens* — the leaping point, the first pulsing speck in the embryo that the old embryology saw as the beginning of life. The image is chosen with care and it is the chapter's governing one: "**This disruption of the notion into the difference of its constituent functions — a disruption imposed by the native act of the notion — is the judgment.**" (*Enc.* §166)

Which yields the illustration that makes the doctrine graspable in a single stroke. The germ of a plant "**contains its particular, such as root, branches, leaves, etc.: but these details are at first present only potentially, and are not realised till the germ uncloses. This unclosing is, as it were, the judgment of the plant.**" (*Enc.* §166)

Take the image seriously and the inversion is complete. The plant does not judge; nothing here is psychological. What the plant does is **articulate itself into its own moments**, which were implicit in it and are now set out as distinct and relatively self-subsistent — root really is not leaf. That articulation is judgment's logical structure exhibited in nature. And this is precisely §27's claim — Hegel's own — that the Concept "constitutes a stage of nature as well as of spirit" (*SL* §1299), now cashed out in a particular form rather than asserted. Hence the generalization: "**all things are a judgment. That is to say, they are individuals which are a universality or inner nature in themselves — a universal which is individualised.**" (*Enc.* §167)

The corpus has met this pattern before and should name it. §24's law was not our summary of appearances but appearance's own stable content; §25's actuality was not our modal notion but the thing's own unity of inner and outer. Here judgment is not our act but the thing's own self-articulation. Each time, a category the tradition had lodged in the knowing subject is shown to be a determination of the object — and each time, without denying that we also perform the corresponding act. The logic does not deny that people judge. It denies that judging is intelligible as a merely subjective performance.

## III. Judgment Is Not Proposition

Before the development can begin, a boundary has to be drawn that the school logic draws badly or not at all, and that matters more for the sciences than its brevity in the text suggests.

Grammatical form is not logical form. "**We may take this opportunity of remarking, too, that though a proposition has a subject and predicate in the grammatical sense, this does not make it a judgment. The latter requires that the predicate be related to the subject as one Notion determination to another, and therefore as a universal to a particular or individual.**" (*SL* §1350)

The examples are unusually concrete for Hegel, and both translations supply them. From Miller: "**'Aristotle died at the age of 73, in the fourth year of the 115th Olympiad,' is a mere proposition, not a judgment.**" (*SL* §1350) From Wallace: "**'Caesar was born at Rome in such and such a year waged war in Gaul for ten years, crossed the Rubicon, etc.', are propositions, but not judgments**," and — the case that shows the criterion is not about grandeur of subject matter — "**it is absurd to say that such statements as 'I slept well last night' or 'Present arms!' maybe turned into the form of a judgment.**" (*Enc.* §167)

What is the criterion? Not importance, and not truth. "Aristotle died at 73" is the sort of claim evidence settles either way, and historically weighty besides — and as it happens the figure is wrong, since the conventional dates give about sixty-two, which changes nothing whatever about its logical form. It is not a judgment because its predicate does not stand to its subject as **universal to singular**; it records a singular circumstance of a singular subject. Nothing universal is at stake, so nothing is being *divided* — and where there is no division there is no *Urteil*.

But the same words can become a judgment, and Hegel's account of how is the interesting part: "**It would partake of the nature of a judgment only if doubt had been thrown on one of the circumstances, the date of the death, or the age of that philosopher, and the given figures had been asserted on the strength of some reason or other. In that case, these figures would be taken as something universal, as time that still subsists apart from this particular content of the death of Aristotle.**" (*SL* §1350) And the domestic version: "**the news that my friend N. has died is a proposition; and it would be a judgment only if there were a question whether he was really dead or only in a state of catalepsy.**" (*SL* §1350)

This deserves more weight than it usually gets. The transformation from proposition to judgment happens when the content is **put in question and asserted on grounds** — when the date is no longer merely reported but maintained against a rival date, so that "the fourth year of the 115th Olympiad" functions as a determinate position in a universal order of time rather than as a fact adhering to Aristotle. The same sentence is a proposition in a chronicle and a judgment in a disputed chronology.

Two consequences follow, and both bear directly on the corpus's question about scientific method.

First, **the protocol sentence is not the atom of science.** Much twentieth-century philosophy of science tried to build knowledge from basic observation reports — "the needle reads 4.7", "the counter registered 217 at 11:03" — treated as the secure minimum. Hegel's distinction says something sharp about that programme without needing to anticipate it. Such a sentence, taken strictly as the record of a singular occurrence, is functioning as a proposition — it registers that this happened, as a chronicle registers a date. It is a judgment insofar as its predicate is taken as a universal: the reading referred to the scale, the instrument's theory, the kind of thing being sampled, and thereby made contestable and groundable. Hegel is explicit that even the humblest perceptual ascription is already a judgment, if a poor one — "This wall is green" is his own example (*Enc.* §171, quoted below) — so the claim is not that reports fall outside judgment altogether. It is that the supposedly secure minimum is secure only in proportion as it is *not yet* doing the epistemic work required of it. What makes a measurement evidence is exactly what makes it contestable. This is not a refutation of empiricism; it is a reason to expect the difficulties §03 traced.

Second, **the sciences do not consist of catalogues.** A table of observations, read as a register of singular occurrences, is a set of propositions. It becomes science when its entries are read as instances of universals — when "this specimen has such a bone structure" is taken as "this specimen is a mammal", with all the commitments that carries. The passage from data to science is the passage from proposition to judgment, and it is not a passage of accumulation.

## IV. The Copula, and the Contradiction Every Judgment Contains

Now the engine. Every judgment whatever, Hegel claims, beneath whatever content it carries, asserts one thing:

> "The immediate pure enunciation of the positive judgment is, therefore, the proposition: the individual is universal. This enunciation must not be put in the form: A is B; for A and B are entirely formless and consequently meaningless names; the judgment as such, however, and therefore even the judgment of existence, has Notion determinations for its extremes. A is B can represent any mere proposition just as well as a judgment." (*SL* §1363)

The parenthetical attack on "A is B" is worth pausing over, because §27 expressly left it to this chapter. There the dismissal of the calculus was split in two: taken as counsel against pursuing formalization it was wrong, but the categorial objection — that signs so used no longer have **Notions** for their objects — was not touched by the later fertility of formal methods. The objection raised here is the categorial one, and this is the common object §27 promised. It is not the complaint that the letters are uninterpreted; that is notation's virtue, and taken as counsel it would fail a second time. It is that schematizing judgment as "A is B" hides that the two places are **not symmetrical**. The subject place is the place of singularity, the predicate place the place of universality, and the difference between them is not notational. On that ground the objection holds. What it does not establish is the general dismissal, since a formal logic can perfectly well type its places — and once it does, the objection bears on the untyped schema rather than on formalization as such.

And now the difficulty that drives everything. The positive judgment says *the singular is the universal*. But the singular **is not** the universal — that is what it is to be singular. The judgment's form asserts an identity that its own terms refuse. Hegel does not treat this as a defect in particular judgments to be repaired by choosing better ones; it is constitutive. "This rose is red" says that a concrete thing, with an odour, a shape, a history, and much else, **is** an abstract universal which belongs equally to blood and to bricks. The *Encyclopaedia* gives the image: "**The subject and predicate in the immediate judgment touch, as it were, only in a single point, but do not cover each other.**" (*Enc.* §172)

Where is this mismatch registered? In the **copula** — and the copula is therefore where the whole development is lodged. At the outset it is the thinnest thing imaginable: "**As the two Notion determinations are determined, so also is their relation, the is or copula; it too can only have the significance of an immediate, abstract being. On account of the relation which as yet contains no mediation or negation, this judgment is called the positive.**" (*SL* §1362) The *Encyclopaedia*: the copula "**even while stating the identity of subject and predicate, does so at first only by an abstract 'is'.**" (*Enc.* §171)

But the copula is not a mere sign. "**The copula 'is' springs from the nature of the notion, to be self-identical even in parting with its own.**" (*Enc.* §166) That is the Concept's definition from §27 — the universal as free power that remains itself in its other — restated as the meaning of the word *is*. The copula is the Concept's self-identity **surviving its own division**. Which is why the mismatch cannot be left standing and why it cannot be cured by abandoning judgment: the copula already asserts what the terms cannot yet sustain.

The resolution, therefore, is not to find judgments in which subject and predicate happen to match. It is for the copula to be progressively **filled** — for the bare *is* to be replaced by a connection that actually exhibits how this singular is this universal. Hegel's statement of the programme is the hinge of the chapter:

> "Conformably to such an identity the subject has to be put also in the characteristic of the predicate. By this means the latter also receives the characteristic of the former: so that the copula receives its full complement and full force. Such is the continuous specification by which the judgment, through a copula charged with content, comes to be a syllogism." (*Enc.* §171)

A copula charged with content **is** a middle term. When the bare *is* of "this action is good" has been filled out into "this action, being of such and such a constitution, and such constitution being what makes actions good, is good", the copula has become a **reason**, and the judgment has become a syllogism. The chapter's destination is fixed in advance by this sentence, and the four classes are the stages of the filling.

The corpus should note the formal resemblance to §22. There, the immediate relation of ground and grounded proved unstable until the ground was determined as **real** ground, then as **complete** ground, and finally determined itself as **conditioning mediation**, the connective acquiring content at each step. Here the same shape governs the copula. This is not repetition: Essence's relations were between a thing and its other, the Concept's are between the Concept's own moments. But the method is visibly the same instrument, which is part of what §13 claimed.

## V. Why the List Is a Ladder

Here the debt §27 recorded comes due. What that chapter left explicitly outstanding was whether the three moments **do the work** the rest of the Subjective Logic asks of them — the complaint being that a derivation arriving at exactly three moments bearing exactly the traditional names has arranged its own destination. This chapter is the first instalment of the answer, and the answer is affirmative: the three moments do the work, generating the classes of judgment and driving each form into its successor, as §IV below exhibits in detail. A second and narrower question was previewed rather than booked there, and it is convenient to settle it here as well. It concerns the **inventory**: even if the terms are re-derived, is not the *list* of judgment-forms simply inherited, with a speculative commentary attached?

Hegel's answer is the most explicitly methodological passage in the chapter, and it begins by conceding the complaint against his predecessors in full:

> "After we are made aware of this continuous specification of the judgment, we can see a meaning and an interconnection in what are usually stated as the kinds of judgment. Not only does the ordinary enumeration seem purely casual, but it is also superficial, and even bewildering in its statement of their distinctions. The distinction between positive, categorical, and assertory judgments is either a pure invention of fancy, or is left undetermined. On the right theory, the different judgments follow necessarily from one another, and present the continuous specification of the notion; for the judgment itself is nothing but the notion specified." (*Enc.* §171)

The example chosen is well chosen. Positive, categorical, and assertoric are, in the textbook tables, three headings drawn from three different columns — quality, relation, modality. On the textbook's own terms there is no way to say how they are related, because there is no principle ordering the columns. They are "either a pure invention of fancy, or … left undetermined."

Then the general thesis:

> "The various kinds of judgment are no empirical aggregate. They are a systematic whole based on a principle; and it was one of Kant's great merits to have first emphasised the necessity of showing this." (*Enc.* §171)

Note carefully what is credited. Not Kant's table — Kant's **demand**: that the forms of judgment be shown to form a system rather than a collection. This is exactly the structure of §27's treatment of apperception, and of its verdict on the critical philosophy: the decisive insight is granted without reserve, and the execution is then found wanting by the insight's own standard. The verdict follows at once. Kant's division "**cannot be called satisfactory, partly from the merely formal application of this categorical rubric, partly on account of their content. Still it rests upon a true perception of the fact that the different species of judgment derive their features from the universal forms of the logical idea itself.**" (*Enc.* §171)

**Merely formal application** is the precise charge, and it is §27's charge restated: Kant announced that the table must be systematic and then took it over from the logic of his day, arranging it under four headings whose own necessity he did not derive. The demand was right; the discharge was borrowing.

What replaces it is an **internal criterion**, and this single clause is the chapter's methodological core:

> "Far from occupying the same level, and being of equal value, the different species of judgment form a series of steps, the difference of which rests upon the logical significance of the predicate." (*Enc.* §171)

Judgments are **ranked**, and ranked by what kind of universal the predicate is. That is a criterion internal to judgment itself, not imported from a table of categories; and it immediately yields the architecture:

> "When we look at the two preceding spheres of Being and Essence, we see that the specified notions as judgments are reproductions of these spheres, but put in the simplicity of relation peculiar to the notion. … If we follow this clue, it will supply us with three chief kinds of judgment parallel to the stages of Being, Essence, and Notion. The second of these kinds, as required by the character of Essence, which is the stage of differentiation, must be doubled." (*Enc.* §171)

Three kinds, the middle doubled — hence **four classes**: the judgment of **existence** (Being), the judgments of **reflection** and of **necessity** (Essence, doubled because Essence is the sphere of differentiation), and the judgment of the **Concept**. The whole prior Logic is thus reproduced inside one of its own late forms. That is §13's circle again, and it is the strongest available answer to the charge of borrowing: the classes are not four items on a list but the three spheres of the Logic re-enacted in the medium of judgment.

Hegel then offers a test that anyone can apply, and it is a good one because it appeals to a discrimination we already make:

> "That judgments differ in value is evident in our ordinary ways of thinking. We should not hesitate to ascribe a very slight faculty of judgment to a person who habitually framed only such judgments as 'This wall is green', 'This stove is hot'. On the other hand we should credit with a genuine capacity of judgment the person whose criticisms dealt with such questions as whether a certain work of art was beautiful, whether a certain action was good, and so on. In judgments of the first-mentioned kind the content forms only an abstract quality, the presence of which can be sufficiently detected by immediate perception. To pronounce a work of art to be beautiful, or an action to be good, requires on the contrary a comparison of the objects with what they ought to be, i.e. with their notion." (*Enc.* §171)

The appeal is to ordinary usage — we really do distinguish someone who can report from someone who can judge — and the analysis of the difference is the criterion just stated: in the first case the predicate is an abstract sensible quality detectable by perception; in the second it is the **concept of the thing**, against which the thing is measured. The ladder runs from predicates that require only a look to predicates that require the thing's own standard.

**How much of the debt does this pay?** Honestly: most of it, and not all. The derivation of the four classes from Being–Essence–Concept is genuinely internal, and the criterion — the logical significance of the predicate — is genuinely a criterion rather than a rubric. That is a real achievement and it is more than Kant offered. But the inherited inventory still visibly governs the exposition. The forms treated under each class are, with few exceptions, the forms the textbooks treated: positive, negative, infinite; singular, particular, universal; categorical, hypothetical, disjunctive; assertoric, problematic, apodeictic. Hegel re-orders them and re-motivates each transition, and in several cases — the infinite judgment above all — he gives a form that was a curiosity a serious function. But he does not derive the *existence* of these particular forms from the Concept without reference to what the tradition handed him; the derivation shows why there must be a graded series and what governs the grading, and then finds the tradition's forms fitting the grades. That is a re-derivation of a received list, and it is a substantial thing, but it is not the same as generating the list from nothing. The corpus records this as the honest residue, in the same spirit in which §27 recorded that Hegel's dismissal of a logical calculus was not borne out.

## VI. The Judgment of Existence

The first class takes the copula at face value: the bare *is*, an immediate universal for predicate, an immediate singular for subject. Its three forms exhaust what can happen to a judgment whose predicate is a sensible quality.

### (a) Positive

"The individual is a particular" (*Enc.* §172) — or in Miller's rendering of the same thought, the individual is universal. "This rose is red." And the mismatch is there from the first: "**But the individual is not a particular: or in more precise language, such a single quality is not congruous with the concrete nature of the subject.**" (*Enc.* §172) The rose "**is a concrete thing, and so is not red only: it has also an odour, a specific form, and many other features not implied in the predicate red. The predicate on its part is an abstract universal, and does not apply to the rose alone.**" (*Enc.* §172)

Here the corpus reaches what may be the single most useful thing in the chapter for its own governing question.

> "It is one of the fundamental assumptions of dogmatic Logic that Qualitative judgments such as 'The rose is red' or 'is not red' can contain truth. Correct they may be, i.e. in the limited circle of perception, of finite conception and thought: that depends on the content, which likewise is finite, and, on its own merits, untrue. Truth, however, as opposed to correctness, depends solely on the form, viz. on the notion as it is put and the reality corresponding to it. … Correctness, generally speaking, concerns only the formal coincidence between our conception and its content, whatever the constitution of this content may be. Truth, on the contrary, lies in the coincidence of the object with itself, that is, with its notion." (*Enc.* §172)

The distinction must be stated carefully or it becomes an equivocation, and Hegel warns of exactly that: "**In common life the terms truth and correctness are often treated as synonymous: we speak of the truth of a content, when we are only thinking of its correctness.**" (*Enc.* §172)

**Correctness** is the ordinary property: the statement agrees with how things are. Nothing in Hegel disparages it, and nothing in this corpus does either — a false measurement is not redeemed by profundity. **Truth**, in his technical sense, is not a relation between statement and world at all; it is a relation of a thing **to its own concept**. A thing is true insofar as it is what it ought to be — a true friend, a true work of art, in the ordinary idiom he is relying on.

The examples make it concrete and also make it uncomfortable: "**That a person is sick, or that some one has committed a theft, may certainly be correct. But the content is untrue. A sick body is not in harmony with the notion of body, and there is a want of congruity between theft and the notion of human conduct.**" (*Enc.* §172)

Two misreadings must be blocked at once, because both are common.

It is **not** a claim that "this person is sick" might be false when the person is sick. Hegel says explicitly that it "may certainly be correct". The judgment is correct; the *content* — the sick body — is untrue, that is, out of accord with what a body is.

And it is **not** a licence to call whatever one dislikes "untrue". The standard invoked is not the speaker's preference but the object's own concept, and §27's discipline applies with full force: a concept is not a list of marks assembled by a classifier but a principle containing the thing's own development. Where no such concept is available, the vocabulary of truth-as-adequacy has nothing to attach to and should not be used.

Within those limits the distinction does real work, and the sciences use it constantly without a theory of it. Physiology distinguishes function from malfunction; medicine distinguishes health from pathology; engineering distinguishes a working mechanism from a failed one. None of these is a distinction between correct and incorrect descriptions — the description of a diseased organ can be impeccably correct. They are distinctions between a thing's accord and discord **with what it is**. A physician who says a heart is defective is not reporting a preference and is not merely describing; the judgment presupposes a standard internal to the organ. Empiricism cannot derive such standards from observation, and the reason is worth stating plainly, since the corpus has not had occasion to state it before: no accumulation of *is* yields an *ought to be*. Hegel's answer is that the standard is not added to the object but is the object's own concept, and that a logic which cannot state this has failed to describe what the sciences actually do. Whether the answer succeeds depends on whether the concept of an organism really is the organism's own, and that question belongs to the Doctrine of the Idea, where Life is treated; it is flagged here, not settled.

### (b) Negative

The mismatch of the positive judgment is not left to be noticed by us. The judgment states it: since the singular is not the abstract universal, the positive judgment's own content demands its negation.

But negation here is not annihilation, and the corpus should recognize the instrument. "**Thus the negative judgment is not total negation; the universal sphere which contains the predicate still subsists, and therefore the relation of the subject to the predicate is essentially still positive.**" (*SL* §1376) The example, in both texts: "**in saying that the rose is not red, it is assumed that it has a colour, but a different one. In respect of this universal sphere the judgment is still positive.**" (*SL* §1376) Wallace adds the consequence — the negation "**still leaves the connection of the subject with the predicate subsisting. The predicate is in that manner a sort of relative universal, of which a special phase only has been negatived.**" (*Enc.* §173)

This is **determinate negation** (§11, §21) operating in the theory of judgment, and it produces a genuine advance rather than a blank. Denying *red* does not return us to nothing; it returns us to *colour*, with a determinate gap where red was. The predicate has been raised from a specific quality to a sphere with an unspecified difference in it — which is to say the predicate is no longer merely universal but **particular**, a universal with determinateness in it. Hegel notes the gain: "not only is the universal sphere left but determinateness, too, is preserved, though converted into an indeterminate" one (*SL* §1377). The negative judgment is thus the mediating step toward reflection.

Hegel also disposes of a long scholastic argument in a sentence: "**It was, therefore, unnecessary for logicians to make such a fuss over the not of the negative judgment being attached to the copula.**" (*SL* §1374) Whether one writes "the rose is not-red" or "the rose is-not red" is immaterial, because in a judgment a determination of an extreme is already a determinate relation. The two readings do not differ in content. The dispute was an artifact of treating the copula as a separate joint.

### (c) Infinite

Now the form that the school logic kept as a curiosity and that Hegel makes into a turning point. The step is not arbitrary, and it repays seeing why it is compelled. The negative judgment left one positive relation standing: the rose that is not red is still somewhere within the sphere of *colour*. But that surviving sphere is a **worse** predicate for an individual than the determinate one it displaced, precisely because it is wider — the individual "**is not an undetermined determinate, but the determined determinate**", and "**the rose is not a thing of some colour or other, but has only the specific colour that is rose-colour**" (*SL* §1379). Hegel draws the consequence exactly: the negative judgment retained the universal sphere of the predicate, and from this side "**contained a universality more purged of limitation than the positive judgment, and for that reason must be all the more negated of the subject as an individual**" (*SL* §1381). Width is not safety; it is emptiness. Hence: "**In this manner, the whole extent of the predicate is negated and there is no longer any positive relation between it and the subject. This is the infinite judgment.**" (*SL* §1381)

The examples are deliberately ridiculous: "**spirit is not red, yellow, etc., is not acid, not alkaline, etc., the rose is not an elephant, the understanding is not a table**" (*SL* §1382); Wallace's "**'The mind is no elephant'; 'A lion is no table'**" (*Enc.* §173). And the verdict is exact:

> "These judgments are correct or true, as the expression goes, but in spite of such truth they are nonsensical and absurd. Or rather, they are not judgments at all." (*SL* §1382)

Here the truth/correctness distinction pays off immediately. A mind is indeed not an elephant; the statement cannot be faulted for correctness. But it is not a judgment, because judgment requires subject and predicate to share a universal sphere within which something is asserted, and here there is none. "**It is supposed to be a judgment, and consequently to contain a relation of subject and predicate; yet at the same time such a relation is supposed not to be in it.**" (*SL* §1382) The form has destroyed itself by being carried out completely. Wallace notes what the tradition did with this: "**The negatively infinite judgment, in which the subject has no relation whatever to the predicate, gets its place in the Formal Logic solely as a nonsensical curiosity.**" (*Enc.* §173)

And Hegel's counter-claim: it is nothing of the kind. "**But the infinite judgment is not really a mere casual form adopted by subjective thought. It exhibits the proximate result of the dialectical process in the immediate judgments preceding (the positive and simply-negative), and distinctly displays their finitude and untruth.**" (*Enc.* §173) The absurd form is the necessary outcome of the sound-looking ones, and it is how they show what was wrong with them.

The objective instances are what make this more than a technicality, and Hegel gives two.

**Crime.** "**In civil litigation, something is negated only as the property of the other party, it being conceded that it should be theirs if they had the right to it; and it is only the title of right that is in dispute; the universal sphere of right is therefore recognised and maintained in that negative judgment. But crime is the infinite judgment which negates not merely the particular right, but the universal sphere as well, negates right as right.**" (*SL* §1382) Wallace draws the legal consequence: the thief "**denies the right of that person in general, and therefore he is not merely forced to restore what he has stolen, but is punished in addition, because he has violated law as law, i.e. law in general.**" (*Enc.* §173) The civil suit is a negative judgment — this thing is not yours, but the sphere of right stands. Crime is an infinite judgment — and the doubling of the response, restitution *plus* punishment, tracks the doubling of the negation exactly. Whatever one thinks of the theory of punishment that follows from it, the logical analysis is doing visible work: a structural difference in the form of negation is offered as the ground of a difference in legal response.

**Death.** "**Similarly death, as a negatively infinite judgment, is distinguished from disease as simply-negative. In disease, merely this or that function of life is checked or negatived: in death, as we ordinarily say, body and soul part, i.e. subject and predicate utterly diverge.**" (*Enc.* §173) Disease negates a determination within life's sphere; death negates the sphere. The living body is the standing judgment "this matter is alive"; death is that judgment's infinite negation, subject and predicate falling apart.

**The positive moment.** Hegel does not leave the infinite judgment at mere collapse, and the step he takes next is the one the whole transition turns on. The negatively infinite judgment is a negation of a negation, and like every such negation since §11 it has a positive result: "**The positive moment of the infinite judgment, of the negation of the negation, is the reflection of individuality into itself, whereby it is posited for the first time as a determinate determinateness. According to that reflection, the expression of the judgment was: the individual is individual.**" (*SL* §1383) And symmetrically: "**The positively infinite judgment equally runs: the universal is universal, and as such is equally posited as the return into itself.**" (*SL* §1384) The subject that entered as a bare *this* leaves as a genuine individual; the predicate that entered as an immediate quality leaves as a universal comprehending distinct terms — each driven back into itself by the very failure of the relation between them. But neither "the individual is individual" nor "the universal is universal" is a judgment any longer: "**in the negatively infinite judgment the difference is, so to speak, too great for it to remain a judgment … in the positively infinite judgment, on the contrary, only identity is present and owing to the complete lack of difference it is no longer a judgment.**" (*SL* §1385) The form fails twice over — once from too much difference, once from too little — and it is this double failure, not the negative one alone, that exhausts the judgment of existence.

With this the judgment of existence has consumed itself, and what it leaves behind is not nothing but terms that have been driven into themselves. "**More precisely, it is the judgment of existence that has sublated itself; hereby there is posited what the copula of the judgment contains, namely, that the qualitative extremes are sublated in this their identity. Since however this unity is the Notion, it is immediately sundered again into its extremes and appears as a judgment, whose terms however are no longer immediate but reflected into themselves. The judgment of existence has passed over into the judgment of reflection.**" (*SL* §1386) The name of the next class is therefore a result and not a label: the judgment of **reflection** is so called because its terms are the terms of the judgment of existence **reflected into themselves**.

## VII. Reflection, Necessity, and the Judgment of the Concept

The remaining three classes are treated here more briefly than the first, in proportion to what they add to the corpus's governing question rather than to their length in Hegel's text. The criterion is constant throughout: **what kind of universal is the predicate?**

**The judgment of reflection.** The predicate is no longer a sensible quality but a determination of **relation** — useful, harmful, dangerous, fit for a purpose. "This plant is medicinal" does not report a look; it relates the plant to something else. The subject correspondingly ceases to be a bare *this* and is taken in its extent: **singular** ("this thing is useful"), **particular** ("some things are useful"), **universal** or of allness ("all men are mortal"). Hegel's note on the particular judgment is characteristic and worth keeping: "**The judgment: some men are happy, involves the immediate consequence that some men are not happy.**" (*SL* §1395) Positive and negative no longer fall apart; the particular judgment is both at once, which is why it is unstable and drives on to allness.

And allness, reached by enumeration, is the weakest form of universality — the **common element** §27 called the lowest possible conception of the universal. "All men are mortal" as an all-ness judgment rests on the men there happen to be. That is precisely §03's problem of induction, arrived at from the side of the predicate rather than from the side of inference: a universal collected from instances is hostage to the instances. The remedy is the passage to **genus** — from *all* to *the kind* — and that passage is the transition to the judgment of necessity.

**The judgment of necessity.** The predicate is now the subject's **substantial nature**: genus and species. **Categorical** — "gold is a metal" — where the predicate is the subject's own kind, not a collected commonality. Then **hypothetical** — the necessity externalized as dependence between two existences — and **disjunctive**: "A is either B or C or D", where the genus is exhibited as the totality of its species, and where the universal is at last present *as* the complete particularization of itself. The disjunctive judgment is the high point of the objective universality the sciences seek in a classification: not a list of what has been found, but a division that claims to exhaust the kind.

**The judgment of the Concept.** And here the predicate becomes the thing's **concept**, against which the thing is measured — the point already announced in §171's test of the wall and the work of art. The forms are three.

*Assertoric*: "**The subject is a concrete individual in general, and the predicate expresses this same as the relation of its actuality, determinateness, or constitution to its Notion. (This house is bad, this action is good.)**" (*SL* §1422) The subject "**ought to be something**"; the predicate says whether it is.

But asserted flatly, this judgment has no ground in itself, and Hegel presses the point without mercy: "**Accordingly, the assurance of the assertoric judgment is confronted with equal right by its contradictory. When one is assured that 'this action is good', then the opposite assurance that 'this action is bad', is equally justified.**" (*SL* §1424) Bare assurance meets bare assurance. Why? "**because the subject of the judgment is an immediate individual, in this abstraction it does not as yet possess posited within it the determinateness that should contain its relation to the universal Notion; thus the subject is still something contingent which may or may not conform to the Notion.**" (*SL* §1424) The subject was named but not characterized; nothing in "this action" says why it should be good.

*Problematic*: which is simply that recognized — "**The judgment is therefore essentially problematic.**" (*SL* §1424) The *ought* and the *is* have come apart, and their coincidence is left contingent.

*Apodeictic*: and the repair is not a stronger assertion but the **restoration of the missing content to the subject**. "**The subject of the apodeictic judgment (the house constituted so and so is good, the action constituted so and so is right) has within it, first, the universal, what it ought to be, and secondly, its constitution; this latter contains the ground why a predicate of the Notion judgement applies or does not apply to the whole subject, that is, whether the subject corresponds to its Notion or not.**" (*SL* §1430)

This is the chapter's destination and it should be read slowly. The subject is no longer a bare *this* but a *this so constituted*; it carries within itself both its concept (what it ought to be) and its constitution (how it actually is). The copula is now anything but abstract — it states that the constitution **grounds** the predicate, and *ground* is Hegel's own word for what the copula has become: it has "**now further developed itself into ground in general**" (*SL* §1433), which is why the resemblance to §22 remarked above is more than a loose analogy. The predicate has developed to match, being no longer the genus or the ought-to-be taken by itself but "**this correspondence that is the universality which constitutes the predicate of the apodeictic judgment**" (*SL* §1431). Judgment has become, in Hegel's phrase from a later retrospect, "**the apodeictic judgment — the consummation of the judgment**" (*SL* §1536).

And in consummating itself it ceases to be judgment. If the subject already contains the universal, the particular constitution, and the singular thing, then the "division" that judgment was has been undone from within — the extremes are no longer external to one another, because the middle by which they connect is now present. A judgment whose subject contains its own ground is a **syllogism** written on one line. The copula charged with content, promised in §171, has arrived; and what it has become is a **middle term**.

## VIII. Where This Meets the Sciences

Four places, stated with the same care about limits the corpus has used since §01.

**1. Observation reports and the myth of the secure minimum.** §III's distinction between proposition and judgment bears directly on the programme of founding science on basic observation statements. A report does the work of a judgment only insofar as it is referred to a universal and can thereby be grounded and contested. So the very feature that would make a report incorrigible — its being a bare registration, committed to nothing beyond itself — is what would disqualify it from functioning as evidence for anything. This does not show that the programme failed; the history of that programme shows it failed. It gives a reason, from the side of logical form, why the difficulty was structural and not an accident of how the programme was carried out.

**2. The ranking of predicates, and what classification is for.** Judgments form "a series of steps, the difference of which rests upon the logical significance of the predicate" (*Enc.* §171). A taxonomy resting on sensible marks is operating at the level of the judgment of existence; one resting on collected commonalities is at the level of reflection; one that exhibits a genus exhaustively divided into its species is at the level of necessity. That is a recognizable description of the movement from artificial to natural classification — from sorting by convenient characters to dividing by what makes the kinds the kinds. §27's contrast of **mark** and **principle** is here given its form in judgment. What the Logic supplies is the *form* of such an ascent and a criterion for recognizing where one stands on it. It supplies no taxonomy, decides no case, and cannot tell a biologist which characters are the principled ones. That is empirical work and the guardrail of §27 stands.

**3. Normative predicates in the sciences.** The judgment of the Concept is the form of "this heart is defective", "this specimen is malformed", "this proof is invalid", "this instrument is miscalibrated". Each measures a thing against its concept, and none is a report of a sensible quality. The dominant modern account treats such predicates as either disguised descriptions or smuggled values. Hegel's account is a third option: they are the highest form of judgment, and the standard is the object's own. The corpus notes two things about this. It is a genuine alternative, and it corresponds to what practitioners take themselves to be doing. And it is only as good as the availability of the relevant concept, which is why it is far more secure for a manufactured artifact or a proof than for an organism, and why the disputes about function and malfunction in the philosophy of biology are exactly where one would expect them to be. Hegel's own warrant for organisms comes later, in Life; here the form is established and the warrant is deferred.

**4. The infinite judgment and category mistakes.** The infinite judgment "the understanding is not a table" is correct and yet not a judgment, because subject and predicate share no universal sphere. A twentieth-century reader will think of the doctrine of category mistakes — the claim that certain grammatically impeccable sentences are not false but ill-formed, because their terms belong to different categories. The resemblance is real and worth registering: both hold that grammatical propriety does not guarantee logical propriety, and both locate the defect in a mismatch of spheres rather than in falsity. The corpus records it as a resemblance and nothing more. This is a structural parallel noticed in retrospect; no claim of influence is made here, and the surrounding doctrines differ sharply — Hegel's infinite judgment is a *necessary result* of a dialectical development and has objective instances in crime and death, which is no part of the later doctrine at all.

## IX. Three Misreadings, Answered Directly

**"Hegel derives Kant's table of judgments."** He does not, and says so. Kant's division "cannot be called satisfactory". What is credited is the demand for system; what is supplied is a different principle of division — the logical significance of the predicate — yielding four classes parallel to Being, Essence (doubled), and the Concept, not Kant's four headings. The forms treated within the classes are largely the inherited ones, and §V records that residue honestly. But the architecture is not Kant's.

**"The truth/correctness distinction licenses calling inconvenient facts untrue."** It does not, and the text forecloses it. "That a person is sick … may certainly be correct." Correctness is never denied to a correct statement. Truth in the technical sense is a property of the **object** in relation to its concept; and where Hegel does predicate it of a judgment, he is grading the judgment's *form* — whether that form is adequate to what it purports to express — which is why he can call the positive and the negative judgment alike untrue ("**The negative judgment is as little a true judgment as the positive**", *SL* §1382) and the apodeictic "**the truth of the judgment in general**" (*SL* §1431). What the distinction never does is re-rank a correct statement as a worse report of fact; and the concept in question is the object's own, subject to §27's insistence that a concept is a principle of development and not a classifier's preference. Where no such concept can be shown, the distinction has no application and invoking it is bluff.

**"Judgment is a mental act, so this is psychology after all."** This is §27's misreading in a new place and has the same answer. The plant's unclosing is judgment; crime and death are judgments; "all things are a judgment". The claim is about a logical structure that things exhibit and that thinking, being one such thing, also performs. Hegel's actual complaint against the tradition runs the other way: treating judgment as merely our act is what left formal logic unable to say why judgment occurs at all.

## X. What the Method Did Here — Observations

**The transition was suffered, not applied.** Nothing external moved the Concept into judgment. Singularity — the Concept's own culmination — is self-externalization, and judgment is what that externalization is. As at §14, §21, and §26, the form's success is what breaks it.

**Negation stayed determinate, and paid.** The negative judgment does not annul the positive; it preserves the universal sphere and converts a specific predicate into a sphere-with-a-gap, which is to say into a particular. §11's principle is not a slogan here — it is what generates the next form rather than a blank.

**Completion, not failure, produced the crisis.** The infinite judgment is not a judgment that goes wrong. It is negation carried out *fully*, and carrying it out fully abolishes the form. This is the same figure as §13's bad infinite and §21's contradiction: the point where a determination, pressed to its limit by its own logic, ceases to be able to be what it is.

**The criterion of advance was internal and single** — "the logical significance of the predicate" (*Enc.* §171). One criterion, drawn from judgment's own structure, ranks every form in the chapter. Compare the school logic's four independent rubrics, under which — as Hegel observes — one cannot even say how "positive", "categorical" and "assertoric" are related. This is what §01 meant by demanding a logic whose content and method agree.

**The prior Logic was reproduced inside a late form.** Being, Essence, Concept reappear as the four classes. The corpus has seen this at every scale — §13's circle, §26's retrospect on substance — and it is the structural reason Hegel can claim that nothing is left behind. It is also a falsifiable claim, and here it is discharged in some detail rather than asserted.

**A limit, recorded.** The classes are derived; the forms within them are, in the main, inherited and re-motivated. §27 recorded that Hegel's dismissal of a logical calculus was not vindicated by later history. This chapter's residue is of the same kind: an achievement with a visible seam, better acknowledged than smoothed over. A reader who wants to know whether the method can generate a form nobody had catalogued should watch the infinite judgment, which is the nearest thing here to a case in point — and note that even there the *name* came from the textbooks.

## XI. What Comes Next

- **§29 — The Syllogism.** The copula fully charged, judgment's extremes rejoined by the middle that the apodeictic judgment placed inside its subject. "Everything is a Syllogism" (*Enc.* §181), and the figures re-derived from the Concept's moments rather than enumerated — with the traditional apparatus of moods and figures faced directly, including where Hegel's treatment of it has not worn well. The syllogism of existence, of reflection, and of necessity, ending in the disjunctive syllogism, where the mediation becomes so complete that the mediating drops away — and subjectivity realizes itself as **objectivity**.

- **Beyond.** Mechanism, chemism, and teleology — the last of these where the concept of an end returns as an objective category and the long argument with external purposiveness is settled; then **Life**, where the warrant deferred in §VIII above is finally addressed; then Cognition, and the **Absolute Idea**, in which the method becomes its own content; and the Idea's release into Nature.

## Coda

The school logic's chapter on judgment is a chapter of definitions and a table. It tells the student what a judgment is — a combination of two concepts — and then sorts judgments by quality, quantity, relation, and modality, four headings whose mutual relation it does not raise and could not answer. Between its chapter on concepts and its chapter on judgments there is no argument, only a page break.

What this chapter has done is supply the missing argument and then use it to convert the table into a ladder. Judgment occurs because the Concept, achieving itself in singularity, thereby divides itself; the division is registered in a copula that asserts an identity its own terms cannot yet sustain; and the forms of judgment are the stages by which that copula is filled until it becomes a middle term. The inherited words all survive — positive, negative, infinite, categorical, disjunctive, apodeictic. As doctrines they do not. The negative judgment is no longer the positive with a *not* inserted but the preserver of a universal sphere; the infinite judgment is no longer a curiosity but the self-destruction of qualitative judgment as such, with crime and death for its instances; the evaluative judgment is no longer an intruder from ethics or aesthetics into the province of fact but the highest form of judgment, in which the predicate is "**the soul of the subject, by which the subject, as the body of this soul, is characterised through and through**" (*Enc.* §172).

And the chapter ends by dissolving its own subject matter. The apodeictic judgment succeeds so completely — putting the universal, the constitution, and the thing into one subject — that the division which made it a judgment is closed. What §27 called the Concept's downfall has run its course, and what it produces is not a repaired judgment but a different form, in which the connective is no longer a bare *is* but a reason.

The name of that form is the syllogism, and the school logic has a table for it too.
