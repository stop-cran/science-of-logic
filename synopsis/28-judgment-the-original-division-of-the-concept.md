# Judgment: The Original Division of the Concept

*The nineteenth installment of Section III — the **second chapter of Subjectivity**, following the Concept into **judgment**. "**The judgment is the self-diremption of the Notion**" (*SL* §1349): its moments become self-subsistent while their original unity remains their essential relation. Hegel's reading of **Urteil** as original division illustrates this argument; the etymology does not prove it. The plant's unclosing "**is, as it were, the judgment of the plant**" (*Enc.* §166), and "**all things are a judgment**" (*Enc.* §167) states the objective thesis, not a psychological one or a conclusion proved by the plant alone. **Proposition and judgment** are distinguished by what their terms do: a chronicle records Aristotle's death; a disputed chronology relates the date to a universal order and asserts it on grounds (*SL* §1350). Judgment's abstract formula is "**the individual is universal**" (*SL* §1363), but its terms and relation develop rather than remaining fixed beneath that formula. The immediate subject exceeds its singled-out property, and the universal predicate exceeds this individual. Hegel's demand for their mediated adequacy must be distinguished from the restricted correctness of ordinary predication. The **copula** carries this development: "**through a copula charged with content**", judgment "**comes to be a syllogism**" (*Enc.* §171). The proposed **four-class architecture** recasts Being, Essence, and Concept within judgment, with reflection and necessity distinguished within Essence. Its necessity must be shown through their content, not inferred from the number of headings. Hegel ranks forms by "**the logical significance of the predicate**" (*Enc.* §171); Kant's distinct dimensions of classification nevertheless have their own rationale and are not thereby refuted. The **judgment of existence** makes the **truth/correctness** distinction explicit: "**That a person is sick, or that some one has committed a theft, may certainly be correct. But the content is untrue**" (*Enc.* §172). The distinction concerns adequacy to the Concept, not permission to deny an unwelcome fact. **Determinate negation** preserves a presupposed sphere — "**in saying that the rose is not red, it is assumed that it has a colour, but a different one**" (*SL* §1376) — while its still-indeterminate relation fails to express the determinate individual. **Infinite judgment** exposes both the destruction of that relation and the reflected identity of its terms; their positive return, not collapse alone, leads to **reflection**. Hegel's applications to crime and death are reconstructed without making the logical schema a sufficient legal or medical ground. Reflection develops the subject through **this, some, all**; its passage from allness to **genus** rests on reflected individuality, not enumeration producing an essence. **Necessity** develops substantial connection through categorical, hypothetical, and disjunctive judgment; the **judgment of the Concept** then asks whether the singular's constitution corresponds to its universal nature. Assertoric assurance becomes problematic; **apodeictic** grounding makes the constitution mediate the assessment. The passage to syllogism requires that mediation to be distinguished as a **middle term relating both extremes**, not merely included in a richer subject. Four scientific comparisons retain their own standards: observation reports can be revisable evidence, as Neurath's account illustrates; Newton's distinction between homogeneous and mixed orange challenges Hegel's colour verdict on the target's actual grounds; warranted functional assessments need not await proof of Hegel's ontological account; and **category mistakes** offer a limited retrospective comparison, not one uniform doctrine equivalent to infinite judgment. The debt to §27 remains differentiated: later performance does not replace the local warrant for the triad, and the reconstructed transitions do not certify the inventory's complete necessity. In particular, the passage from developed **some** to **some not** remains a determinate question rather than a result secured by its subsequent use.*

## I. Where We Are: A Maturity That Is a Loss

§27 ended on a sentence that should be read as a piece of method rather than as rhetoric: the Concept's highest maturity is the stage at which its downfall begins. It is worth being precise about why, because the whole of this installment depends on it and because the point is easy to mistake for a pious paradox.

The Concept's three moments — universal, particular, singular — were not three items laid side by side. Each was the whole, determined differently. The universal was **free power** that keeps itself in its other; the particular was the universal's own self-diremption; the singular was the point at which the Concept, in Hegel's phrase quoted at §27, seizes itself — "the profundity in which the Notion seizes itself" (*SL* §1339). But precisely there a difficulty arises that no amount of care in stating the triad can avert. In singularity the Concept is **this** — determinate, exclusive, set off against what it is not. And a determination that excludes is a determination that stands over against something. The Concept, at the very point of grasping itself, becomes **external to itself**.

That externality is not imposed from outside by a logician who insists on writing propositions. In singularity, the Concept's determinate moment has itself become a totality and excludes the universal, which nevertheless remains its own moment. Its sides therefore stand as **self-subsistent and essentially related**, not as unrelated items subsequently joined by a connective (*SL* §§1345–1347). This tension is **judgment**: the Concept's original unity persists as the relation of extremes which have not yet sublated their independence.

The transition belongs to the corpus's recurring question: how does a form's achieved determination require its further development? The earlier arguments must not be reduced to a common image of collapse. §13's true infinite was reached when the finite's own going-beyond proved to be its own return; §21 developed self-excluding opposition into contradiction and then its resolution into ground; §26 retained reciprocal mediation while contesting the substances' supposed independence within it. Here the issue is neither an external collision nor an emptied unity, but the self-subsistence of moments whose essential relation remains.

Hegel states the result flatly. The judgment's sides are "**totalities which to begin with are essentially self-subsistent**", and their unity is "**at first only a relation of self-subsistents; not as yet the concrete and pregnant unity that has returned into itself from this reality, but only a unity outside which the self-subsistent sides persist as extremes that are not sublated in it**." (*SL* §1349)

Two things matter in that sentence. First, **the extremes are totalities** — not fragments or halves. Their determinations are nevertheless still to develop through judgment; calling each a totality does not mean that each already explicitly exhibits the whole Concept (*SL* §1347). Second, the unity is "**only a relation**" of self-subsistents. Hegel distinguishes beginning from their externality from beginning with the **original unity** which grounds their division (*SL* §1349). The chapter must exhibit that unity without merely asserting it against the extremes' independence.

And the retrospect is immediate. Judgment is not merely the next form; it is what the earlier transitions were all along, now arrived at their truth:

> "In the sphere of being, the object becomes and others itself, the finite perishes or goes under in the infinite; in the sphere of Existence, the object issues from its ground into Appearance and falls to the ground, the accident manifests the wealth of substance as well as its power; in being, there is transition into an other, in essence, reflected being in an other by which the necessary relation is revealed. This movement of transition and reflection has now passed over into the original partition of the Notion which, while bringing back the individual to the in-itself of its universality, equally determines the universal as something actual." (*SL* §1353)

This is Hegel's retrospective claim about the preceding Doctrines, not a warrant for making their movements interchangeable (§06). Transition in Being and reflection in Essence are retained in a changed form: the universal becomes actual in the singular, and the singular is determined through its universal. Their original unity grounds judgment, but their **posited unity** is precisely what its development has still to establish.

## II. *Urteil*: Original Division, Not Combination

The received account of judgment is so entrenched that stating it feels like stating a truism. There is a subject, out in the world. There is a predicate, a general term, in the mind. Judging consists in bringing the second to the first. The copula records that the bringing has been done.

Hegel's objection is not that this account is useless. It is that it is an account **of the outside of judgment**, mistaken for its nature. He grants the appearance its due: "**But regarded from the side of externality, the Notion is present in the judgment as Appearance, since its moments therein attain self-subsistence, and it is on this external side that ordinary thinking tends to fasten.**" (*SL* §1349) The externality is real — it is the whole point of the chapter that the Concept has become external to itself — but it is the **result** of a division, not the starting condition of a combination.

The German word is his witness. *Urteil* — *Ur-Teil*, original division. "**It is thus the original division [Teilung] of what is originally one; thus the word Urteil refers to what judgment is in and for itself.**" (*SL* §1349) The *Encyclopaedia* puts it in one sentence that may be the cleanest statement of the doctrine anywhere in Hegel: the etymology "**goes deeper, as it were declaring the unity of the notion to be primary, and its distinction to be the original partition. And that is what the Judgment really is.**" (*Enc.* §166)

A caution is owed here, and the corpus's own rules require it. An etymology is not an argument. If the case for judgment-as-division rested on the accident that German *Urteil* can be read as *Ur-Teil*, it would rest on nothing. Editorial note 43 to the Russian edition disputes this derivation and, citing Kluge (*Etymologisches Wörterbuch*, 9th ed. 1921, S. 470), relates *Urteil* to *erteilen* — what is awarded or adjudged, as by a judge. The same note identifies a precedent for the *Ur-Teil* reading in Schelling's *System des transzendentalen Idealismus*. We have inspected the note, not the two works it cites; its report is not a direct philological verification. The conceptual argument must stand **independently** of the etymology: what we reconstructed above was the Concept's singularity as its self-externalization. The word can serve as a mnemonic for that argument, not as evidence of its necessity. This corpus takes it in that bounded way, without claiming to have settled the philology.

From the division-reading, Hegel challenges three features of the received account.

**That judgments combine heterogeneous concepts.** Half-right, says Hegel: the theory "**is correct, so far as it implies that it is the notion which forms the presupposition of the judgment, and which in the judgment comes up under the form of difference. But on the other hand, it is false to speak of notions differing in kind.**" (*Enc.* §166) Subject and predicate are not two concepts of different species brought into contact; they are the one Concept under the form of difference. This is why the account of §27 had to come first. If the universal were the common element and the singular a bare *this*, subject and predicate really would be heterogeneous, and their union really would be an external act.

**That the predicate is ascribed by us.** "**Language like this looks upon the subject as self-subsistent outside, and the predicate as found somewhere in our head.**" (*Enc.* §166) Against it, the plain deliverance of the copula: "**By saying 'This rose is red', or 'This picture is beautiful', we declare, that it is not we who from outside attach beauty to the picture or redness to the rose, but that these are the characteristics proper to these objects.**" (*Enc.* §166) The argument is modest and strong: whatever our theory of judgment, our practice of judging asserts objectivity, and a theory that cannot accommodate what judging says it is doing has misdescribed it.

**That judgment simply turns up.** Formal logic, Hegel says, "**makes the judgment look as if it were something merely contingent, and does not offer any proof for the advance from notion on to judgment.**" (*Enc.* §166) The charge concerns the **necessity of the advance**, not the inability to define judgment or reason with it. A logic abstracting from the development of its forms does not thereby refute that development, but neither does its restricted task supply it. Hegel's answer is that the Concept is not inert — "**It is rather an infinite form, of boundless activity, as it were the punctum saliens of all vitality, and thereby self-differentiating.**" (*Enc.* §166) The image is of a living, active source; its argumentative content is the self-differentiation reconstructed in §I: "**This disruption of the notion into the difference of its constituent functions — a disruption imposed by the native act of the notion — is the judgment.**" (*Enc.* §166)

Which yields the illustration that makes the doctrine graspable in a single stroke. The germ of a plant "**contains its particular, such as root, branches, leaves, etc.: but these details are at first present only potentially, and are not realised till the germ uncloses. This unclosing is, as it were, the judgment of the plant.**" (*Enc.* §166)

The plant is not performing a psychological act. Its unfolding supplies Hegel's natural illustration of **self-articulation**: distinctions implicit in the germ acquire distinct, relatively self-subsistent existence. This carries §27's claim that the Concept "constitutes a stage of nature as well as of spirit" (*SL* §1299) into the account of judgment. Hegel's general thesis is broader: "**all things are a judgment. That is to say, they are individuals which are a universality or inner nature in themselves — a universal which is individualised.**" (*Enc.* §167) The illustration makes that thesis intelligible; it does not by itself prove its unrestricted application or settle §27's question about the Concept's organic and inorganic realization.

The corpus has met this claim before. §24's law was not merely our summary of appearances but appearance's own stable content; §25's actuality was not merely our modal notion but the thing's own unity of inner and outer. Here Hegel likewise claims judgment as an objective determination, without denying the act of judging. His objection is to treating that act as a merely subjective combination, not to acknowledging that people perform it.

## III. Judgment Is Not Proposition

Before the development can begin, Hegel distinguishes judgment from a proposition which shares its grammatical form. The distinction bears directly on what a scientific report is doing.

Grammatical form is not logical form. "**We may take this opportunity of remarking, too, that though a proposition has a subject and predicate in the grammatical sense, this does not make it a judgment. The latter requires that the predicate be related to the subject as one Notion determination to another, and therefore as a universal to a particular or individual.**" (*SL* §1350)

The examples are unusually concrete for Hegel, and both works supply them. From the *Science of Logic*, in Miller's translation: "**'Aristotle died at the age of 73, in the fourth year of the 115th Olympiad,' is a mere proposition, not a judgment.**" (*SL* §1350) From the *Encyclopaedia*, in Wallace's: "**'Caesar was born at Rome in such and such a year waged war in Gaul for ten years, crossed the Rubicon, etc.', are propositions, but not judgments**", and — the case that shows the criterion is not about grandeur of subject matter — "**it is absurd to say that such statements as 'I slept well last night' or 'Present arms!' maybe turned into the form of a judgment.**" (*Enc.* §167)

What is the criterion? Not importance, and not correctness. "Aristotle died at 73" is the sort of claim evidence settles either way — and the conventional dates, 384–322 BCE, give about sixty-two, without changing its logical form ([SEP, “Aristotle,” §1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle/)). Taken merely as a chronicle entry, it records a singular circumstance of a singular subject rather than expressly relating **universal to singular**. That is the use Hegel contrasts with judgment; it is not a claim that dates have no universal determinations.

But the same words can become a judgment, and Hegel's account of how is the interesting part: "**It would partake of the nature of a judgment only if doubt had been thrown on one of the circumstances, the date of the death, or the age of that philosopher, and the given figures had been asserted on the strength of some reason or other. In that case, these figures would be taken as something universal, as time that still subsists apart from this particular content of the death of Aristotle, whether as time filled with some other content, or even as empty time.**" (*SL* §1350) And the domestic version: "**the news that my friend N. has died is a proposition; and it would be a judgment only if there were a question whether he was really dead or only in a state of catalepsy.**" (*SL* §1350)

This deserves more weight than it usually gets. The transformation from proposition to judgment happens when the content is **put in question and asserted on grounds** — when the date is no longer merely reported but maintained against a rival date, so that "the fourth year of the 115th Olympiad" functions as a determinate position in a universal order of time rather than as a fact adhering to Aristotle. The same sentence is a proposition in a chronicle and a judgment in a disputed chronology.

Two consequences follow, and both bear directly on the corpus's question about scientific method.

First, **a report's logical role is not fixed by its brevity.** Our examples — "the needle reads 4.7", "the counter registered 217 at 11:03" — can be used as records of singular occurrences, but referring a reading to a scale or treating it as an instance of a kind already involves universality. Hegel's own "This wall is green" is a judgment, although he ranks it low (*Enc.* §171). This does not show that a bare record is incorrigible, or that observation reports cease to be evidence when they become contestable. Nor did the twentieth-century protocol-sentence debate have one foundationalist answer. Neurath's publicly assessable, structured, revisable protocols retain an evidential role without serving as an infallible foundation ([SEP, “Otto Neurath,” discussion of protocol sentences](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/neurath/)). Our comparison with Hegel concerns the universals and grounds involved in a report's use; it neither refutes that position nor licenses treating its reports as atoms stripped of those relations.

Second, **the sciences do not consist of catalogues.** A table of observations, read as a register of singular occurrences, is a set of propositions. Reading its entries as instances of universals gives them the form of judgment, but scientific work does not stop at that boundary. "This specimen has such a bone structure" is already a judgment, not a mere proposition; taking that structure as grounds for "this specimen is a mammal", with all the commitments that carries, advances from a quality to the thing's kind. The movement therefore includes both the passage from proposition to judgment and the development between forms of judgment. Neither is mere accumulation.

## IV. The Copula, and the Contradiction Every Judgment Contains

Now the engine. Hegel says that every judgment can be stated **abstractly** as the singular's unity with the universal (*SL* §1363). The immediate positive judgment gives this relation its first, still unmediated form:

> "The immediate pure enunciation of the positive judgment is, therefore, the proposition: the individual is universal. This enunciation must not be put in the form: A is B; for A and B are entirely formless and consequently meaningless names; the judgment as such, however, and therefore even the judgment of existence, has Notion determinations for its extremes. A is B can represent any mere proposition just as well as a judgment." (*SL* §1363)

The attack on "A is B" is worth pausing over, because §27 expressly left this comparison to the judgment chapter. There Hegel's polemical verdict was distinguished from the methodological task of comprehending a calculus's achievements and limits, and of asking whether it exhibits the self-development of the **Concept**. The judgment-forms are the first half of the common object §27 promised — the syllogistic figures are the other half, and §29 takes them up. The objection is not that uninterpreted letters are useless; it is that schematizing judgment as "A is B" leaves its categorial difference unspecified. At this initial stage the subject place is the place of singularity and the predicate place the place of universality: the two places are **not symmetrical**, and an arbitrary pair of letters does not express why. A formal logic can distinguish these roles in its notation. Registering their distinction, however, is not yet deriving it, nor showing how the relation develops through successive judgment-forms. It is that further task which Hegel undertakes, without thereby invalidating the formal representation or the inferences correctly made with it.

The difficulty has **two directions**, not merely the singular's difference from the universal. In form, the singular subject is referred to a universal predicate which extends beyond it; in content, the concrete subject contains a manifold of determinations of which the predicate selects only one (*SL* §§1364–1369). The rose is more than redness, and redness is not confined to this rose. "**The subject and predicate in the immediate judgment touch, as it were, only in a single point, but do not cover each other.**" (*Enc.* §172) This is Hegel's objection to the form's **adequacy to the Concept**, not a discovery that ordinary predication mistakenly asserts numerical identity or exhaustive description. A correct ascription need make neither claim. His stronger demand is that the unity asserted across the difference become explicit in the terms and their mediation; unless that demand is justified, the mismatch does not refute the more restricted achievement (§VI).

Where is this mismatch registered? In the **copula** — and the copula is therefore where the whole development is lodged. At the outset it is the thinnest thing imaginable: "**As the two Notion determinations are determined, so also is their relation, the is or copula; it too can only have the significance of an immediate, abstract being. On account of the relation which as yet contains no mediation or negation, this judgment is called the positive.**" (*SL* §1362) The *Encyclopaedia*: the copula "**even while stating the identity of subject and predicate, does so at first only by an abstract 'is'.**" (*Enc.* §171)

But the copula is not a mere sign. "**The copula 'is' springs from the nature of the notion, to be self-identical even in parting with its own.**" (*Enc.* §166) That is the Concept's definition from §27 — the universal as free power that remains itself in its other — restated as the meaning of the word *is*. The copula is the Concept's self-identity **surviving its own division**. Which is why the mismatch cannot be left standing and why it cannot be cured by abandoning judgment: the copula already asserts what the terms cannot yet sustain.

The resolution, therefore, is not to find judgments in which subject and predicate happen to match. It is for the copula to be progressively **filled** — for the bare *is* to be replaced by a connection that actually exhibits how this singular is this universal. Hegel's statement of the programme is the hinge of the chapter:

> "Conformably to such an identity the subject has to be put also in the characteristic of the predicate. By this means the latter also receives the characteristic of the former: so that the copula receives its full complement and full force. Such is the continuous specification by which the judgment, through a copula charged with content, comes to be a syllogism." (*Enc.* §171)

The moments developed in §27 do not remain ready-made terms to which a relation is subsequently attached. As subject and predicate they receive **further determinations through their relation**: the singular is raised into universality, while the universal is determined as actual in the singular (*SL* §§1352–1355). These are not two independent operations, nor is the copula a third thing changing between fixed extremes. Their reciprocal determination is what fills it. Nor does judgment's two-sided form eliminate **particularity**: the developing determinateness of its extremes belongs to the Concept no less than their universality and singularity. Subject and predicate consequently cannot be assigned once for all to two of the Concept's moments; what each expresses must be followed in the developing judgment. The task is not to classify newly acquired properties under an unchanged triad, but to exhibit how the moments themselves become further determined in this form.

A copula charged with content leads to a **middle term** when the mediating determination is also distinguished from the extremes and relates to both. In our expanded example, "this action, being of such and such a constitution, and such constitution being what makes actions good, is good", the particular constitution supplies both connections, not just an extra description of the subject. §171 announces this destination; the development must establish it rather than take the announcement for a proof (§VII).

The development offers a bounded comparison with §22. There, **formal** ground presented one content in two forms, **real** ground distinguished contents while leaving their connection externally determined, and **complete** ground mediated that connection through another relation which it still presupposed. The result was **conditioning mediation**, not a completed explanation. Here the copula likewise cannot remain an empty connection. But this comparison does not derive its development from ground's sequence: Essence's reflective relations and the Concept's own differentiation must each be followed in their particular form.

## V. Why the List Is a Ladder

§27 left **two connected tests**, not one. The local question is whether *SL* §1276's mediation warrants universality, particularity, and singularity without importing the familiar classificatory triad. The further question is what those moments do in judgment and syllogism. Their subsequent work can illuminate their significance; it cannot replace the argument for their derivation. Here we follow that work and ask a related question about judgment's **inventory**: does each form's determination require the next, or does the inherited order silently supply the necessity?

Hegel's answer begins with a methodological criticism of the received enumeration:

> "After we are made aware of this continuous specification of the judgment, we can see a meaning and an interconnection in what are usually stated as the kinds of judgment. Not only does the ordinary enumeration seem purely casual, but it is also superficial, and even bewildering in its statement of their distinctions. The distinction between positive, categorical, and assertory judgments is either a pure invention of fancy, or is left undetermined. On the right theory, the different judgments follow necessarily from one another, and present the continuous specification of the notion; for the judgment itself is nothing but the notion specified." (*Enc.* §171)

This criticism must meet the table on its own terms. Positive, categorical, and assertoric belong to **different dimensions**, not three mutually exclusive classes: a judgment can receive all three determinations. Kant's account distinguishes quantity, quality, relation, and modality, and expressly says that modality adds nothing to the content but concerns the copula's relation to thought (*Critique of Pure Reason*, Analytic of Concepts, chapter I, section II; [Meiklejohn translation, “Of the Logical Function of the Understanding in Judgements”](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/4280)). That is not an inability to relate the headings. Hegel demands something further: that the forms themselves develop necessarily, changing the determinations of the terms and their relation. His complaint is not established merely by observing that the table lacks a single ranking across its dimensions.

Then the general thesis:

> "The various kinds of judgment are no empirical aggregate. They are a systematic whole based on a principle; and it was one of Kant's great merits to have first emphasised the necessity of showing this." (*Enc.* §171)

Note carefully what is credited. Kant's **demand** that the forms of judgment be shown to form a system is one achievement; the guiding perception that their differences express the universal forms of the logical Idea is another. Neither amounts to endorsing his table as it stands. As in §27's treatment of apperception, Hegel retains an insight while finding its execution wanting. Kant's division "**cannot be called satisfactory, partly from the merely formal application of this categorical rubric, partly on account of their content**" (*Enc.* §171). But the guiding perception is precisely what Hegel follows in the passage quoted below.

**Merely formal application** is the charge here. It bears on the complaint reported in §27 that Kant borrowed his categories from received logic instead of deriving their necessity. These remain Hegel's charges, not verdicts on Kant established by this synopsis. Nor does borrowing the inventory by itself settle the question: Hegel's own account must show what work its inherited forms do.

Hegel's proposed advance includes an **internal criterion** for ranking the forms, and this single clause is the chapter's methodological core:

> "Far from occupying the same level, and being of equal value, the different species of judgment form a series of steps, the difference of which rests upon the logical significance of the predicate." (*Enc.* §171)

Judgments are **ranked**, and ranked by what kind of universal the predicate is. That is a criterion internal to judgment itself. To ground the architecture of the classes, Hegel follows the guiding perception he has credited to Kant and develops it in his own terms: judgment reproduces the stages already developed in the logical Idea. The ranking criterion distinguishes the forms within this systematic account; it does not, stated alone, derive its architecture:

> "Still it rests upon a true perception of the fact that the different species of judgment derive their features from the universal forms of the logical idea itself. If we follow this clue, it will supply us with three chief kinds of judgment parallel to the stages of Being, Essence, and Notion. The second of these kinds, as required by the character of Essence, which is the stage of differentiation, must be doubled. We find the inner ground for that systematisation of judgments in the circumstance that when the Notion, which is the unity of Being and Essence in a comprehensive thought, unfolds, as it does in the judgment, it must reproduce these two stages in a transformation proper to the notion." (*Enc.* §171)

This yields Hegel's **four-class architecture**: the judgment of **existence** (Being), the judgments of **reflection** and of **necessity** (Essence), and the judgment of the **Concept**. But calling Essence differentiation does not, by arithmetic alone, prove why it requires just these two classes. Their difference must be exhibited: reflection relates the singular to an essential universal still shared across a manifold; necessity makes that universality the subject's substantial ground. §§VI–VII follow the transition. The parallel with the preceding Logic is an argument to reconstruct through that content, not a certificate that the inventory is exhaustive.

Hegel then offers a test that anyone can apply, and it is a good one because it appeals to a discrimination we already make:

> "That judgments differ in value is evident in our ordinary ways of thinking. We should not hesitate to ascribe a very slight faculty of judgment to a person who habitually framed only such judgments as 'This wall is green', 'This stove is hot'. On the other hand we should credit with a genuine capacity of judgment the person whose criticisms dealt with such questions as whether a certain work of art was beautiful, whether a certain action was good, and so on. In judgments of the first-mentioned kind the content forms only an abstract quality, the presence of which can be sufficiently detected by immediate perception. To pronounce a work of art to be beautiful, or an action to be good, requires on the contrary a comparison of the objects with what they ought to be, i.e. with their notion." (*Enc.* §171)

The appeal makes Hegel's criterion recognizable: detecting a sensible quality differs from assessing an object against its **Concept**. It does not establish that every perceptual report is easy, every evaluative assertion well grounded, or one person more capable merely because of the subjects discussed. The later distinction between assertoric assurance and apodeictic grounding will itself expose that difference. The ranking concerns the logical determination expressed, not a substitute for examining the evidence or the quality of a particular judgment.

**What has been established so far?** Hegel supplies a determinate programme: subject and predicate develop through their relation; the predicate's logical significance orders the forms; the earlier spheres recur within the Concept. None of that is merely a new name for a column. Yet neither the historical source of the names nor the presence of this programme settles its execution. The following sections reconstruct the principal transitions and identify where a premise remains contestable — especially the adequacy demanded of predication and the particular judgment's passage to *some not*. The local derivation questioned in §27 remains distinct from this further performance. Nor may our compression be charged to Hegel as an omission: a verdict on a transition must engage his argument, not just the space we give it.

## VI. The Judgment of Existence

The first class takes the copula at face value: the bare *is*, an immediate universal for predicate, an immediate singular for subject. Here the abstract universal of §27, VI stands as a predicate opposed to an immediate singular: one determination, such as *red*, is held apart from the concrete thing whose determination it is. Hegel develops this immediate relation through positive, negative, and infinite judgment. (§09 met this class as **Judgments of qualitative being**, a phrase of its own leaning on Wallace, whose section heading is *Qualitative Judgment* and whose §172 renders the term *the judgment of definite Being*; Miller's *judgment of existence* renders the same *Urteil des Daseins* and is used throughout here.)

### (a) Positive

The *Science of Logic* gives the positive judgment's formula as "the individual is universal" (*SL* §1363). The *Encyclopaedia* instead has "The individual is a particular" (*Enc.* §172). This is a difference between Hegel's two presentations, not merely their translations: in the *Science of Logic*, "The individual is a particular" is the positive expression of the negative judgment, expressly not itself a positive judgment (*SL* §1373). The development followed below is the former work's. The *Encyclopaedia* nevertheless makes the initial mismatch vivid: "**But the individual is not a particular: or in more precise language, such a single quality is not congruous with the concrete nature of the subject.**" (*Enc.* §172) The rose "**is a concrete thing, and so is not red only: it has also an odour, a specific form, and many other features not implied in the predicate red. The predicate on its part is an abstract universal, and does not apply to the rose alone.**" (*Enc.* §172)

Here the corpus reaches what may be the single most useful thing in the chapter for its own governing question.

> "It is one of the fundamental assumptions of dogmatic Logic that Qualitative judgments such as 'The rose is red' or 'is not red' can contain truth. Correct they may be, i.e. in the limited circle of perception, of finite conception and thought: that depends on the content, which likewise is finite, and, on its own merits, untrue. Truth, however, as opposed to correctness, depends solely on the form, viz. on the notion as it is put and the reality corresponding to it. … Correctness, generally speaking, concerns only the formal coincidence between our conception and its content, whatever the constitution of this content may be. Truth, on the contrary, lies in the coincidence of the object with itself, that is, with its notion." (*Enc.* §172)

The distinction must be stated carefully or it becomes an equivocation, and Hegel warns of exactly that: "**In common life the terms truth and correctness are often treated as synonymous: we speak of the truth of a content, when we are only thinking of its correctness.**" (*Enc.* §172)

**Correctness** concerns agreement with the content represented; Hegel expressly allows it to a judgment whose content or form he ranks as untrue. The passage does depreciate that content's claim to **truth**, so we should not soften his hierarchy into an assurance that he disparages nothing. What survives is the correctness of the report — a false measurement is not redeemed by profundity. The stronger truth at issue here concerns the object's agreement **with its own Concept**, and the judgment-form's adequacy to express that relation, not merely correspondence between an assertion and a fact.

The examples make it concrete and also make it uncomfortable: "**That a person is sick, or that some one has committed a theft, may certainly be correct. But the content is untrue. A sick body is not in harmony with the notion of body, and there is a want of congruity between theft and the notion of human conduct.**" (*Enc.* §172)

Two misreadings must be blocked at once, because both are common.

It is **not** a claim that "this person is sick" might be false when the person is sick. Hegel says explicitly that it "may certainly be correct". The judgment is correct; the *content* — the sick body — is untrue, that is, out of accord with the concept invoked as its standard.

And it is **not** a licence to call whatever one dislikes "untrue". The standard invoked is not the speaker's preference but the object's own concept, and §27's discipline applies with full force: a concept is not a list of marks assembled by a classifier but a principle containing the thing's own development. Where no such concept is available, the vocabulary of truth-as-adequacy has nothing to attach to and should not be used.

The distinction bears on a recognizable feature of scientific judgment. Physiology distinguishes function from malfunction; medicine distinguishes health from pathology; engineering distinguishes a working mechanism from a failed one. A correct description of the failure does not make the failed thing successful. A physiological assessment can ask whether a heart maintains circulation and test its performance against that specified role. Calling the result a malfunction presupposes the role as a standard; the evidence supports the assessment relative to it, not by itself the standard's ultimate authority. This bounded assessment does not establish Hegel's stronger claim that the standard is the organ's own Concept, but neither does it depend on proving that claim. Hegel's positive account of the organism's immanent standard belongs to Life; here its warrant remains a task.

### (b) Negative

The mismatch of the positive judgment is not left to be noticed by us. The judgment states it: since the singular is not the abstract universal, the positive judgment's own content demands its negation.

But negation here is not annihilation, and the corpus should recognize the instrument. "**Thus the negative judgment is not total negation; the universal sphere which contains the predicate still subsists, and therefore the relation of the subject to the predicate is essentially still positive**" (*SL* §1376). The example, in both texts: "**in saying that the rose is not red, it is assumed that it has a colour, but a different one. In respect of this universal sphere the judgment is still positive.**" (*SL* §1376) Wallace adds the consequence — the negation "**still leaves the connection of the subject with the predicate subsisting. The predicate is in that manner a sort of relative universal, of which a special phase only has been negatived.**" (*Enc.* §173)

This is **determinate negation** (§11, §21) operating within judgment's relation. The sphere of colour is **presupposed and preserved**, not inferred from an arbitrary denial of *red*: Hegel's argument concerns the continuity of Concept-determinations, not an unrestricted rule about every negative sentence (*SL* §§1375–1376). Within that sphere, negation retains determinateness but leaves unspecified which colour belongs to the subject. The predicate is thereby **particular**, a universal with determinateness in it: "not only is the universal sphere left but determinateness, too, is preserved, though converted into an indeterminate" one (*SL* §1377). This gain prepares the next difficulty rather than completing the subject's determination.

Hegel also criticizes detaching negation of the relation from determination of its extremes: "**It was, therefore, unnecessary for logicians to make such a fuss over the not of the negative judgment being attached to the copula.**" (*SL* §1374) In his account, "the rose is not-red" and "the rose is-not red" cannot be separated simply by relocating the *not*, because the predicate's determination is already a determinate relation. This is his argument about judgment; it does not erase distinctions that another formal analysis explicitly makes between a negative predicate and negation of a proposition.

### (c) Infinite

The negative judgment preserved the subject's relation to *colour*, but left its colour indeterminate. That does not satisfy the correspondence Hegel demands: the individual "**is not an undetermined determinate, but the determined determinate**", and "**the rose is not a thing of some colour or other, but has only the specific colour that is rose-colour**" (*SL* §1379). This is not a rule that wider predicates are always worse; the defect is their failure here to express the subject's determinate individuality. Hegel therefore says that the preserved sphere "**contained a universality more purged of limitation than the positive judgment, and for that reason must be all the more negated of the subject as an individual**" (*SL* §1381). The negative side of this further negation removes the whole immediate relation: "**In this manner, the whole extent of the predicate is negated and there is no longer any positive relation between it and the subject. This is the infinite judgment.**" (*SL* §1381) Its positive side, already implicit in the negative judgment, is the individual's mediated return into itself (*SL* §§1379–1380); the transition cannot be reconstructed from the destruction of the relation alone.

The examples are deliberately ridiculous: "**spirit is not red, yellow, etc., is not acid, not alkaline, etc., the rose is not an elephant, the understanding is not a table**" (*SL* §1382); Wallace's "**'The mind is no elephant'; 'A lion is no table'**" (*Enc.* §173). And the verdict is exact:

> "These judgments are correct or true, as the expression goes, but in spite of such truth they are nonsensical and absurd. Or rather, they are not judgments at all." (*SL* §1382)

Here the truth/correctness distinction pays off immediately. A mind is indeed not an elephant; the statement cannot be faulted for correctness. But it is not a judgment, because judgment requires subject and predicate to share a universal sphere within which something is asserted, and here there is none. "**It is supposed to be a judgment, and consequently to contain a relation of subject and predicate; yet at the same time such a relation is supposed not to be in it.**" (*SL* §1382) The form has destroyed itself by being carried out completely. The *Encyclopaedia* characterizes its place in formal logic polemically: "**The negatively infinite judgment, in which the subject has no relation whatever to the predicate, gets its place in the Formal Logic solely as a nonsensical curiosity.**" (*Enc.* §173) That characterization does not cover every use of the name. Kant's cited account (§V) gives the infinite judgment a limitative role in transcendental logic, while general logic rightly classes it as affirmative: the soul is placed in the sphere remaining after the mortal is excluded, without thereby being positively determined. This is not Hegel's relationless case, and the charge of mere curiosity does not describe its rationale.

For Hegel, his negatively infinite judgment is not a mere curiosity. "**But the infinite judgment is not really a mere casual form adopted by subjective thought. It exhibits the proximate result of the dialectical process in the immediate judgments preceding (the positive and simply-negative), and distinctly displays their finitude and untruth.**" (*Enc.* §173) On Hegel's account, the absurd form is the necessary outcome of the sound-looking ones, and it is how they show what was wrong with them.

The objective instances are what make this more than a technicality, and Hegel gives two.

**Crime.** "**In civil litigation, something is negated only as the property of the other party, it being conceded that it should be theirs if they had the right to it; and it is only the title of right that is in dispute; the universal sphere of right is therefore recognised and maintained in that negative judgment. But crime is the infinite judgment which negates not merely the particular right, but the universal sphere as well, negates right as right.**" (*SL* §1382) Wallace's account draws the legal consequence: the thief "**denies the right of that person in general, and therefore he is not merely forced to restore what he has stolen, but is punished in addition, because he has violated law as law, i.e. law in general.**" (*Enc.* §173) Hegel thus distinguishes contesting a title within acknowledged right from violating right as such. That distinction gives his application definite content; it does not, without further premises about right and its restoration, establish that punishment is justified or what punishment should be. The logical schema is part of the proposed ground, not a deduction of a legal response by itself.

**Death.** "**Similarly death, as a negatively infinite judgment, is distinguished from disease as simply-negative. In disease, merely this or that function of life is checked or negatived: in death, as we ordinarily say, body and soul part, i.e. subject and predicate utterly diverge.**" (*Enc.* §173) On this application, disease negates a function within a persisting living unity; death is the dissolution of that unity. The body-and-soul formulation belongs to the source. Neither it nor the logical contrast supplies a medical criterion for determining when an organism has died.

**The positive moment.** Hegel does not infer a positive result merely from calling something a double negation. The preceding movement has negated the subject's immediate determination through the predicate and negated that still-indeterminate result: individuality is now explicitly self-related through this mediation. "**The positive moment of the infinite judgment, of the negation of the negation, is the reflection of individuality into itself, whereby it is posited for the first time as a determinate determinateness.**" (*SL* §1383) Its expression is "the individual is individual". Symmetrically, "**The positively infinite judgment equally runs: the universal is universal, and as such is equally posited as the return into itself.**" (*SL* §1384) The universal's abstract determinateness has likewise been sublated: its identity now contains distinction rather than merely confronting the singular as an immediate quality. But neither formula remains a judgment: "**in the negatively infinite judgment the difference is, so to speak, too great for it to remain a judgment … in the positively infinite judgment, on the contrary, only identity is present and owing to the complete lack of difference it is no longer a judgment.**" (*SL* §1385) This two-sided result, not the negative collapse alone, exhausts the judgment of existence.

With this the judgment of existence has consumed itself, and what it leaves behind is not nothing but terms that have been driven into themselves. "**More precisely, it is the judgment of existence that has sublated itself; hereby there is posited what the copula of the judgment contains, namely, that the qualitative extremes are sublated in this their identity. Since however this unity is the Notion, it is immediately sundered again into its extremes and appears as a judgment, whose terms however are no longer immediate but reflected into themselves. The judgment of existence has passed over into the judgment of reflection.**" (*SL* §1386) The name of the next class is therefore a result and not a label: the judgment of **reflection** is so called because its terms are the terms of the judgment of existence **reflected into themselves**.

## VII. Reflection, Necessity, and the Judgment of the Concept

The remaining three classes are treated here more briefly than the first, in proportion to what they add to the corpus's governing question rather than to their length in Hegel's text. The predicate's logical significance remains the criterion of rank, but it cannot be followed apart from the subject whose determination it is. We must ask both **what kind of universal is the predicate?** and **how is the subject determined in relation to it?**

**The judgment of reflection.** The predicate is no longer an immediate quality but a determination of **relation** — useful, harmful, dangerous, fit for a purpose. "This plant is medicinal" does not report a look; it determines the plant through its connection with the illness it cures (*Enc.* §174). Here being **reflected into self** does not mean withdrawing from relations: the thing's reflection into its essential universal is at the same time its relation to another. The universal now expresses an essential continuity through a manifold of concrete existences, rather than an isolated property. Hegel explicitly places this universality in the sphere of **Appearance** (*SL* §§1387–1391): the essential manifests itself through the existing manifold, as §24, III developed, rather than subsisting in a beyond. The earlier form is reproduced within the Concept's development, not substituted for it. Such a relative determination nevertheless does not yet express the thing's fully developed substantial nature; medicinality does not exhaust the plant's Concept.

The subject is consequently determined through this essential universal, and its immediate singularity no longer suffices. Hegel marks an asymmetry: in the judgment of existence the movement ran primarily through the predicate; here the predicate supplies the basis and the subject develops directly, further determining the predicate in turn (*SL* §1390). The forms are **singular**, **particular**, and **universal** or of allness. In the singular judgment, *this* does not exhaust the essential universal: if this is useful, others may be too. Its extension gives *some*, but only an indeterminate plurality, not the universality to which the subject must correspond (*SL* §§1392–1394). Hegel then argues that this particular subject both positively relates singularity and universality and negates their adequacy to one another. He takes the subject's negative determination to determine the relation, or copula, itself — the connection between extremes and relation already argued in §1374. On that basis: "**The judgment: some men are happy, involves the immediate consequence that some men are not happy.**" (*SL* §1395) The difficulty belongs to that transfer: does the negative relation establish an excluded remainder, or only leave the predicate's scope indeterminate? The reconstruction must show why the limitation also delimits where the predicate does not hold, rather than merely how far its assertion extends. Hegel's developed particularity is not simply an independently fixed *at least one*, but that distinction alone does not warrant *some not*. §29, IV carries this question into the syllogism; the subsequent use of the premise does not prove it here.

**Allness** addresses the next defect: *some* lacks the determinacy of *this*, while *all* would exhaust the extension to which the predicate belongs (*SL* §§1396–1399). Yet if its individuals remain indifferent, their universality is only an external collection. Hegel's positive argument is not that enough enumeration produces a genus. The singular that emerged from the preceding judgments was already **reflection into self** — negative identity, not a bare immediate atom. Allness must therefore express the unity presupposed in its individuals, not merely add them together. Hegel expressly refuses simply to anticipate the genus before exhibiting this mediation (*SL* §§1400–1401). When the subject is determined as universality and the predicate's relation to it becomes their identity, the universal is posited as their **substantial ground**: the judgment of necessity (*SL* §§1402–1404). This develops the logical requirement for a kind; it does not turn an observed common property into a necessary essence merely because it belongs to every observed instance. The weakness of induction (§03) poses a connected problem, but is not itself Hegel's derivation.

**The judgment of necessity.** In the **categorical** judgment — Hegel's example is "gold is a metal" (*Enc.* §177) — the predicate expresses the subject's substantial nature (*SL* §1407). Yet the subject's particular determination still confronts the genus as contingent; why this particularity belongs within that substantial connection is not explicit (*SL* §§1408–1409). The **hypothetical** judgment makes dependence explicit: if A is, B is. It asserts the necessary connection, not the separate actuality of either term. Their identity is still an inner relation between distinct existences; the **disjunctive** judgment makes the universal itself subject and its complete particularization predicate (*SL* §§1410–1411). Genus and mutually excluding species now constitute one articulated content. Hegel distinguishes this **necessary disjunction** from empirically collected species, whose apparent completeness is not guaranteed by their collection (*SL* §§1412–1416). The achievement claimed is therefore an immanent division of the kind, not a list certified exhaustive by its syntax.

**The judgment of the Concept.** Completed disjunction contains both the identity of the genus and the negative unity of its excluding particulars. But the singular is not yet explicitly determined as the unity of that universal nature and its particular constitution: this further determination is what Hegel requires next (*SL* §§1417–1421). He disputes the account of modality as the copula's value only in relation to thought: on his account, the judgment has the Concept in its own determinateness as its basis, rather than the Concept as related to contingent thinking (*SL* §§1418–1419). The question becomes whether the singular's actuality corresponds to its **Concept**, not whether a new evaluative topic should be added to the old table. Its three forms progressively make that correspondence explicit.

*Assertoric*: "**The subject is a concrete individual in general, and the predicate expresses this same as the relation of its actuality, determinateness, or constitution to its Notion. (This house is bad, this action is good.)**" (*SL* §1422) The subject "**ought to be something**"; the predicate says whether it is. *Constitution* here renders ***Beschaffenheit***, the term §11–§13 established in the logic of being — a thing's alterable how-it-happens-to-be, as against its *Bestimmung*, what it is determined to be. We read this as a transformed return of that distinction: constitution is again distinguished from what the subject is determined to be, but Hegel's explicit counterpart here is its universal nature as what it ought to be. The contrast has become the subject's own internal division under its Concept, not a simple repetition of the earlier pair.

But asserted flatly, this judgment has no ground in itself, and Hegel presses the point without mercy: "**Accordingly, the assurance of the assertoric judgment is confronted with equal right by its contradictory. When one is assured that 'this action is good', then the opposite assurance that 'this action is bad', is equally justified.**" (*SL* §1424) Bare assurance meets bare assurance. Why? "**because the subject of the judgment is an immediate individual, in this abstraction it does not as yet possess posited within it the determinateness that should contain its relation to the universal Notion; thus the subject is still something contingent which may or may not conform to the Notion.**" (*SL* §1424) The subject was named but not characterized; nothing in "this action" says why it should be good.

*Problematic*: "**The judgment is therefore essentially problematic.**" (*SL* §1424) This is not merely our hesitation about the assertion. The subject's alterable **constitution**, distinguished from its universal nature, can conform or fail to conform to that nature. The problematic judgment makes this objective contingency explicit; the apodeictic must determine which constitution grounds the assessment (*SL* §§1425–1429).

*Apodeictic*: and the repair is not a stronger assertion but the **restoration of the missing content to the subject**. "**The subject of the apodeictic judgment (the house constituted so and so is good, the action constituted so and so is right) has within it, first, the universal, what it ought to be, and secondly, its constitution; this latter contains the ground why a predicate of the Notion judgement applies or does not apply to the whole subject, that is, whether the subject corresponds to its Notion or not.**" (*SL* §1430)

This is the chapter's destination and it should be read slowly. The subject is no longer a bare *this* but a *this so constituted*; it carries within itself both its concept (what it ought to be) and its constitution (how it actually is). The copula is now anything but abstract — it states that the constitution **grounds** the predicate, and *ground* is Hegel's own word for what the copula has become: it has "**now further developed itself into ground in general**" (*SL* §1433), which is why the resemblance to §22 remarked above is more than a loose analogy. The predicate has developed to match, being no longer the genus or the ought-to-be taken by itself but "**this correspondence that is the universality which constitutes the predicate of the apodeictic judgment**" (*SL* §1431). Judgment has become, in Hegel's phrase from a later retrospect, "**the apodeictic judgment — the consummation of the judgment**" (*SL* §1536).

Looking back, the development has changed **both the terms and their relation**, not successively attached richer predicates to an unchanged subject. Existence developed the predicate against the immediate singular; reflection developed the subject through *this*, *some*, and *all* in relation to an essential universal. Necessity made the substantial connection explicit, until in disjunction the genus itself occupied the subject place and its articulated species the predicate (*SL* §1411). **Particularity** was present in the negative judgment's predicate (§VI), in the reflective *some* only as an extension of the singular in external reflection (*SL* §1394), and in disjunction as the totality of species; it now appears as the constitution through which the singular's correspondence with its universal is grounded. The singular does not first acquire a constitution at this last stage. What is new is that the judgment explicitly posits this particular determination as the ground of the relation (*SL* §§1430–1435). The universal, particular, and singular have therefore not remained fixed beneath the changing judgment-forms: their further determination is the development that has given the copula its content.

The transition requires more than packing the universal, constitution, and singular thing into a richly described subject. Hegel requires **both extremes as the whole Concept**, with their relating particularity also **distinguished from them** as their mediation (*SL* §§1434–1435). The constitution must relate the singular to the universal through a universal connection of its own: why this constitution makes the thing conform, or fail to conform, to its Concept. Once that mediating determination is explicit as a **middle term**, the judgment becomes a **syllogism**. The new form is not defined by how many lines the sentence occupies, nor has judgment's division simply disappeared; its extremes are joined through an articulated mediation.

## VIII. Where This Meets the Sciences

Four places, stated with the same care about limits the corpus has used since §01.

**1. Observation reports and their evidential role.** §III distinguished the logical use of a report from a supposed guarantee of incorrigibility. A record can support inquiry through relations to scales, instruments, kinds, and other reports; those commitments can be examined and revised. Neurath's protocols make this a positive evidential practice, not a failed attempt at an infallible atom. The Hegelian question is what universality and mediation the report expresses. It does not follow that a report is useless until it explains its object, or secure in proportion as it commits itself to less.

**2. The ranking of predicates, and what classification is for.** Judgments form "a series of steps, the difference of which rests upon the logical significance of the predicate" (*Enc.* §171). Reporting a sensible mark, stating a relation across instances, and exhibiting a genus's immanent division are different logical tasks. But the mere use of a visible character does not assign an entire taxonomy to the first class: that character may serve as evidence within an explanation of kinds. §27's contrast of **merely external mark** and **principle** concerns this explanatory work, not a prohibition on sensible evidence. The logical demand is to show why the differences belong to the kind; which scientific account does that must be judged through its actual content.

Hegel makes a concrete judgment here. He calls the seven-colour disjunction "barbarous", treats violet and orange as mixtures, and proposes colour as "the concrete unity of light and dark" (*SL* §1414). On that premise, he requires a simple colour holding the opposition in equilibrium, contrasting relations of light and dark, and, because a natural phenomenon is involved, their indifferent neutrality. This is his positive principle of division, not merely a dislike of seven names. He also charges the procedure with showing "too little reflection even for empiricism" (*SL* §1414). Editorial notes 59–63 to the Russian edition identify the Newtonian target and Goethean background; that remains our intermediary for Goethe. Newton's own *Opticks* gives a more discriminating target ([1730 edition, Book I, Part I, definitions VII–VIII; Part II, propositions II and IV](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/33504/33504-h/33504-h.htm)). Homogeneous light is distinguished by equal refrangibility, not by a colour name alone. Newton describes a continuously varying spectrum and distinguishes **homogeneous orange** from orange made by mixing red and yellow: similar appearance does not establish the same constitution, and further refraction discriminates them. Hegel's rejection of orange as merely mixed therefore does not meet the optical distinction Newton actually draws. This does not derive the necessity of exactly seven divisions, vindicate every Newtonian claim, or settle Goethe's alternative. It does establish why the mixture objection is insufficient against this target: an experimentally specified difference is lost if both cases are classified from appearance alone. Hegel's demand for a principled division survives; his particular verdict must answer to that evidence, including by the standard he sets for empiricism, rather than receive immunity from its place in the Logic.

**3. Normative predicates in the sciences.** Our examples — "this heart is defective", "this specimen is malformed", "this proof is invalid", "this instrument is miscalibrated" — assess conformity to a standard rather than merely report a sensible quality. Their grounds differ. A proof can fail stated inferential requirements and an instrument a specified calibration standard without those requirements being deduced from the object's immanent self-development. These are genuine, bounded assessments, not arbitrary preferences or suspended judgments awaiting Hegel's ontology. His stronger question is whether the standard expresses the object's **own Concept**. For the organism, Life supplies his positive argument; §VI has distinguished that further warrant from the evidence supporting a functional assessment. The logical form of assessment is recognizable here without treating every standard as already proved immanent.

**4. The infinite judgment and category mistakes.** Hegel's "the understanding is not a table" (*SL* §1382) invites comparison with the later problem of **category mistakes**, notably Ryle's account. But there is no single doctrine under which all such sentences are grammatically sound and meaningless: syntactic, semantic, truth-valued, and pragmatic accounts compete ([SEP, “Category Mistakes,” §§1–3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/category-mistakes/)). The bounded resemblance is that grammatical form alone need not establish a fitting relation of terms. The difference matters just as much: Hegel concedes correctness to this **negative** assertion while denying it the developed relation required for judgment; a meaningless predication, or its negation, is not thereby the same case. His claim that infinite judgment results necessarily from the preceding forms, and his applications to crime and death, also require their own arguments. This is a retrospective comparison, not a claim of influence or identity of doctrines.

## IX. Three Misreadings, Answered Directly

**"Hegel derives Kant's table of judgments."** Not in the sense of vindicating it as it stands: Hegel calls Kant's division unsatisfactory. But he credits both the demand for system and the guiding perception that judgment-forms express the forms of the logical Idea. The qualitative, quantitative, relational, and modal groups remain recognizable in his four classes. What he seeks to establish anew is their ground and necessary development: Being, Essence (doubled), and the Concept supply the systematic articulation, while the logical significance of the predicate distinguishes the grades. §V separates this work from the question of provenance. Inherited grouping is neither a sufficient derivation nor evidence that derivation has failed.

**"The truth/correctness distinction licenses calling inconvenient facts untrue."** It does not, and the text forecloses it. "That a person is sick … may certainly be correct." Correctness is never denied to a correct statement. Truth in the technical sense is a property of the **object** in relation to its concept; and where Hegel does predicate it of a judgment, he is grading the judgment's *form* — whether that form is adequate to what it purports to express — which is why he can call the positive and the negative judgment alike untrue ("**The negative judgment is as little a true judgment as the positive**", *SL* §1382) and the apodeictic "**the truth of the judgment in general**" (*SL* §1431). What the distinction never does is re-rank a correct statement as a worse report of fact; and the concept in question is the object's own, subject to §27's insistence that a concept is a principle of development and not a classifier's preference. Where no such concept can be shown, the distinction has no application and invoking it is bluff.

**"Judgment is a mental act, so this is psychology after all."** This is §27's misreading in a new place. Hegel's plant, crime, and death examples apply judgment to objective determinations; "all things are a judgment" (*Enc.* §167) states the general claim. It is not disproved by observing that people judge, nor proved by redescribing any process as a division. Hegel's complaint is that an account confined to combining representations has not exhibited the Concept's own differentiation. Its further warrant, not a denial of psychological acts, is the issue.

## X. What the Method Did Here — Observations

**The transition arose within the achieved determination.** In Hegel's argument, singularity makes the moments self-subsistent while leaving their essential relation intact. Judgment is that tension, not an externally supplied combination or the simple disappearance of unity. Its particular mediation matters more than a general formula that every success breaks its form (§I).

**Negation preserved its ground.** The negative judgment retains a presupposed universal sphere while negating the predicate's specific determination. The resulting particularity advances beyond the positive judgment, but still fails to express the determinate singular. Neither the retained sphere nor the next step follows from the word *not* alone (§VI).

**The crisis had a positive result.** Infinite judgment exhibits both the loss of the immediate relation and its terms' mediated return into themselves. Too much difference and bare identity each abolish judgment's form; the reflected terms are the content retained in the next class. The positive mediation must be reconstructed, not supplied by analogy with §13 or §21.

**The ranking criterion concerns judgment's own content** — "the logical significance of the predicate" (*Enc.* §171). It must be followed through the subject's reciprocal determination, not used as a scale pasted onto fixed terms. Kant's different classificatory dimensions answer a distinguishable question (§V). Hegel's demand for development goes beyond that classification; it does not refute its bounded results merely by doing so.

**The prior Logic supplies a determinate architecture, not its own certificate.** Being, Essence, and Concept reappear in transformed judgment-forms. The distinction between reflection's essential relation and necessity's substantial ground gives the doubling of Essence content. Whether the whole architecture is necessary and exhaustive must nevertheless be shown through the transitions, not inferred from the recurrence of those names.

**A limit, specified.** The reconstruction establishes what the principal transitions are meant to achieve and identifies their mediations; it does not certify every necessity by reproducing the scheme. §27's local question about the triad's derivation remains distinct from its subsequent work. Within this chapter, the stronger adequacy demanded of ordinary predication and the step from indeterminate *some* to *some not* are identifiable points requiring argument, not generic reservations. Historical borrowing neither establishes nor defeats that argument. The positive achievements also remain: the terms develop reciprocally, allness is distinguished from a substantial kind, and the assessment's ground becomes an explicit middle relating both extremes.

## XI. What Comes Next

- **§29 — The Syllogism.** Judgment's extremes are related through a middle distinguished from both, not simply through additional content inside the subject. "Everything is a Syllogism" (*Enc.* §181) announces Hegel's objective claim. The traditional apparatus of moods and figures must be faced directly, including the mathematical syllogism and the distinction between valid inference and the immanent development of its forms. The syllogisms of existence, reflection, and necessity culminate in the disjunctive syllogism and the realization of subjectivity as **objectivity**.

- **Beyond.** Mechanism, chemism, and teleology — the last of these where the concept of an end returns as an objective category and the long argument with external purposiveness is developed; then **Life**, where the warrant deferred in §VIII above is addressed; then Cognition, and the **Absolute Idea**, in which the method becomes its own content; and the Idea's release into Nature.

## Coda

A table can distinguish judgment's forms and support valid inferences without deriving those forms. Hegel asks the further question: why does the Concept divide into judgment, and why must judgment's determinations develop? The table's restricted achievement survives that question; its success within an accepted form is not an answer to the form's genesis.

The chapter has reconstructed Hegel's answer: singularity makes the Concept's moments self-subsistent, while their original unity persists as the demand for mediation. Negation preserves and transforms that relation; its two-sided infinite result yields reflected terms; reflection advances from an externally collected universal toward substantial unity; and judgment of the Concept makes conformity depend on the subject's constitution. The predicate is no longer merely a detached quality but, in Hegel's image, "**as it were, the soul of the subject, by which the subject, as the body of this soul, is characterised through and through**" (*Enc.* §172). These are determinate changes of content, not new labels. Their reconstruction also makes the outstanding questions precise rather than declaring the ladder proved in advance.

At the close, the particular constitution grounds the singular's relation to its universal; as an explicit middle, it must relate to and be distinguished from both extremes. The result is not simply a judgment with a longer subject. What §27 called the Concept's downfall has led to a form in which the connection is articulated as mediation.

The name of that form is the syllogism, and the school logic has a table for it too.
