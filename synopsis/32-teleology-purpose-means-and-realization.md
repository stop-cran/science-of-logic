# Teleology: Purpose, Means, and Realization

*The twenty-third installment of Section III — the sixth in the **Doctrine of the Concept**, and the third and final chapter of **Objectivity**. Chemism left the Concept with an independent standing as **end** (*Zweck*), but still confronting the objectivity through which it must realize itself. **Teleology** (*Teleologie*) develops this opposition through the **subjective end**, the **means** (*Mittel*), and the **realized end**. The end's own claim to self-determination makes its merely inward existence inadequate; its activity must become objective without losing itself in its result. Mechanical and chemical processes supply the mediation, not an obstacle that purpose miraculously bypasses. The **cunning of reason** names this maintenance through the objects' own activity, while the enduring means can disclose more rational content than the passing satisfaction it serves. Yet successful production does not by itself overcome **external purposiveness**: the product remains material or means for another end, and the presumed separation of purpose from objectivity generates a regress within the mediation itself. Hegel's further argument makes explicit the unity already operative in the process, preserving means and mediation while sublating their external standing. Its result is the **Idea**, not the animation of an artefact or the discovery of a designer behind nature. Kant's distinction between regulative inquiry and constitutive explanation is retained as a genuine challenge to Hegel's criticism. Prebiotic research supplies a bounded comparison: experimentally producing a compound and explaining a geochemically plausible route to it are connected but different achievements; neither alone establishes living organization.*

## I. The End Has Become Independent — and Therefore One-Sided

§31 did not conclude that chemistry needs a mind to complete its reactions. Its result concerned the standing of the determining unity. The chemical objects and processes could no longer count as independently given terms whose connection merely happened afterwards. Their differentiation and reunion disclosed one mediation; the Concept became explicit as its determining principle. This was **end**, no longer submerged in immediate objectivity.

But the liberation had a limit. The end now stands **over against** a world of objects. Its independence seems secured precisely by separating it from the material in which it must become actual. Teleology begins with this new inadequacy, not with the introduction of an empirical planner after the appearance of chemical compounds (*SL* §1600; *Enc.* §§204–205).

An **end** is not simply an event that comes last. It is a determination that governs an activity and is to maintain itself through the activity's objective result. An externally observed sequence can finish without having had an end in this sense. Conversely, an end can be operative before it is achieved. The question is how its determining activity and its objective realization belong together.

This is why **subjective** cannot here mean merely imaginary, nor **objective** merely perceptible. The subjective end is the Concept in an independent but one-sided standing; objectivity is the totality developed through §29–§31, not raw material newly introduced from outside the Logic. Human planning is a familiar realization of the first relation, not the source from which the category is inferred. Hegel expressly warns against restricting end to its form as a mental representation (*Enc.* §204).

The primary sequence is *SL* §§1593–1630: an introductory confrontation of mechanism and teleology, followed by **the subjective end**, **the means**, and **the realized end**. *Enc.* §§204–212 provides the shorter development. This installment reads the supplied Miller and Wallace transcriptions through the entry into the Idea, not freshly collated printed editions. Our exposition separates the successful realization of a finite end from the further overcoming of external purposiveness; Hegel develops both within his final subsection.

## II. Mechanism, Design, and Kant's Distinction

Hegel begins by rejecting a familiar way of settling the dispute: first picture the world, then ask whether mechanism or an intelligent designer explains that picture more conveniently. The concepts themselves must be examined. If they are opposed, their mere occurrence does not establish their equal and independent adequacy; the question is whether one develops into the other, or both into a more adequate determination (*SL* §§1593–1594).

His criticism nevertheless preserves a substantial achievement of mechanical investigation. It seeks to comprehend nature through nature's own determinations rather than explain an arrangement by an extramundane intention. Hegel recognizes its striving to grasp nature as a whole not requiring an external supplement. He even credits mechanism with a consciousness of freedom in comparison with a teleology that elevates trivial uses into absolute purposes (*SL* §1595). A philosophical account that dismissed this achievement as mere blindness would miss his own contrast.

The weakness of such **external purposiveness** (*äußere Zweckmäßigkeit*) is not merely that its proposed designer cannot be observed. It makes the thing's significance depend upon a purpose foreign to its nature. In the *Encyclopaedia* illustration, explaining the cork-tree by the corks needed for wine-bottles confuses an actual human use with the tree's own determining principle (*Enc.* §205 Addition). The use is real; its elevation into an explanation of the tree is the illicit step.

Hegel credits **Kant** with restoring **internal purposiveness** (*innere Zweckmäßigkeit*) and thereby opening the Concept of life and the Idea. He also recognizes the significance of reflective judgment, which seeks a universal rather than merely subsuming a particular under one already given. His objection is that treating mechanism and teleology as subjective maxims leaves their own truth and connection insufficiently investigated (*SL* §§1597–1599). This is the demand his subsequent development must meet.

But Kant's position cannot fairly be reduced to switching between contradictory assertions as convenience dictates. In the *Critique of Judgment* §§69–71, he distinguishes **regulative principles of investigation** from **constitutive principles of the possibility of objects**. Investigating mechanical explanation as far as one can does not assert that only mechanical production is possible; judging organized products through purposes does not prove that an intelligent cause produced them. In §78, Kant both insists on retaining mechanical investigation and subordinates its means to purposive judging without claiming theoretical knowledge of their supersensible common ground ([Bernard translation, 1914](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/48433/48433-h/48433-h.htm)).

**Our judgment of Hegel's objection is therefore bounded.** His demand to investigate the categories' objective unity is not answered merely by exhibiting the maxims' necessity for our judging. But neither does that demand show that Kant's distinguished maxims contradict one another in the same way as constitutive assertions. The distinction does real work: it prevents a requirement of our judging from being passed off as knowledge of how an object was produced. Hegel must establish the stronger unity against Kant's claim that its common ground remains theoretically indeterminable, not obtain it by cancelling that distinction in advance. His contribution cannot be that he alone retained mechanism within purposive inquiry; Kant already does so. It must lie in the proposed derivation of their relation.

## III. The Subjective End: Its Own Inwardness Becomes a Defect

The **subjective end** (*subjektiver Zweck*) contains the moments of the Concept. As **universal**, it is self-identical activity; in **particularizing** itself, it gives itself determinate content; as **singular**, it is the negative unity that determines itself and turns towards objectivity. These are not three things subsequently collected. The determining unity specifies itself, and its specification includes the distinction between what is inwardly determined and what is to be made objective (*SL* §§1601–1605; *Enc.* §§206–207).

The end consequently differs from a force awaiting solicitation or a cause whose effect initially confronts it as another. What is to result is already its own determination. This is a logical priority, not an effect travelling backwards in time. The process is governed by a content that is to remain identifiable through its realization, rather than become an unrelated outcome. Hegel does not deny causal efficacy; he argues that force and cause state only incomplete sides of the end's relation (*SL* §§1601–1603).

Its **finitude is twofold**. First, the content is limited and given: this thing is to be made, this want satisfied. The form of self-determination does not make every chosen content rational in itself. Second, an objective world stands presupposed as material and condition. The end's determining activity is therefore not yet the determination of the whole relation; it depends upon something whose standing it initially accepts as independent (*SL* §1604; *Enc.* §205).

The contradiction is internal to the end's own claim. It is not content to be a private picture: it is a determining unity that is to have objective validity. Yet, as merely subjective, it lacks the very objectivity belonging to its realization. Its activity must therefore negate **both** the object's merely presupposed independence and its own merely inward standing. Realization is not the conquest of an outside by an unchanged inside; it is also the overcoming of the end's deficient form (*SL* §1605).

Hegel names the outgoing self-determination **resolve** (*Entschluss*). Resolve gives the end a determinate direction, but does not conjure its object into existence. The object first brought under that direction is **means**. The immediate result is thus an incipient objectification, not yet the whole accomplished end.

## IV. The Means: Why the Middle Is More Than a Tool

The end cannot remain on one side while its realization happens on the other. It must unite itself with objectivity through a middle that belongs to both. The **means** (*Mittel*) is an object determined by the end and capable, as an object, of acting upon another object. Its double standing makes the syllogistic relation possible (*SL* §§1606–1608):

**Subjective end — means and purposive activity — objective realization.**

This recalls §29's problem of the **middle**, now in objectivity rather than merely in the exposition of subjective syllogism. The middle must actually unite the extremes; naming something a means cannot confer the required capacity upon it. Its objective constitution enables its action, while its relation to the end makes that action a means to this result rather than an indifferent occurrence.

The middle is therefore not exhausted by an implement lying ready for use. Hegel distinguishes the **activity**, the **object serving as means**, and the **determination of that object by the end** which connects them. The middle itself becomes an articulated mediation. In finite purposiveness, however, activity and implement still appear externally joined: the object does not have this particular end simply by being the object it is (*SL* §§1609–1611; *Enc.* §208).

Hegel's claim that the object has no power of resistance against the end belongs to this argument, and must not be silently replaced by the weaker observation that some tools are usable. He grounds it in the object being the Concept **in itself**, whereas the end is the Concept explicitly determining itself; the preceding mechanical and chemical developments have already undermined the object's absolute self-subsistence (*SL* §1610). On those premises, purpose is not an alien substance trying to penetrate something wholly foreign to it.

That is a claim about the object's categorial standing, not an experimental guarantee that a particular intention can overcome any material resistance. Indeed, the next paragraph retains the means' externality and relative independence: its immediate connection with the end has not yet removed the presupposition (*SL* §1611). To infer universal practical mastery from the first claim would erase the second and substitute the efficacy of any finite aim for the asserted identity of Concept and objectivity.

## V. Realization and the Cunning of Reason

The means now acts upon the object in which the end is to be realized. This is the second premise of the teleological syllogism, and its process is **mechanical or chemical**. Purpose does not substitute an immaterial force for the object's determinate operations. Those operations are retained within a relation in which their result answers to an end (*SL* §§1612–1613; *Enc.* §209).

Hegel calls the mediation the **cunning of reason** (*List der Vernunft*). Instead of entering an immediate struggle with the object, the end interposes another object and lets objects act upon one another. The means bears the wear; the end maintains its determination through their interaction (*SL* §1614). The point is not that reason escapes objective conditions, but that it works **through** them rather than needing to suspend them.

The means can consequently have a significance exceeding the passing satisfaction it serves. Hegel's comparison is the plough: its use embodies a power that persists beyond an immediately consumed enjoyment (*SL* §1615). This does not assert an indestructible tool; the previous paragraph expressly exposed the means to attrition. Nor does it make instrumental efficiency the supreme good. Hegel has just distinguished the rational form of mediation from the finite content of an end, which need not itself be rational. A durable capacity can serve a poor purpose.

In the realization, the end is not merely sheltered behind the means; it is the determining content maintained throughout the activity. What was first specified subjectively now has objective existence. Hegel can therefore say that the end is also the beginning: the outcome realizes the determination governing the process (*SL* §§1616–1619; *Enc.* §210). No material result is thereby supposed to exist fully formed before its production.

The *Encyclopaedia* Addition extends the cunning of reason to **divine providence**, where human passions accomplish an end different from the agents' own (*Enc.* §209 Addition). That theological extension belongs to Hegel's account; it is not established merely by showing how an implement mediates an action. The logical structure, its practical realization, and that larger interpretation require distinguishable warrants.

## VI. Why a Successfully Produced Object Can Still Be Only a Means

The end has become objective. Why does teleology not stop here?

Because **successful execution and the overcoming of external purposiveness are not the same achievement**. A product can embody its maker's purpose while remaining indifferent, in its own constitution, to that particular use. The unity imposed on its parts is purposive, but its relation to the material remains external. The result can therefore become a means or material for another end (*SL* §1620; *Enc.* §211).

Hegel's examples are a house and a clock. They are ends relative to the tools that produced them and means relative to sheltering or timekeeping. Nothing about occupying the last position in a manufacturing sequence makes the product an **objective end** in the stronger sense now at issue. The designation remains relative to another activity. This is not the discovery that houses cannot shelter or clocks cannot tell time; it is the failure of those achievements to give the end an internally adequate objectivity (*SL* §§1621–1622).

Hegel also emphasizes their exposure to pressure, chemical alteration, friction, and consumption. Here the concrete illustration needs discrimination. The pressure borne by a house's beams belongs to their load-bearing function; it is not simply deterioration. Wear, by contrast, exhibits the perishability of the medium and can accompany the performance of its function. Hegel's claim that the object fulfils its end only by being worn away therefore overstates what these different cases establish. His logical argument concerns the object's status as material for an end external to it; it cannot be supported by a general physical equation of usefulness with destruction (*SL* §1622).

The deeper regress lies **inside the mediation**, not merely in a series of further uses. If end and object are absolutely independent, their first connection requires a means. But this means would already have to be an object united with an end; another means is therefore required to effect precisely that union, and so indefinitely. Likewise, if means and material are absolutely self-subsistent, they can be united only through a third, which reproduces the same requirement. The premises presuppose the unity the conclusion is supposed to establish (*SL* §§1620–1624).

This is §29's difficulty in a transformed role: immediate premises cannot ground the mediated totality simply by being asserted. Here the issue is not a missing proposition but the supposed externality of the activities and objects themselves. Adding more implements does not repair the form of a relation that continually presupposes a further external connection.

External purposiveness thus reaches a double limit. Its limited content cannot supply the Concept's adequate objective existence; and its mediation requires the unity of terms it presupposes as independently complete. The next step must exhibit that unity **within the mediation already developed**, not append a final instrument or an observer who sees the whole.

## VII. The Result Is the Idea — Not an Improved Artefact

Hegel does not answer the regress by jumping to an organism as an unquestionable counterexample. He returns to what the end, the means, and the objective process have already done. The closing movement (*SL* §§1625–1630; *Enc.* §212) is difficult because the Concept is relating to itself within objectivity. Its first union with an object already contains the return into itself that appeared to belong only to the final result; the first sublation contains the second, and conversely (*SL* §§1627–1628). The opposition being sublated is the end's own presupposition, not merely an observer's way of understanding it.

**First, making an object a means already realizes the end.** It is not simply preparation occurring before any union of Concept and objectivity. The end has objective existence in the means and the activity that employs it. The demand for an additional bridge arises only if this actual mediation again presupposes two self-subsistent sides needing connection. Hegel grounds the first union in the object's already developed conceptual nature, not in an inexplicable capacity of a mental image to move matter (*SL* §§1625, 1627).

**Second, the objective process is the mediation's own activity, not a foreign event between its terms.** One object acts upon another; the immediacy of the material is altered, while the means itself is employed, altered, or consumed. For Hegel this is a second negation: it overcomes not only the initial object's immediacy but also the provisional standing of the means as an object merely subsumed under an end. Objectivity returns as the end's realized existence, not merely as something standing outside it (*SL* §1626).

**Third, means and realized end cease to be externally fixed ranks.** The means already contains objective realization; the realized end preserves means and mediation as its reality. Hegel's result is that "**in the fulfilled end, means and mediation are preserved**" (*SL* §1629), not that mediation has been discarded once a product arrives. When §1626 says that the means and mediation vanish, what vanishes is the means' separate standing as objectivity only immediately subordinated to the end, and mediation's standing as a relation between external terms. The later formulation makes their preservation explicit.

**Our reconstruction of the warrant** is this: the contradiction arose because realization required a unity that the alleged independence of its terms excluded. But that unity is not merely postulated to solve the difficulty. It has been exhibited in the end's own objectification and in objectivity's alteration through its own processes. The subjective end, the means, and the process each cease to be an isolated term and prove to contain the whole mediation under a distinct determination (*SL* §§1627–1629). A further external bridge would abstract again from the very unity it was supposed to effect.

On the premises of the preceding development, this warrants Hegel's **internal end-relation** and **objective end** (*SL* §1625): the opposition of subjectivity and objectivity is preserved within their unity, not as **absolute externality**. His positive conclusion goes further than the observation that plans sometimes succeed: the Concept is the self-determining identity of the objective totality, retaining differentiation within its unity (*SL* §1630). The content is no longer simply a limited aim given to the form from elsewhere; the Concept's own determining activity is its content (*Enc.* §212). That is the **Idea** (*Idee*), the unity of Concept and objectivity.

The change of logical standing must not be mistaken for an empirical metamorphosis. A manufactured chair has not become alive because the syllogism of its production has been understood. The finite product remains finite; what has been overcome in the categorial development is the standing of subjectivity and objectivity as independently complete terms. Hegel's stronger conclusion depends on his account of objectivity as the Concept in itself and on the development that makes this identity explicit. This is the substantive disagreement with Kant's §78: Hegel claims determinate theoretical knowledge of the objective unity whose ground Kant regards as theoretically indeterminable for our understanding. Successful intervention alone neither settles that disagreement nor supplies the organism's immanent organization. The latter has still to be developed as **Life**.

Nor is the Idea a motionless coincidence of two things. Its identity exists through the differentiation and return whose mediation the result preserves. The *Encyclopaedia* calls the Idea essentially a process (*Enc.* §215). The opening of the larger work's next section likewise rejects an Idea eternally remaining an unrealized ought (*SL* §§1631–1634). This is Hegel's ontological claim, not merely a proposal to improve our descriptions.

Its theological reach must also remain visible. The Addition to *Enc.* §212 says that the absolute Good is already accomplished as well as eternally accomplishing itself. A reconstruction that retains the criticism of external mediation without that claim must name the difference: it cannot turn Hegel's achieved objective unity into an indefinite programme of better approximations and present the replacement as his conclusion. Conversely, the success of a finite purposive action does not prove that everything actual satisfies an arbitrary human standard or that every existing arrangement is good.

## VIII. Where This Meets Scientific Investigation

Scientific practice gives a concrete case of **external purpose that is legitimate in its own sphere**. An investigator arranges apparatus and conditions to produce, separate, or measure something. The purpose governs the arrangement; the process succeeds only through the actual capacities of instruments and materials. Explaining the arrangement by its experimental aim does not require attributing that aim to each reaction occurring within it.

This also fixes a limit on the warrant supplied by success. Producing a target compound can establish that a specified procedure works under specified conditions. It does not by itself explain how those conditions arise without the investigator, or establish that this route occurred historically. These are different questions, not reasons to discard the experimental achievement. Purpose organizes the inquiry; it does not decide in advance what the evidence warrants.

The distinction is particularly instructive in **prebiotic chemistry**. The account [*Do-Nothing Prebiotic Chemistry: Chemical Kinetics as a Window into Prebiotic Plausibility*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11713876/) asks how experimentally established chemistry can proceed without direct intervention by a chemist. Its Conspectus, Introduction, and implementation sections distinguish suitable boundary conditions from the likelihood of reactions occurring in sequence. They propose measurements of rate constants and reaction-network models as means of investigating that difference. This is an existing scientific programme, not a task that philosophy has discovered on behalf of an otherwise unreflective chemistry.

Our comparison concerns **the investigation of a presupposition**. Selecting convenient precursors is a defensible step in testing a reaction; an account of prebiotic plausibility must additionally explain their availability and the connected conditions under which the pathway can operate. Convenient ingredients are not yet a plausible sequence. The paper's explicit distinction between kinetic control and a sufficient condition for life also blocks a second shortcut: removing the chemist does not by itself establish living organization.

Hegel's distinction helps keep the questions apart. The external purpose belongs to the **experiment**; it cannot silently become a purpose attributed to the prebiotic environment. A natural route to the experimentally obtained result must be supported by chemical, geological, and historical evidence. The further question of an organization whose activities maintain the unity through which its members function belongs to the comparison with **Life**, developed next. Neither the logical transition nor the analogy supplies the missing empirical conditions, and self-maintenance would not mean independence from material and energy exchange.

The methodological gain is therefore not a permission to abandon mechanism when an explanation becomes difficult. It is a demand to determine **what the mechanism explains, how it enters the purposive activity, and which of that activity's conditions remain merely presupposed**. A bounded causal account can be correct and valuable without settling the broader question. Conversely, giving the broader question a categorial name does not answer it.

## IX. Three Misreadings, Answered Directly

**1. Teleology puts a conscious designer behind nature.** Not in the derivation developed here. The end emerges from the preceding forms of objectivity; Hegel expressly distinguishes it from a mere mental representation and criticizes the explanation of nature by externally assigned uses. His theological claims must be read where he makes them, not substituted for the local argument.

**2. Purpose replaces mechanical and chemical explanation.** Their processes are precisely the means of its realization. The contrast concerns their standing as independently sufficient forms, not the suspension of their operations. A determinate aim that cannot work through determinate objective conditions is not rescued by being called an end.

**3. A successful product, a feedback loop, or indefinite persistence is already the Idea.** None of these descriptions by itself establishes the required unity. A feedback mechanism can maintain an externally assigned aim; persistence can reproduce an inadequate organization. The logical result concerns the sublation of one-sided subjectivity and objectivity, and its concrete realization requires a further account. The transition to Life is not an honorary title conferred on a well-designed machine.

## X. What the Method Did Here — Observations

**1. It found the defect in the achievement.** Chemism liberated the determining principle; the end's independent standing then disclosed its own one-sidedness. The next movement did not deny the earlier result. It made the result answer for what its achieved form still excluded.

**2. It tested the mediation, not only the endpoints.** The means was not accepted as a ready-made bridge. Its own unity of objective capacity and purposive determination became the problem. The regress exposed a presupposition within the purposive activity itself rather than an insufficient number of steps.

**3. It returned to an earlier form with a changed function.** Mechanism and chemism now operate within purposive mediation; the syllogism's critique of immediate premises now concerns the activities and objects constituting that mediation. This is **developing-out-of**, in §06's sense, not a repetition of the same example or the importation of a richer category from elsewhere.

**4. It distinguished realization from adequacy.** An end can succeed without overcoming external purposiveness. The same distinction constrains our reading: a valid experiment is not yet a complete account of its conditions; a demonstrated limit is not yet the positive derivation of what supersedes it. The concluding argument has to exhibit the unity it claims, and remains answerable to the premises on which that exhibition depends.

## XI. What Comes Next

**§33 — The Idea and Life.** The Idea first has the form of immediacy: a living unity whose differentiated members are not merely implements collected for a purpose lying outside them. We will follow the living individual, its process with an external world, and the genus process. The question deferred from §28 — how the organism's own Concept supplies its standard — must be faced there, not assumed from the vocabulary of function.

**Beyond.** Cognition differentiates the unity again as knowing and its object, through the theoretical and practical Idea. The Absolute Idea will make the method itself explicit. Teleology has reached the threshold of that final sphere; it has not already completed it.

## Coda

The end seems most independent when it remains outside the process. Its realization shows the opposite: it can maintain itself only through objective mediation. The finite product still points beyond itself, but the mediation does not merely fail and begin again. It discloses that the end was already objective in its means, and that its objective result preserves the activity through which it is what it is. Hegel names the explicit unity **Idea**. The next question is how that unity lives.
