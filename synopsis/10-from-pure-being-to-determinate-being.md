# From Pure Being to Determinate Being

*The first installment of Section III — a close reading of the opening of the* Science of Logic, *re-traversing at full resolution the move from Being and Nothing through Becoming to* Dasein, *and showing why the abstract* Dasein *genuinely **is** — why it plays on its own field rather than reducing to pure Being all over again.*

---

## Section III: What This Section Does

Section II gave the *Logic* its first traversal — the bird's-eye picture of the entire circle, in which each sphere was characterized by its dominant mode of transition (passing-over in Being, shining-into in Essence, developing-out-of in Concept) and the major dialectical arc was sketched. That traversal earned the right to call the *Logic* a single self-developing whole. But it bought that right at a cost: dozens of times we said things like "and once Becoming has settled into Determinate Being, we have a *something*," compressing into a half-sentence a movement whose internal articulation occupies pages of Hegel's own text. The IOUs flagged at the close of Section II (the question of the calculus, the substance-to-Concept transition, the syllogistic forms, the self-articulation of the Absolute Idea, the alienation question) are the most conspicuous of these compressions — but the entire Section II is a sequence of such compressions, hardly any of which were fully unfolded.

Section III is the deeper traversal, in which we slow down to the pace of the actual moves. We do not aim at encyclopedic completeness — Hegel's own Greater Logic remains the close work, and no synopsis can substitute for it — but we do aim at a fidelity that Section II deliberately did not aim at: every "must" in the text earned in the text, every "passes over" exhibited as a passage, every "sublated" displayed as what it positively retains and what it negatively cancels.

The pay-off is not just better understanding of Hegel. It is something the reader can carry away as a practice: retaining the Understanding's work of fixing determinations while examining their grounds and limits, and asking whether their own content requires them to change (§03). The pure logical argument can train this discipline; it cannot decide in advance whether a contradiction in a particular science is real or productive, nor what that science's evidence and arguments warrant (§02).

A word about what Section III is *not*. It is not a commentary on Hegel — not an exegesis whose office would be to track the surface of the text passage by passage and adjudicate the secondary literature. The aim is rather to **do** the close reading, in our own words, with attention to what Hegel actually argues and to the contemporary reader who is more likely to misunderstand the argument by importing one of two opposed bad habits: either a logical-positivist suspicion that the moves are wordplay, or a hermeneutic indulgence that takes the wordplay to be the moves. Each major step will be developed with anticipation of the most common confusions; Hegelian terminology will be used and explained but not multiplied; the German will appear where it earns its keep.

We begin where the *Logic* begins, and where §02 began in an introductory form. What follows is a re-traversal of that opening at the resolution Section III requires.

## I. Why Begin Again

A reader who has worked through Section II might reasonably ask: have we not already done this? §02 gave the move from Being through Nothing and Becoming to Dasein, and §VI of that installment explicitly distinguished the genuine dialectical step from its schoolroom caricature. What more is there to do?

The answer is not that §02 merely announced a proof: it already gave the opening argument and distinguished its immanent movement from an externally imposed schema. But its task was introductory. Here we linger over what that account compressed: the sameness and distinction of Being and Nothing, the two directions of Becoming, and the different unity in which its moments survive its sublation. An argument is not exhausted by knowing its sequence; its warrant must be retraced at each step.

Returning to Hegel's *Logic* is in this respect like returning to a piece of music whose broad structure one knows: familiarity with the sequence allows attention to fall more precisely on its internal articulations. The second traversal does not supply movement to an inert outline; it follows more closely the movement the first traversal already began to exhibit.

What is in question is also more than narrowly philosophical. The *Logic* purports to be the science of the very forms in which anything whatever can be thought. If those forms are indeed identical with the forms in which anything whatever can **be** — Hegel's deepest claim — then to follow the *Logic* slowly is to acquire, at the level of pure thought, the discipline that mature science is supposed to embody at the level of its concrete content. The test is whether this discipline sharpens inquiry into concrete determinations without replacing the evidence and arguments proper to each science (§§02–03).

## II. Pure Being, Once More — and What It Costs

Take the determination *Being*, with absolute strictness: *is*, without specification of what is. Withhold every quality, every distinction, every "such-and-such," every "thus-and-so." This is a demand on the reader, not a derivation of Being by stripping a given thing of its properties: the beginning is indeterminate before any further determination is posited (*Enc.* §86 Addition; §02).

It is essential at this point to resist three opposed temptations.

The first is the temptation to think that pure Being is *some* particular thing or kind of thing — the totality of beings, the supreme being, the substratum of all that is, raw existence as opposed to essence. Each of these is a determinate thought, with its own internal structure, and bringing any of them into the beginning is bringing a content. Pure Being, as Hegel uses the term, is none of them. It is the bare thought of *is*-ness, with every further specification withheld.

The second temptation is the opposite: to think that pure Being is just an empty word, and that emptiness is a defect that disqualifies it as a beginning. "If your beginning is empty," the objection runs, "then it cannot ground anything." But this objection mistakes what is being asked of the beginning. The beginning of a *science of all determinations* cannot carry any *further* determination — for then it would presuppose what it must produce. This does not place the beginning outside the determinations: pure Being is itself a thought-determination, the least one there can be (§02). The emptiness of Being is not a failure to be a beginning; it is the only thing a genuine beginning could be.

The third temptation, subtler, is to think that pure Being is a *presupposition* — that one has to assume "thought can think being" in order to start. But the beginning is not the *assumption* that thought thinks being; it is the **act** of considering thought without presupposing a determinate relation between thought and being. Even the assumption "thought can think being" is, strictly, a *content* — a relation between thought and being — and so cannot be smuggled in at the beginning. What is thought here is the bare *is* itself, without any specification of what does the thinking, of what is thought, or of how. To stand at this point requires nothing to be assumed; it requires only that one let the bare *is* stand.

Withholding further determination does not mean speaking or thinking without categories. These already operate in ordinary language and thought (§06); the *Logic* investigates their necessity rather than accepting their familiar use as their justification (*SL* §34). Words such as "content" help articulate the present thought, but the later-developed relations of the corresponding categories must not be borrowed as grounds for its transition. Such language cannot substitute for showing what follows from the determination under examination (*SL* §193; see §VI below).

As §02 noted, Hegel presents the beginning as mediated by the *Phenomenology of Spirit*'s account of pure knowing, yet immediate within the logical exposition. The *Logic* also permits entry through the resolve to consider thought as such (*SL* §98). Sections I and II of this synopsis prepare the reader for that task; they do not replace the phenomenological argument or the systematic grounding. Here we adopt the standpoint of pure thought and attend to pure Being itself.

## III. Pure Nothing, and Why It Is Pure Being

Attend to pure Being. Try to think it.

Notice immediately what happens. There is nothing to attend to. No quality to seize, no relation to traverse, no determination on which to fix. The thought has no internal structure to inhabit. To think pure Being is to be in possession of a thought that, when one looks for what one is thinking, finds — nothing.

This is the first move, and it is essential not to misdescribe it. The argument is *not*: "Being is empty; Nothing is empty; therefore Being and Nothing are the same." That would be external comparison — measuring two thoughts against each other on the criterion of their content. Hegel does not measure; he reports what happens *within* the attempt to think pure Being. What happens is that the attempt collapses into the thought of pure Nothing. Not that pure Being *means* nothing in the sense of being meaningless, but that pure Being, when thought, **is** the very thought of Nothing.

The same in reverse. Attempt to think pure Nothing. Not a particular absence (the absence of this, the absence of that — those are already determinate, and so already richer than the beginning), but pure indeterminacy as such, total emptiness. To think this — actually to think it, not merely to entertain the word — is, again, to be in possession of a thought with no internal structure, no content, no determination. Hegel initially speaks of Nothing as existing in our intuiting or thinking, but immediately corrects the expression: "or rather it is empty intuition and thought itself, and the same empty intuition or thought as pure being" (*SL* §133). The return to Being is not inferred merely from the occurrence of an act of thinking Nothing. It concerns what is thought: pure Nothing, without further determination, is the same empty thinking as pure Being.

An objection worth testing here is that the exposition exploits an equivocation in the word "is." There is, the objection runs, a difference between *is* in the sense of *exists* and *is* in the sense of *is identical with*; the argument slides between them and so produces a paradox that is really a confusion.

The objection concerns the reasoning, not merely the content of the category; the beginning's indeterminacy does not by itself answer it. On the reading just given, however, the argument does not run: an act of thinking Nothing exists, therefore Nothing is identical with Being. It asks what content pure Nothing has when no further determination is admitted, and finds the same absence of determination as in pure Being. No inference from the existence of a thought to the identity of its content with Being performs the work. This answers that particular equivocation charge; whether the result warrants Becoming still requires the further argument.

The result is not identity alone. Pure Being and pure Nothing are not independently given thoughts that happen to share a property: neither supplies a content by which it could be held apart from the other. Yet Hegel equally insists that they are absolutely distinct and inseparable (*SL* §134). Their **content** is the same pure indeterminacy; their opposed **meanings** — utter affirmation and utter negation — have no determinate content to sustain their separation. The emptiness does not manufacture the opposition; it deprives each side of a footing independent of the other. Nor can the distinction simply be dropped: without it there would be no vanishing of either into its opposite. Their truth must contain both their unity and their distinction, rather than reduce them to a motionless identity.

This is also why the alleged unity cannot be explained away merely as a verbal trick disguising two independently given contents. No fixed qualitative difference has been supplied; nevertheless the opposed moments cannot be omitted. The substantive philosophical claim concerns their inability to subsist separately, not a licence to exchange their names.

## IV. Becoming as the Truth of Being and Nothing

What follows from Being's being Nothing and Nothing's being Being?

It does not follow that one is right and the other wrong; both are equally indeterminate, equally pure. Nor does it follow that we should pick a winner; that would be external arbitration. What follows is that the thought-determination we are dealing with is not actually either Being or Nothing taken in isolation — for neither of them, taken in isolation, can sustain itself — but their reciprocal passage. Hegel stresses that each *has passed over* into the other (*SL* §134): there is no first interval in which either subsists independently and only afterward begins to move.

This movement is itself a determination, and Hegel names it: **Becoming** (*Werden*).

Becoming is not a *thing* that joins Being to Nothing — as if Being and Nothing were two pre-existing items between which a relation called Becoming were established. Such an external picture would already have presupposed the determinations as separable, which is precisely what the move shows they are not. Becoming is, rather, the immanent structure that came to light when Being and Nothing were each pursued purely. It names this immediate vanishing of each into the other, not the mental effort by which we follow it.

Hegel distinguishes within Becoming two moments:

- **Coming-to-be** (*Entstehen*) — the passage of Nothing into Being. In this moment, Becoming is read from the side of Nothing: what is at issue is how Nothing is, in itself, the passage into Being.
- **Ceasing-to-be** (*Vergehen*) — the passage of Being into Nothing. Here Becoming is read from the side of Being: what is at issue is how Being is, in itself, the passage into Nothing.

These are not two separate movements that happen in sequence (first one comes-to-be, then it ceases-to-be); they are the **same** movement, the very restlessness of pure Being and pure Nothing, read in two different directions. To attempt to think them apart is to find that each contains the other: coming-to-be already contains ceasing-to-be (the Nothing-side of the passage), and ceasing-to-be already contains coming-to-be (the Being-side of the passage). They are inseparable as moments.

Becoming, then, is the first determination of the *Logic* that has **internal articulation**. Pure Being had no inside; pure Nothing had no inside; Becoming has an inside, structured by its two moments. It is also the first place where one can specify, in the *Logic*'s own resources, what *Aufhebung* — sublation — comes to. In Becoming:

- Being and Nothing are **preserved**: they are still present, as the two moments coming-to-be and ceasing-to-be.
- They are **negated** in their false independence: neither survives Becoming as a stable separate determination; each is now a moment of a more comprehensive thought.
- They are **lifted** into a richer determination: Becoming is more concrete than either, in the precise Hegelian sense that it contains determinations (the two moments) that Being and Nothing did not have.

This is the first concrete instance of *Aufhebung*, and the reader who has watched it carefully will recognize that the term is doing real work. It is not a vague gesture toward "synthesis" or "compromise" — Becoming is not a compromise between Being and Nothing, nor an average, nor an oscillation between two terms that would remain what they are. It is what comes to light when each of them, taken purely, is held to itself: the immanent movement that was already in the determination from the start.

A common worry at this point is that Becoming, having been arrived at by its own argument, is now itself just *another* fixed term — that we are simply piling up vocabulary, with no principled stopping-point. The worry would be apt if Becoming were treated as a final determination. It is not. The next move — to which we now turn — is precisely the demonstration that Becoming, taken with the same strictness as Being and Nothing were taken, cannot rest in itself either, and that it has its own sublation, its own truth in a further determination. Every finite determination in the *Logic* will undergo the same fate, and that restlessness is what the *Logic* makes systematic. With the Absolute Idea Hegel claims completion of logical form. He nevertheless describes the still-logical Idea as confined within pure thought and as the urge to sublate that confinement (*SL* §1816). Its release into Nature is an act of "absolute liberty" (*Enc.* §244), not another deficient logical category passing into its successor (*SL* §1817; §09). Extending finitude to that logical completion is this synopsis's own commitment, not Hegel's (§05; §09).

## V. The Vanishing of the Vanishing

Becoming has two moments — coming-to-be and ceasing-to-be — that are inseparable and reciprocally constitutive. Hold this as strictly as one held the earlier determinations.

When the two directions are taken with full equipoise, what results is in the first place Becoming itself. But they are not two forces cancelling each other from outside. In coming-to-be, Nothing passes over into Being — yet Being equally sublates itself and is transition into Nothing, which is ceasing-to-be. In ceasing-to-be, Being passes over into Nothing — yet Nothing is equally the opposite of itself, transition into Being, which is coming-to-be. "They are not reciprocally sublated — the one does not sublate the other externally — but each sublates itself in itself and is in its own self the opposite of itself" (*SL* §179).

The contradiction that undoes Becoming concerns its constitutive moments, **Being and Nothing**: Becoming *is* only through their distinction, yet they are present in it only as vanishing moments (*SL* §§180–181). Their vanishing therefore undoes Becoming itself. A union whose own movement destroys what it requires destroys itself.

Hegel formulates this with a striking phrase: what comes to an end is "the vanishing of becoming or **the vanishing of the vanishing** itself" (*SL* §180). The first vanishing was the vanishing of Being into Nothing and of Nothing into Being. The second vanishing is the cancellation of *that* vanishing — the cessation of the restless movement itself.

What remains when that movement has come to rest? *Not* simply Being, for the move that arrives here has gone through Becoming, and what arrives is no longer pure Being but Being-as-the-result-of-a-passage. *Not* simply Nothing, for the same reason. *Not* the movement itself, for it has cancelled itself. What remains is the *settled* outcome: a determinate immediate, Being with non-being retained in it as determinacy.

This is **Determinate Being** — *Dasein*.

It is worth pausing here at the objection that Hegel is *willing* the result — that he wants the oscillation to settle, and so it conveniently does. The objection is understandable: something seems to be *produced* rather than merely *found*. But what is being produced is not a new content imported from outside; it is the structure that the cancellation itself leaves behind. To say "the oscillation cancels itself" is already to say that what remains is not the oscillation but its settled outcome. Two things must be added, and Hegel adds both. The result cannot be pure Nothing: that "would only be a relapse into one of the already sublated determinations, not the resultant of *nothing and being*" (*SL* §182). And the result carries the form of Being — it is "the unity of being and nothing which has settled into a stable oneness… yet no longer as a determination on its own but as a determination of the whole" (*SL* §182), a being with its own non-being retained in it as determinacy. That outcome is *Dasein*. The Hegelian move is not an act of will; it registers what the self-cancellation retains: not pure Being or pure Nothing, but their unity in the form of Being.

Hegel glosses *aufheben* itself as twofold — "to preserve, to maintain, and equally… to cause to cease, to put an end to" (*SL* §185) — and in Becoming the word's ordinary third sense, of *raising up*, is at work alongside them. So *Aufhebung* here shows three faces: it cancels the pure separability of Being and Nothing (they are no longer two independent absolutes); it preserves them (as the moments coming-to-be and ceasing-to-be); and it lifts them into a determination richer than either (which contains both as moments). When Becoming itself undergoes a further sublation, preservation again changes the preserved moments. Being and Nothing now belong to a different unity: Being with non-being retained as determinateness, not an ongoing coming-to-be and ceasing-to-be. Hegel explicitly says that in *Dasein* they are differently determined moments (*SL* §187).

## VI. Determinate Being, and Why It Is on Its Own Field

We have arrived at *Dasein*. The question Section II compressed — and which Section III owes a full answer — is now to be put directly: why is the abstract *Dasein* genuinely **on its own field**? Why does it not simply collapse back into pure Being, of which it might seem to be a verbal variant?

The answer is that *Dasein* differs from pure Being in three respects, each of which is decisive.

**First, *Dasein* is immediate, but its immediacy is mediated.** Pure Being's immediacy was the immediacy of having no internal structure at all. *Dasein*'s immediacy is the immediacy of *the result*: it is what stands when the movement of Becoming has settled. Within the *Logic* nothing preceded pure Being; *Dasein*, by contrast, is *resulted* immediacy. Its mediation lies behind it; what is immediately present is determinateness, not the process of Becoming as such (*SL* §§191–193).

**Second, *Dasein* contains its negation as a moment of itself.** Pure Being had no internal articulation in which Being and Nothing could subsist as moments; thought purely, it vanished into Nothing. In *Dasein*, by contrast, non-being is retained in simple unity with Being, and this constitutes determinateness (*SL* §192). Negation is not imposed by an outside comparison: it belongs to what *Dasein* is. This is no longer the abstract opposition Being-vs-Nothing, but their determinate unity.

We have not yet developed the categories *Quality*, *Reality*, *Negation* in their full *Logic*-internal sense, and the next installment will do so. The introductory phrase "being-this-rather-than-that" (§02) anticipates that development; it must not be used as if a separate Something and Other had already been derived. What has been reached here is determinateness itself. Hegel expressly distinguishes this present content from reflections that indicate the course still to be exhibited (*SL* §193).

**Third, *Dasein* has an internal field of articulation.** This is what is meant by saying *Dasein* "plays on its own field." Pure Being admitted no further determination. *Dasein*, by contrast, contains determinateness whose affirmative and negative moments can now be developed as *Quality*, *Reality*, and *Negation*. Their development must proceed from this content, not import distinctions from outside. The next installment will exhibit it. The point now is that there is something to articulate, whereas pure Being offered no such internal field, only its vanishing into Nothing.

This is also what makes *Dasein* the **first concrete category that stands**, in the precise Hegelian sense. "Concrete," as §03 already noted, means in Hegel not "sensorily present" but "rich with internal determinations." Pure Being is the most *abstract* of all categories: it has no further determination. Becoming is already more concrete than Being or Nothing, but it subsists only as its own vanishing; *Dasein* is the first determination that is richer than its predecessors *and* remains — containing them as sublated moments and adding a determinacy of its own. Each subsequent category in the *Logic* will be more concrete than its predecessors in this same sense: not closer to sense-experience, but richer in the determinations it contains within itself (*SL* §§1809–1812).

A natural worry is that one might be tempted to think *Dasein* "is something" only by furnishing it surreptitiously with empirical content — by thinking, say, of a stone or a sound as the *Dasein* under consideration. This must be resisted. The *Dasein* of the *Logic* is not yet a stone or a sound; it is the *category* under which stones and sounds (and indeed everything determinately existing) fall, but at the level of the *Logic* this category has no empirical filling and needs none. What it has is the structure we have just identified: resulted immediacy, internalized negation, and an internal field of articulation. That is enough, at this level, to constitute a determination — and it is exactly what pure Being lacked.

## VII. The First Dialectical Step, Now Watched From Inside

Pull back. What we have just retraced — Being → Nothing → Becoming → *Dasein* — is the opening movement of the *Logic*. §02 gave its introductory argument; the closer traversal distinguishes more precisely what each transition requires and preserves.

Three features of the method can now be specified more closely.

The first is the **compulsion** of each step. At the schematic level, "the determination negates itself" is a phrase. Inside the move, the claim has a specific warrant: pure Being offers no determination by which it could be held apart from Nothing. The negation is not added by the philosopher; it emerges from the determination under examination. This is what "immanent" means in Hegel: the negation is brought up by the determination itself when held to its own concept, not imposed from outside.

The second is the **conservation** characteristic of *Aufhebung*. At the schematic level, sublation can sound like a magic word that resolves an opposition. Inside the move, it is a structure: Becoming contains Being and Nothing as vanishing moments; in *Dasein* they are differently determined within a settled unity. Their independence and earlier form are cancelled, not merely stored alongside the result (*SL* §187). Preservation is therefore also transformation. This is why Hegel can write — and §09 already quoted — that "the truth is the whole": the whole develops through these transformations, rather than being assembled from unchanged pieces.

The third is the **strictness required to test the argument**. Substituting "highest being" or "totality of beings" for *pure* Being changes the determination under examination. The reader must withhold such additions if the opening claim is to be tested on its own terms. But strict attention is not a guarantee of assent, and a felt necessity is not its proof. The test remains whether each passage follows from the content at hand without borrowing a further determination. A reader's disagreement must be answered at that point, not diagnosed merely as a failure to think strictly.

This is also what distinguishes Section III's mode of presentation from Section II's. Section II could compress individual arguments because its task was to make the whole circle visible at once. Section III must unfold the claimed necessity of each step, rather than allow a familiar sequence to stand in for its warrant. The same dialectical structure that organized the bird's-eye picture is at work here, but at a finer resolution; and at every later step in Section III, the same will be true.

## VIII. What Comes Next

We have arrived at *Dasein* as the first concrete category that stands. The next installment develops its determinateness as *quality*, distinguishes **reality** and **negation**, and follows their relation through to the emergence of **something** and **other**; the **limit** follows in §12, and **finitude** together with the great confrontation between the **spurious** and the **true infinite** in §13.

The reader who has followed this installment carefully has already, in microcosm, learned the discipline that Section III is meant to cultivate: hold the determination strictly; watch it negate itself; identify what its self-negation cannot cancel; let *that* be the next determination. The discipline holds at every level; the *form* of the movement does not — passing-over in Being, shining-into in Essence, developing-out-of in the Concept. The categories grow in concreteness, and the movement grows articulated along with them.

## Coda

The reader's work in this installment has been to retrace a movement, not supply it by the effort of reading. Holding the determinations strictly, following their vanishing, and asking what the result retains are practices through which the argument can be understood and tested. They do not make the reader's experience identical with the categories' logical development. Section III cultivates this attention to the content: neither familiarity with the sequence nor conviction that it must work can replace the argument at the next step.
