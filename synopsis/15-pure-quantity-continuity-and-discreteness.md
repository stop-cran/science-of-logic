# Pure Quantity: Continuity and Discreteness

*The sixth installment of Section III — and the first in the sphere of Quantity. A close reading of **pure quantity** as the sublated being-for-self that the previous installment produced: the determinacy that has become **indifferent** to the being it determines; its two inseparable moments, **continuity** and **discreteness**, and their development into continuous and discrete magnitude; Hegel's treatment of the puzzles of division and **Kant's Second Antinomy**, distinguishing his critique of the proofs and his positive account of quantity from Kant's own resolution; the limits of a comparison with mathematical and physical questions; and the **limitation** by which quantity becomes the **Quantum**, without yet determining any particular amount.*

---

## I. Where We Are: from Quality to Quantity

The previous installment closed the sphere of Quality. It did so not by reaching a final quality but by watching a quality — the exclusive One — sublate its own exclusiveness. Repulsion posited many Ones; but repulsion proved to contain attraction, and attraction repulsion, so that neither could be had alone; and the two, shown to be moments of one self-mediating movement, left the One present only as sublated, its limit a limit which is just as much no limit. That indifference of determinacy to the being it determines is **Quantity** (*Quantität*), and it appears first in the form of *continuity*. The bare repeatable **unit of counting** has its ground in that result, but not yet its development — that waits on Number (§16).

This installment takes up Quantity at its purest — before it has given itself a limit, before it is *this much* rather than *that much*. Its first simplicity is not featurelessness: it contains the continuous and discrete moments whose distinction must be developed without breaking their unity. Hegel finds in their separation the ground of the Second Antinomy. Following that argument will require us to distinguish the moments he preserves from the cosmological assertions he rejects, and his verdict on Kant from Kant's own account.

## II. What Quantity Is — Sublated Being-for-self

Begin by fixing what kind of determination quantity is, for the whole sphere turns on it. In Quality, the determinacy *was* the being: a thing was *this* by its quality, and to alter the quality was to make it cease to be this thing and become another. Quantity is the determination in which this immediate identity of determinacy and being has been sublated. To anticipate its limited form, a red can become brighter or paler while remaining red: the alteration does not yet change its colour into another (*SL* §393). Determinacy has become **indifferent** (*gleichgültig*) to the being it determines. The illustration concerns a quantum; pure quantity is the categorial ground of such indifferent limitation, not already a measurable amount.

This is why Hegel calls quantity **sublated being-for-self** (*SL* §395). The being-for-self of the One was the sharpest self-assertion of qualitative determinacy: *this* one, excluding all else. Its independent exclusiveness has not survived intact, but neither has the One vanished into an empty unity. Attraction is retained as continuity, in which the Ones continue themselves into one another; repulsion is retained as discreteness within that continuity (*SL* §§395–398). Quantity does not arrive from outside the Quality sphere; it is that sphere's own result. Preservation means this determinate change of standing, not the thinning of an otherwise unchanged content (§14).

Pure quantity is thus not indeterminate Being restored, nor a collection awaiting an external count. It is the determinate unity of continuity and discreteness, initially present in the form of continuity, without a posited quantitative limit (*SL* §§398–399). More-and-less will belong to the alteration of quantum; to use it as the definition here would anticipate the development. We must first see how the two moments are present in this unity and how their distinction is posited.

## III. Continuity and Discreteness — the Two Moments

Pure quantity has two moments, and they are precisely the sublated forms of attraction and repulsion.

The first is **continuity** (*Kontinuität*). Attraction sublated the many Ones' independent externality, not their manyness (§14). Their self-sameness is therefore not an empty unity that has swallowed its differences: the plurality remains, but no longer interrupts their continuation into one another (*SL* §396). Taken as a moment of magnitude, this is continuity: the unbroken self-equality of quantity, its self-continuation through its differences. Continuity is the legacy of attraction — the moment of *oneness*, of uninterrupted self-identity, in magnitude. The line, the interval, the duration, the smooth gradient: these are quantity with its continuous moment to the fore.

The second is **discreteness** (*Diskretion*). The same Ones were *many*; their distinction has not disappeared with their independent exclusiveness. But it is repulsion *sublated*, and Hegel marks the difference exactly: this is "a coalescent discreteness, where the ones are not connected by the void, by the negative, but by their own continuity" (*SL* §397). Their manyness does not break their self-sameness. Discreteness is therefore not an aggregate of ready-made particles out of which continuity must be assembled. It is the distinctness of the Ones within their own continuous unity. A row of marks can illustrate manyness, but its already counted units and intervals are not the logical starting point.

The decisive distinction is between **moments of quantity and the forms in which those moments are posited**. Hegel first presents their unity as pure quantity (*SL* §§395–398). After the Remarks on its conception and the antinomy, he develops continuous and discrete magnitude (*SL* §§429–433). We bring that development forward here so that the Remarks can be read with the distinction in view. Continuity, posited as the coherent unity of the discrete, is the whole quantity in the form of continuous magnitude, not merely one isolated side. But immediacy cannot be quantity's complete determination: quantity is itself sublated immediacy, and the One contained in it must also be posited. It is then discrete magnitude, in which distinct Ones still have the same unity (*SL* §§430–431). Hegel consequently allows these forms to be species of quantity, provided their distinction follows its own moments rather than an external classification (*SL* §433). Each contains both moments; the difference is which determination is posited and which remains implicit (*SL* §432). This is not a prohibition on mathematical distinctions between continuous and discrete structures: Hegel's common unity of Ones is not, just by bearing the name continuity, mathematical continuity.

## IV. Why the Two Moments Are Inseparable

Watch the inseparability directly, for it is the heart of the matter.

A purely **continuous** magnitude, with discreteness wholly removed, would not be the continuity just derived. It is tempting to prove this from the schoolbook definition of magnitude as *what can be increased or diminished*; but Hegel calls that definition circular, since increase already means making a magnitude more. He nevertheless credits it with expressing the indifference of quantitative alteration (*SL* §394). Its defect as a definition does not erase that insight; its subject, moreover, is quantum, not pure quantity. The argument here has a different ground: continuity is "not, however, an *immediate* unity, but a unity of ones which possess being-for-self," in which "*the asunderness of the plurality* is still contained… but at the same time as not differentiating or *interrupting* it" (*SL* §396). The One is not absent but continues into the Ones from which it is distinguished. Thus "quantity is simply the omnipresent *real possibility* within itself of the one" (*SL* §399): division is possible within it because distinctness is its own moment. Removing that moment would discard the mediation constitutive of this continuity, not purify quantity into a more adequate form.

A purely **discrete** magnitude — a manyness of units with no continuity whatever — would be a heap of absolutely separate ones with nothing holding them together as *one* magnitude. But that is exactly what discreteness in quantity is not, and Hegel is careful to say so: "with this plurality of ones… we are not again in the presence of the plurality of atoms and the void, repulsion in general. Because discrete magnitude is quantity, its discreteness is itself continuous. This continuity in the discrete consists in the ones being the same as one another, or in having the same unity" (*SL* §431). The sameness at issue is their being Ones, not a further qualitative likeness such as colour (*SL* §403); discrete magnitude is accordingly "the asunderness of the manifold one as self-same, not the manifold one in general but posited as the *many of a unity*" (*SL* §431). So discreteness, to be the discreteness *of a magnitude*, must carry continuity within it. The continuity is not added from outside; it is implicit in the discreteness as its own other moment.

Neither moment is sufficient in isolation: continuity is the self-continuation of the distinct Ones, discreteness their distinction within a common unity. This recalls the mutual implication reached in repulsion and attraction, but does not replace it with a new oscillation between independent terms. Hegel expressly says that these determinations are not to be resolved back into repulsion and attraction: each is now to be taken in its unity with the other, as the whole quantity (*SL* §430). The advance is the positing of the distinction within the achieved unity.

Hegel connects this distinction with the Eleatic arguments about motion. He does not work through all of them here: he defers their fuller treatment to space, time and the history of philosophy (*SL* §426). What he does defend, crediting Aristotle, is the distinction between *divisibility* and an accomplished *dividedness*: "divisibility itself even is only a possibility, not an *existing* of the parts, and the plurality as such is posited in the continuity only as a moment, as sublated" (*SL* §427). The point is not that no finite division can be completed, but that infinite divisibility does not itself give an already completed division into independently existing parts. Nor is walking across a room a logical answer to a difficulty about motion: Hegel's account of Diogenes expressly rejects replacing the work of thought with an appeal to sight (*SL* §426). This reports Hegel's use of Aristotle, not an independent reconstruction of every Zenonian argument.

A limited modern comparison can make the distinction useful without pretending to settle those arguments. In the series *1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + …*, every finite partial sum is less than one, yet one is its limit: for any positive error bound, sufficiently long finite partial sums remain within that bound of one. A final summand is not required. This is a mathematical achievement on its own terms; it neither depends on rejecting completed infinite sets nor proves Hegel's derivation of quantity. Our comparison concerns the distinction between successive finite articulations and their determinate relation to a whole (§07), not an identity between mathematical and Hegelian senses of continuity, and not a claim that analysis can grasp only one side at a time.

Nor should the quantization of energy be enlisted as discreteness first appearing in an otherwise purely continuous quantity. The discrete and continuous moments belong together already in Hegel's category; a physical theory's allowed energy values are a further, determinate matter. Discrete energy levels need not be equally spaced, and a theory may also admit a continuous spectrum. The relevant question for the later comparison is how quantitative determinations depend on the kind of system, not whether manyness has at last arrived. A comparison with **Measure**, and especially with its nodal line, remains an extension for this synopsis to justify in §18. Neither energy levels nor the quantum of action are deduced here.

## V. Kant's Second Antinomy — the Critique and Its Scope

Hegel takes the Second Antinomy as a case in which the two moments of quantity are separated and each asserted as sufficient. But his account contains two distinct claims: a criticism of Kant's proofs, and a positive account of the determinations which, in Hegel's reading, those proofs isolate. Neither claim should be confused with Kant's own resolution, or accepted merely because the other has been established.

Kant's **Second Antinomy** opposes the claim that every composite substance in the world consists of simple parts to the claim that no composite thing in the world consists of such parts; their accompanying claims concern whether anything simple exists in the world. The thesis is not a theory of modern physical atoms. In his Observations Kant expressly distinguishes a substantial composite from space, whose parts are possible only within the whole, and confines the inference to the simple to self-subsisting things. His discussion of the antithesis likewise distinguishes extended bodies from the wider question whether absolute simplicity could be established in possible experience ([*Critique of Pure Reason*, Second Conflict and its Observations](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/4280/4280-h/4280-h.htm)). Against Hegel's claim that the substantial substrate makes no difference to the antinomy (*SL* §413), we must preserve the restrictions that constitute Kant's argument.

Hegel calls the thesis *analytical or tautological*: composition is already an external putting-together, and the simple is its other (*SL* §413). He calls the initial conditional inference correct (*SL* §414); more strongly, he grants that composition is indeed contingent and external, not merely that simplicity would follow if it were (*SL* §416). Yet he maintains that the elaborate proof only conceals this assumption and presents as a consequence what it has already used (*SL* §§415–419). His further point is decisive: if the issue were only contingent togetherness rather than continuity, there would be no antinomy worth constructing (*SL* §416). He recasts the conflict as one concerning quantity as such, hence space and time no less than substance (*SL* §422). That is the bridge to his positive account of continuity and discreteness. It is also a change of ground which must be made explicit. In Kant's restricted conception, the contingency of composition belongs to the self-subsistence of its constituents. Hegel's objection does not by itself show that the conclusion fails under those premises; its further force depends on the account of quantity developed in §§III–IV, not on treating the use of a premise as a failure to infer.

Against the antithesis Hegel objects that placing substances in space already supplies the continuity which excludes simple spatial parts (*SL* §§419–422). He also identifies a shift between composition from antecedently independent parts and the manifold within a continuous space; importantly, he credits Kant with correctly distinguishing space from such a composition (*SL* §423). Hegel himself compresses Kant's appeal to appearances into the claim that microscopes and knives have found no simples (*SL* §424). That is not Kant's ground. The Transcendental Aesthetic argues that space is the a priori form of outer intuition, and the antinomy's Observations expressly apply this to bodies as appearances. Hegel knows that restriction but contests the appeal to intuition as sufficient for comprehension (*SL* §§422–424). His demand for a conceptual account must therefore confront that ground, not replace it with an empirical failure to cut matter finely enough.

The positive result Hegel claims is precise. Taken as continuous, magnitude contains the One as the possibility of division, not as an absolutely independent atom; taken as discrete, its Ones already contain continuity in their sameness. Thus "each of the two opposed sides contains its other within itself and neither can be thought without the other," and "neither of these determinations, taken alone, has truth; this belongs only to their unity" (*SL* §425). He preserves the moments, not the opposed assertions that each is sufficient in isolation. The argument for their unity has been supplied in §§III–IV; it is not proved simply by pronouncing both cosmological proofs defective.

Kant's resolution is not exhausted by Hegel's summary that reason must remain within sensuous perception (*SL* §428). It rejects the common assumption that appearances constitute a whole determined in itself independently of the regress through its conditions. In the mathematical antinomies, both opposed assertions can therefore be false. For the Second Antinomy he expressly distinguishes a body's infinite divisibility from its consisting of an already given infinite number of parts: the division is given only in the progressing decomposition (*Critique of Pure Reason*, Antinomy, Sections VII and IX.II). Within Kant's account this preserves unlimited divisibility without asserting a completed infinite composition. The distinction Hegel praises in Aristotle is thus not absent from Kant's solution.

Hegel nevertheless judges that solution insufficient because it leaves the opposed determinations without their truth in the unity of the Concept (*SL* §§410, 428). He credits the antinomies with helping overthrow the old metaphysics and extends the demand for a dialectical account beyond four cosmological cases (*SL* §§407–409). Our conclusion must preserve both the achievement and the dispute. Kant supplies a reasoned distinction between phenomena and things in themselves and between division and a completed aggregate; Hegel demands the immanent development of the determinations themselves rather than their restriction to possible experience. That stronger task cannot be discharged by Kant's restriction, but neither does stating it invalidate Kant's reasoning under that restriction. The issue is the adequacy of the ground and scope of cognition, not a contrast between a Kant who refuses to think and a Hegel who begins.

## VI. The Limitation of Quantity — the Quantum

Pure quantity is *unlimited* in a specific sense: no quantitative limit has yet been posited. This does not make it an infinitely large magnitude, an unfinished count, or a stock of possible amounts. It is the unity whose continuous and discrete forms have now been distinguished. Their distinction supplies the next step; neither form is yet a quantum (*SL* §433).

Discrete magnitude has the One as its principle, a plurality of Ones, and their continuity: it is therefore posited as *one magnitude*, whose determinateness is the One. As distinguished from continuous magnitude, it is a Something; the One is now its first negation, an excluding limit within the unity (*SL* §434). This limit is not merely a boundary between two names in a classification. Related to the continuous unity it negates, and self-related as a One, it is "the enclosing, encompassing limit" (*SL* §435). It gathers the many Ones within one limited magnitude. But the being limited remains continuity, which passes beyond this One and is indifferent to it. Because the limit belongs to continuity in either form, continuous and discrete magnitude both pass into quanta (*SL* §436). Quantity with this **limit** is the **Quantum** (*Quantum*). The argument establishes limited quantity as a category, not why a given magnitude should have one particular numerical value rather than another.

The quantitative limit is peculiar. Unlike the qualitative limit of Something (§12), it can alter without the continuous being thereby becoming a qualitative other. Yet it does determine *this* quantum: change the amount and it is no longer this amount, even though it remains a quantum. Indifference does not mean that the limit has no effect. The tension between definite limitation and continuity beyond the limit belongs to the result itself (*SL* §435). The next installment must develop it through Number and extensive and intensive magnitude before reaching the quantitative infinite; the phrase *more-and-less* does not perform those transitions for us.

## VII. Two Misreadings, Answered Directly

Two standing misreadings are worth meeting before we proceed.

The first is the **arithmetical misreading**: that "quantity" just means number, so this whole analysis is a roundabout way of talking about counting. But number has not yet appeared. Number is *determinate* quantity, quantum articulated into amount and unit — and quantum itself is only emerging now, at the end of this installment, out of quantity's self-limitation. Pure quantity is logically prior to number as the unity whose limitation must first be developed, not as an empty container into which numbers are placed. To read number back into pure quantity is to skip exactly the step — the limitation of quantity — that this installment has been preparing. (We will meet the same temptation, and refuse it, when we come to the quantitative infinite: the endless "ever larger" of the number series is not pure quantity but a feature of the *quantum* and its limit.)

The second is the **physical-continuum misreading**: identifying continuity with space and discreteness with material atoms, and treating their logical unity as a physical theory. Hegel himself offers space, time, matter, light and the ego as illustrations of pure quantity, not as a distribution of its moments among separate objects (*SL* §§402–403). His account does make a claim about their categorial ground; it is not innocent of claims about nature. But in analysing the antinomy he explicitly sets aside the concrete material to consider the thought-determinations on their own (*SL* §409). Establishing their unity does not thereby determine the constitution of physical matter or decide whether a mathematical model should use a discrete or continuous structure. Those connections require their own warrant (§14).

## VIII. What the Method Did Here — Observations

Two observations on the work performed here.

The first: **the sphere of Quantity opens by re-collecting the result of Quality, not by starting afresh.** It would be easy to feel the move into Quantity as a fresh beginning — a new subject, magnitude, with its own vocabulary, displacing the old talk of Something and Other and Limit. The truth is the opposite. Continuity *is* attraction sublated; discreteness *is* repulsion sublated. The exclusive One has lost its immediate independence and remains as quantity's own moment, through which a quantitative limit can be posited. This is the changed positive standing traced in §14, not a diminishing weight of preservation in the sphere of Being. Keeping the genealogy in view prevents us from treating quantity as logically primitive. Its indifference to quality is itself a qualitative result; the further development must establish what that indifference permits and where it ceases to suffice.

The second: **criticism of a proof and development of its determinations are connected tasks, not interchangeable verdicts.** Hegel does not reconcile the antinomy by awarding half the truth to each cosmological assertion. He contests the proofs and shows why continuity and discreteness cannot stand as independent principles. But his own concessions matter: the conditional inference of the thesis is correct, and Kant correctly distinguishes the continuity of space from composition (*SL* §§414, 423). Our account must also preserve Kant's actual solution (§V). The methodological gain is therefore a demand to examine both what follows under a premise and whether that premise adequately determines the object. A demonstration of the first is not a derivation of the second; failure to supply the second does not erase the first. Here the positive result rests on the developed unity of the quantitative moments. It is not a recipe which guarantees that any two opposed claims will become complementary truths.

## IX. What Comes Next

The next installment develops the **Quantum**:

- **Number** (*Zahl*) — the quantum fully determined as the unity of **amount** (*Anzahl*) and **unit** (*Einheit*); why number is the complete posited form of the quantitative limit, and why arithmetic's operations (counting, the species of calculation) are the working-out of the relation of amount and unit.
- **Extensive and intensive magnitude** — the quantum as a plurality of units (extensive) and the same quantum gathered into a simple *degree* (intensive); why the two are not two kinds of quantity but the same quantum in two forms, and what becomes of the old picture of "intensive" qualities.
- **The quantitative infinite** — the quantum's self-transcendence into *more* and *less*, the spurious endless progress and its negation; the recurrence of the §13 figure with a different categorial ground, to be derived anew — and then the long-deferred reading of the **differential calculus**, as an illustration of the qualitative determination reached in the quantitative ratio, not the argument which first produces it.

With number, the quantum, and the quantitative infinite, the second moment of Quantity will be complete, and the way will be open to the **Quantitative Ratio** and, through it, to **Measure**.

## Coda

The achievement here is not to discredit quantitative inquiry but to refuse to treat its form as an unconditioned beginning. The ground of the indifferent unit is the exclusive One sublated; continuity and discreteness preserve attraction and repulsion in changed forms, and their own distinction leads to limited quantity. Counting can apply to qualitatively different things precisely because five is five regardless of what it counts. That power does not make counting an exhaustive account of its objects; equally, deriving quantity philosophically is not a prerequisite for a valid mathematical inference (§14). The antinomy has made the distinction between a premise's use and its further justification especially sharp, while requiring us to preserve what Kant's resolution actually achieves. Quantity has not forgotten its qualitative genesis and need not recover a lost stock of qualities. It has acquired an indifferent limit, and the relation between that limit and its continuity is the next problem. *What looked like a simple beginning has proved to be a mediated result; what that result will become must be earned from its own determination.*
