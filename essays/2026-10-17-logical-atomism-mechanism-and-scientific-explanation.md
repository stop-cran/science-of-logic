# Logical Atomism, Mechanism, and Scientific Explanation

*A working essay accompanying §30. Hegel's development of **mechanism** sharpens a question for logical atomism: can analysis terminate in independent elements without losing the determinate content it was meant to explain? Answering requires distinguishing logical independence from causal, evidential, and constitutive dependence. A coupled-oscillator example shows how relations define dynamically distinguished collective modes while exact analysis remains indispensable. This is an English research companion to the bilingual synopsis, not an additional numbered installment or a claim that Hegel refuted his twentieth-century successors.*

## I. What would count as settling the question?

Apparently separate facts often prove connected. A change in one instrument's calibration affects several results; one body's motion depends on another; a characteristic attributed to a component turns out to belong to an organized system. These are important discoveries, but they are not interchangeable arguments against logical atomism.

The issue belongs beside **Mechanism**, especially its development toward **Chemism**, because §30 asks what independence becomes when the relation initially held outside an object proves essential to its determination. The question is not merely whether objects interact. It is whether the account can still treat what they specifically are as indifferent to the relation through which they are determined.

That comparison must preserve its different subjects. A fact is not a physical particle, a proposition is not the fact it expresses, and neither is automatically Hegel's **Object**. Likewise, the question whether scientific inquiry should begin from facts is not identical with the question whether the facts or their constituents are ultimately independent. The former concerns the justification of a beginning; the latter concerns the structure of what is explained. Their connection requires an argument, not a substitution of names.

## II. What logical atomism actually commits us to examine

Russell's *The Philosophy of Logical Atomism*, Lecture I, distinguishes facts from particular things and from propositions, which can be true or false. The inspected discussion recognizes relational facts and insists that particular facts do not dispense with general facts. A world containing relations is therefore not, just for that reason, a world Russell's account cannot describe. Nor is his fact simply a self-enclosed little object.

The *Tractatus* makes a stronger, precisely located independence claim. Its objects already contain the possibilities of their combination: their independence is bound to their possible occurrence in states of affairs (2.0121–2.0122). Nevertheless, atomic facts are independent of one another (2.061–2.062), and one elementary proposition cannot be inferred from another (5.134). Relational structure and independence thus coexist within the position being examined.

**Possibilities of combination are not the occurrence of a particular combination.** Giving objects a determinate logical form does not, on Wittgenstein's account, make the occurrence of one atomic fact require the occurrence of another. A critique which discovers that objects have relational possibilities has not yet reached the independence thesis.

Nor are ordinary scientific statements thereby certified as elementary propositions. A statement about a body's motion, a measurement result, or a normal mode may be complex. The *Tractatus* expressly connects statements about complexes with statements about their constituents (2.0201). Showing that two ordinary statements entail or exclude one another does not establish that the proposed terminal elements of their analysis do so.

The distinction between these authors matters. Russell's account of facts supplies part of the historical setting; the precise independence thesis confronted here is the **Tractarian** one. The argument will not attribute every feature of Wittgenstein's construction to Russell, or make either author stand for analytic philosophy as a whole. The achievement to retain is analysis that distinguishes kinds of statement, exposes hidden structure, and refuses to turn grammatical appearance into an inventory of ultimate entities.

## III. What Hegel's mechanism has already achieved

Hegel's mechanical object is not an atom. He expressly excludes that identification: the object is a totality, whereas the atoms he contrasts with it are not (*SL* §1546). Initially, the objects' relations remain external to their specific natures. Yet their determinations are referred to other objects, which refer theirs elsewhere in turn. Self-enclosure and determinate self-sufficiency therefore fail to coincide (*SL* §§1543–1552).

The development does not stop at this initial defect. **Free mechanism has immanent law and self-movement**, not only external collision or a pattern imposed by an observer (*SL* §§1572–1575). An account that calls every lawful connection external has criticized a beginning Hegel has already developed beyond.

The remaining tension is more exact. The objects possess their essential self-subsistence in the system's centrality and laws, yet are not themselves differentiated into the law's ideal difference. Their sustaining unity and their specific natures have not been brought into the required correspondence (*SL* §1576). The next determination makes their opposition objective: the relation to a specifically opposed object belongs to what each is, rather than merely to what happens to it (*SL* §§1577–1579). This is the threshold of Chemism, not a deduction that every relation constitutes every feature of everything.

Our methodological reconstruction asks whether scientific analysis preserves that distinction. Does it identify something whose determinate character is explained through the relation, or quietly recover an allegedly self-sufficient element by leaving that character out? As §26 already insists, a stronger demand for explanation is not itself a refutation of an opponent who does not accept it. Hegel's own requirement that criticism meet the opponent on the opponent's own ground, discussed in §27, applies to our comparison too (*SL* §§1287–1288).

## IV. A concrete case: two masses, two normal modes

Before calculating, distinguish four questions:

| Dependence | What must be established |
|---|---|
| **Causal** | A change in one physical condition alters another's evolution under the relevant laws. |
| **Evidential** | The warrant for one result depends on other observations, calibrations, or assumptions. |
| **Constitutive** | A relation belongs to the identity of the determination being explained, not merely to an influence upon it. |
| **Logical** | One proposition entails or excludes another by their logical structure. |

One experiment may bear on several questions. It does not make their answers identical.

Consider a textbook model: two equal masses *m*, each attached to its adjacent fixed wall by a spring of constant *k*, and joined by a third spring with the same constant. Let *x₁* and *x₂* be their one-dimensional displacements from equilibrium. Assume ideal linear springs, positive *m* and *k*, and no damping or external driving. The equations are

*m · d²x₁/dt² = −2kx₁ + kx₂*,

*m · d²x₂/dt² = kx₁ − 2kx₂*.

The local motions are coupled. Recording one displacement in its own column does not make its evolution dynamically independent of the other.

Now set *q₊ = x₁ + x₂* and *q₋ = x₁ − x₂*. Adding and subtracting the equations gives

*d²q₊/dt² = −(k/m)q₊*,

*d²q₋/dt² = −(3k/m)q₋*.

The **normal modes** are collective patterns of motion. In the symmetric mode, *x₁ = x₂*, the middle spring's extension remains unchanged, and the angular frequency is *√(k/m)*. In the antisymmetric mode, *x₁ = −x₂*, that extension changes, and the angular frequency is *√(3k/m)*. General motion is a superposition of the two modes. The original displacements remain recoverable: *x₁ = (q₊ + q₋)/2* and *x₂ = (q₊ − q₋)/2*.

This is not the discovery of two private oscillations to which a relation is subsequently attached. **The relative displacements belong to what these modes are**, but writing a sum or difference alone would establish only a definition. Because the two frequencies differ, the only time-independent linear displacement coordinates that evolve as separate oscillators are nonzero multiples of *q₊* and *q₋*, with their labels interchangeable. The dynamics therefore distinguishes these collective patterns from arbitrary combinations of recordings. Constitutive dependence here concerns those dynamically distinguished motions of the system, not a claim that each mass has no properties apart from every other object. Nor does every feature depend on the middle spring: the symmetric motion leaves it unextended.

A corresponding experiment would prepare equal nonzero displacements with zero initial velocities, first in the same direction and then in opposite directions, and record both motions against a common time reference. Keeping mass 1's initial displacement the same in both preparations, the ideal model predicts different frequencies when mass 2's displacement is reversed. That is the table's causal dependence: the second mass's state changes the first mass's evolution under the stipulated laws.

Removing only the middle spring while leaving the outer springs unchanged gives two uncoupled oscillators, both with angular frequency *√(k/m)*. The same-direction preparation is a null comparison: its frequency is unchanged. The opposite-direction preparation discriminates the coupling's contribution: its frequency falls from *√(3k/m)* to *√(k/m)*. The control also loses the distinct-frequency selection of the two collective patterns: any invertible, time-independent linear change of displacement coordinates now leaves the equations decoupled. The relative patterns can still be defined, but the dynamics no longer singles them out. Actual springs, losses, measurement errors, and the range of linear response would still need investigation. This is a proposed methodological illustration, not a report of an experiment performed here.

The analytic result is as important as the coupling. **The normal coordinates evolve independently in this model.** The equations no longer mix them, although each coordinate describes a collective motion. Relational constitution has enabled a more adequate analysis; it has not forbidden analysis. Independence is recovered at a specified level, through an explicit transformation whose conditions can be stated.

That is dynamical independence, not automatically statistical independence of measured amplitudes or logical independence of elementary propositions. No specific opposition in Hegel's sense has been exhibited between the masses: opposite phases are not opposed specific natures. The analogy concerns the lawful differentiation and mediated independence already reached within free mechanism (§30, V–VI), not its transition to Chemism.

## V. The atomist's reply—and the remaining burden

The strongest reply is straightforward. Everything just calculated concerns complex physical systems and propositions about them. An atomist can accept the collective modes, the coupling, the measurement dependencies, and the exact change of coordinates. None establishes that ultimate analysis must end in mutually dependent elementary propositions.

The distinction between **logical possibility** and **compatibility with a specified physical model** reinforces that reply. An inference using equations of motion and initial conditions is not an inference from one elementary proposition alone. Wittgenstein goes further: he denies a causal nexus that could justify inference from present events to future ones (5.136–5.1361), and recognizes only logical necessity (6.37). A reply on that basis can accept the model's causal dependencies in our stipulated scientific sense without accepting a distinct necessity connecting events. His account also allows that one mechanical description's greater simplicity says something about the world (6.341–6.343). The special utility of normal coordinates is therefore not something his position must deny.

There is nevertheless an internal burden on the proposed analysis: it must preserve the content and exclusions it purports to analyze. The *Tractatus* itself supplies a sharp instance. At 6.3751, Wittgenstein treats two different colours at one place in the visual field as logically incompatible, while reiterating that a conjunction of elementary propositions cannot be a contradiction. In the same passage he sketches how the contradiction presents itself in physics: a particle cannot simultaneously have two velocities or be in two places; particles in different places at the same time cannot be identical. He therefore attempts to locate the exclusion in logical structure, rather than merely declaring the colour assertions non-elementary. The question is whether that route resolves the exclusions without presupposing mutually excluding elementary propositions. This identifies a task for examining the proposed analysis, not a demonstrated impossibility of completing it.

Our Hegelian challenge can therefore be stated without the slogan that everything is connected: **does the proposed analysis recover the determinate relations belonging to its subject matter, or does its appearance of completeness depend on setting them aside?** An account which restores them through its logical structure or complex propositions has answered an objection that a simple inventory would not. The critic must examine that answer, rather than stipulate that only Hegel's vocabulary could count as preserving the content.

There is a substantive disagreement to examine here, not only a difference of philosophical ambition. Against Wittgenstein's restriction of necessity to logic and his refusal to treat natural laws as explanations (6.37–6.372), Hegel presents developed law as a principle of self-movement and free necessity (*SL* §1575). As §30, VI stresses, this is not simply the elevation of empirical regularities into necessity. The open question is whether Hegel earns the immanent necessity his account claims; neither the oscillator equations nor a difference in philosophical projects decides it.

The result is consequently asymmetric. We have a positive example of relation-bearing scientific determinations and a successful analysis of them. We have not obtained a general refutation of logical atomism. What fails is the shortcut in either direction: causal connection does not disprove elementary independence, and successful experimental isolation does not establish it.

## VI. What this changes in scientific inquiry

The methodological gain is a more exact account of **what has been isolated**. A separate instrument channel, a dynamically decoupled coordinate, an independently prepared specimen, and an elementary proposition are not four names for the same achievement.

In the oscillator case, this changes the investigation rather than merely its philosophical description. A sufficiently resolved recording of one mass can identify the modal motion conditional on the model. Recording both masses against a common time reference instead tests the predicted relative motion rather than inferring it from the assumed dynamics. The removal control then distinguishes what isolation preserves—the symmetric frequency—from what it changes—the antisymmetric frequency and the selection of a distinct modal basis. Analysis returns to the local recordings through the inverse transformation, preserving what they measured instead of dismissing them as appearances.

Three questions carry that lesson beyond this example:

- **Which determination is being explained?** Specify the local magnitude, collective pattern, or other object of the claim before asserting its independence.
- **What does isolation preserve, and what does it change?** Removing a relation may eliminate a disturbance, or alter the very determination under investigation. The difference must be established for the case.
- **How is the achievement recovered?** State the transformation, approximation, or controlled regime through which the richer account retains the warranted result of the simpler one.

These are our methodological proposals, informed by §30, not experimental rules deduced by pure Logic. Their warrant includes what they make possible in the worked example and remains answerable to further cases. A procedure is not better because it sounds more dialectical; it is better here because it discriminates the relevant motions, explains their frequencies, and preserves a calculable relation to the observations.

## Coda

The alternative is not isolated facts or an undifferentiated whole. Scientific investigation can discover that a relation belongs to what a determination is, then use that discovery to make a sharper analysis. **Independence becomes a specified achievement rather than an unexamined starting entitlement.** Whether such achievements can finally be grounded in independent elementary propositions remains a distinct philosophical claim, not something an experimental success or failure decides by itself.
