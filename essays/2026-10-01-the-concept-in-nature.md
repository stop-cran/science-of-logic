# The Concept in Nature: Hegel's Case and the Limits of Its Warrant

*A working essay, not a settled verdict. The question has recurred while revising §§09 and 27: does Hegel's account of the Concept warrant his restriction of its explicit natural realization to organic life? This essay keeps the textual evidence, the positive argument, the scientific comparisons, and the remaining burden of proof together. It is an English research companion to the bilingual synopsis, not an additional numbered installment.*

## I. The question that a quotation cannot settle

Two shortcuts lead in opposite directions. One reasons that the Concept develops from reciprocity, with no specifically biological premise, and therefore must be explicitly realized throughout inorganic nature. The other finds Hegel's express restriction to life and treats that discovery as the end of the methodological question. Neither inference is sufficient.

The first confuses the logical reach of a determination with its adequate realization in every particular object. A category may be at work in an object without that object exhibiting it in the form Hegel calls *for itself*. The second confuses an author's conclusion with the warrant for it. Correct attribution is indispensable to criticism; it is not a substitute for criticism.

We therefore need to keep three questions connected without making one answer do the work of all three: **what determination has been derived; what natural realization Hegel assigns to it; and whether that assignment follows on the stated grounds**. A scientific comparison adds its own evidence and may alter the question. An explanatory structure exhibited by a theory is not automatically the self-relation of the object it explains.

This is an instance of the synopsis's broader method–system problem, but naming that problem supplies no result. We must show the tension in this argument, or acknowledge that we have not shown it.

## II. What the derivation does establish

The Concept does not enter the *Logic* as an abstraction from plants, animals, or human consciousness. Hegel explicitly identifies Being and Essence as moments of its becoming, with substance, causality, and reciprocity as its immediate genesis (*SL* §§1275–1276, 1281–1289).

Reciprocity removes the privilege of one self-subsistent cause over another: each side acts and is acted upon. But stopping at mutual dependence leaves the terms and their determinations presupposed. Hegel's further demand is not one more cause behind them. It is that their apparent independence be understood as the differentiation of a unity whose determinations are its own. Universal, particular, and singular express that unity, not three objects subsequently brought together.

**Everything affects everything** is consequently too weak a formula. Even a well-established network of reciprocal effects does not, just by being connected, exhibit the Concept's self-determination. Conversely, the derivation gives no permission to define the Concept retrospectively as whatever only a biological organism can do. The account of life has to make good a determination already reached on logical grounds.

Hegel himself says that “the logical form of the Notion is independent of its non-spiritual, and also of its spiritual, shapes” (*SL* §1299, Miller). This does not say that every shape realizes it equally. It does prevent a concrete illustration from silently becoming the premise of the logical derivation.

## III. Hegel's positive case: externality, individuality, and life

### The Concept is not absent from inorganic nature

The textual boundary is explicit. *SL* §79 places the Concept **for itself** in organic individuality even where it is neither conscious nor known, and **only in itself** in inorganic nature. Section 1299 names life as its natural emergence, unthinking and unaware of itself. Section 1298's exclusion of concrete natural shapes from logic's proper subject matter does not retract those statements.

The contrast is therefore not between things with a Concept and things with none. Nor is *for itself* simply a synonym for consciousness: organic individuality already rules that reading out. The issue is how the determination exists in its object.

### Why externality matters

In the *Philosophy of Nature*, §247, Hegel identifies nature as the Idea in the form of otherness. Externality is not merely nature's distance from a knowing observer; it is the determination under which nature exists. Section 248 then says:

> “In this externality the determinations of the Concept have the semblance of indifferent subsistence and separation from one another; the Concept therefore exists as something inward.”
>
> — *Philosophy of Nature*, §248; our translation from the German.

This gives the organic/inorganic contrast a reason internal to the philosophical project. The Concept's unity must not remain merely an inner connection between terms that outwardly subsist apart. An external observer may comprehend their connection without the things themselves being a subject that maintains itself in it. A system's lawful behaviour is not yet the self-determination at issue.

In *SL* §1788 Hegel calls cause the highest immediate beginning within the sphere of necessity, but says it is “not yet a subject that maintains itself as such even in its actual realisation” (Miller). The next sentence includes the sun among inanimate things whose totality remains inner and whose moments are not posited in subjective form. A critic cannot refute this merely by pointing out that the sun persists, has a structure, or interacts with other bodies.

### What life is supposed to achieve

The *Encyclopaedia Logic* supplies a positive account rather than merely an exclusion:

> “The single members of the body are what they are only by and in relation to their unity.”
>
> — *Enc.* §216, Addition; Wallace.

Hegel's Aristotelian example is the severed hand: the material remains, but it is no longer a hand in the same functional actuality. The point is not simply that parts depend on other parts. Their activity belongs to a whole which maintains them as its members and maintains itself through them. Section 218 makes the return explicit:

> “Yet this action of the several members (organs) is only the living subject's one act to which their productions revert; so that in these productions nothing is produced except the subject: in other words, the subject only reproduces itself.”
>
> — *Enc.* §218; Wallace.

In assimilation, too, Hegel distinguishes life from the chemical process: the outcome is not simply a neutral product in which the original sides disappear; the living individual maintains and develops itself through its relation to the other (§219 and Addition). This is an account of immanent purposiveness, not a designer's intention or a miniature consciousness inside the organism.

There is a particularly revealing opposite case in the *Philosophy of Nature*, §339 and Addition. Hegel calls Earth a geological organism, but distinguishes its enduring formations and externally related conditions from the animal whose members are both products and producers of its process. Earth has a formative history; that is not, for him, the same as presently producing itself as a living subject. The distinction concerns the mode of unity and activity, not the presence or absence of a past. Whether his account correctly characterizes Earth is itself answerable to geoscience, not secured by the philosophical vocabulary.

Nor does organic life remove every inadequacy. The organism remains finite, dependent upon external nature, and mortal; the immediate individual does not exhaust the universality of its genus (*Enc.* §§216, 219–222). Hegel's argument is not that organisms are perfect while inorganic things are defective. Life is the first immediate form of the Idea, and its own limits drive the further logical development.

## IV. “Impotence of nature”: not simply intractability to knowledge

The term is **Ohnmacht der Natur**, conventionally rendered *impotence* or *powerlessness of nature*. “Intractability” can suggest nature's resistance to our classifications, but misses the direction of Hegel's claim. It is nature's incapacity fully to maintain the Concept's determination in the external realization of particulars, not merely our present inability to find the right theory.

In *SL* §1336, the variety of natural genera and species fails to exhibit the strictness of logical division. The passage grounds this in the Concept's self-externality. The *Philosophy of Nature*, §250, states the account more explicitly:

> “Contingency and determinability from outside have their right in the sphere of nature.”
>
> — *Philosophy of Nature*, §250; our translation from the German.

The implication cuts in two directions. Hegel ranks external contingency below the Concept's self-determination; but he also refuses to demand that philosophy manufacture a deduction of every contingent detail:

> “That impotence of nature sets limits to philosophy.”
>
> — *Philosophy of Nature*, §250, Remark; our translation from the German.

The continuation calls it inappropriate to demand that the Concept comprehend, construct, or deduce such contingencies. Traces of conceptual determination may extend into very particular formations without exhausting their determination; taking the trace for the whole yields an unwarranted analogy. This is a substantial constraint on speculative overreach, and it belongs to Hegel's case, not to a qualification supplied by his critics.

The same account creates a difficulty for criticism. It makes room for empirical variation, while also interpreting departures from conceptual types as nature's inadequacy rather than necessarily as a reason to revise the types. Section 250's discussion of intermediate forms and classification makes that tension visible. To decide whether a particular judgment protects a legitimate distinction or immunizes an inadequate classification, one must examine the determination and the evidence. Neither the presence of exceptions nor the invocation of contingency settles every case.

Whether the Concept's self-determination warrants the depreciation of natural profusion remains an evaluative question. A rejection requires a critique of that criterion or a defended alternative, neither of which is established by scientific explanatory success alone. Such success is not a biological falsification of the word *impotence*. Equally, the evaluative dimension must not shelter connected claims about the constitution and capacities of natural objects from scientific assessment. Hegel's ontology, his accounts of particular natural forms, and our assessment of their worth are distinguishable claims.

## V. Natural history: an achievement, not an automatic counterexample

It is too easy to explain Hegel's organic boundary by saying that almost no suitable scientific material existed. Kant's nebular hypothesis of 1755 and Laplace's of 1796 already sought to explain an organized planetary configuration through formation. They were distinct, conjectural accounts, not a modern theory silently present in the eighteenth century. Their interest here is the explanatory question they opened.

Geological succession was also a substantial scientific achievement. William Smith's 1815 map rested on correlating strata through fossils and recognizing an ordered sequence across regions. That is more than collecting unrelated marks, even though the reliable reconstruction of sequence is not itself a complete explanation of the processes producing it. Later evolutionary classification could ground relations among organisms in common descent rather than merely rank observed similarities. These achievements are evidentially specific; none needs to be credited to Hegel to matter to this comparison.

Hegel's objection cannot fairly be reduced to ignorance of natural history. *Philosophy of Nature* §249 distinguishes the necessary sequence of natural stages in the Idea from their temporal generation from one another. Section 339 Addition acknowledges Earth's successive changes and praises Werner's investigation of geological succession, while arguing that rearranging coexistence into chronology does not explain necessity. It also rejects the descent of living forms from one another. These concessions and exclusions must be read together.

His distinction between a sequence and its intelligible connection remains important. Historical science need not stop at sequence: it can establish the processes and conditions through which the differences arise. That achievement must be preserved even if it does not demonstrate a Hegelian subject. Conversely, a successful formation account does not by itself show that the resulting system maintains its differentiations as the activity of such a subject.

The comparison therefore defeats one excuse for Hegel's boundary—the supposed absence of any principled inorganic inquiry—but does not by itself defeat the boundary's strongest argument. Nor does it tell us what Hegel would have accepted had he known later science. His systematic distinction, not only the available evidence, would have to be examined.

## VI. Quantum fields: a more determinate comparison than universal interaction

Natural history is not the only possible site of the question. Quantum field theory supplies another: in the ordinary particle description, particles are excitations of fields rather than self-subsistent little objects to which a field is subsequently attached. This makes the relation between a determining unity and its particular manifestations worth philosophical attention.

But a physical field is not thereby Hegel's universal, nor does one particle picture exhaust interacting quantum field theory. The claim that every field is a particularization of all fields as a whole is also stronger than the physics warrants. Couplings connect determinate sectors; genuinely decoupled sectors need not contribute to one another's predictions. The Standard Model does not derive all its gauge, Higgs, and Yukawa parameters from a single independently established principle.

### A concrete case: electroweak differentiation

After electroweak symmetry breaking, the photon field and the Z field are different linear combinations of the underlying neutral gauge fields. Their relations are not arbitrary associations between independently specified particles. In the Particle Data Group's notation:

*A = B cos θ_W + W³ sin θ_W*

*Z = −B sin θ_W + W³ cos θ_W*

*tan θ_W = g′/g*

At tree level: *M_W = gv/2*; *M_Z = √(g² + g′²) v/2*; *M_photon = 0*.

Here *g* and *g′* are gauge couplings and *v* is the Higgs vacuum expectation value; the field expressions suppress their spacetime indices. The mass relations are leading-order relations, not claims that radiative corrections vanish. The scientific content comes from the theory and its evidence, not from a philosophical deduction. See the PDG electroweak review, equations 10.3–10.5 and §10.2.3.

This is a stronger comparison than *everything influences everything*: differentiated fields, masses, and interactions are constrained by a shared structure. It provides a concrete candidate for asking how apparently separate terms have their determination through a unity. It does not show that the electroweak system produces or maintains itself as a subject in Hegel's sense, still less that the Concept entails the Standard Model.

Two limits need to remain exact. First, measured inputs do not abolish the theory's explanatory achievement: once specified, they constrain relations and predictions that are not independent inputs. Second, a particle's charge quantum number is not the same thing as the scale-dependent electromagnetic coupling. Vacuum-polarization effects from coupled fields enter the latter; they do not mean that every field determines every other field's every property. A philosophical comparison gains strength by naming the actual dependency, not by enlarging it to the universe.

## VII. What an immanent criticism would have to show

The strongest question is not whether Hegel used organic examples. It is whether the exclusion of inorganic realization follows from the determination already established.

His answer has genuine logical force: the Concept is not bare interdependence; its determinations must be its own, and life exhibits an activity in which the whole maintains its members and returns through them to itself. A critic who substitutes mere persistence, complexity, common mathematical description, or feedback has not yet met that argument.

But citing the organism's achievement does not prove its exclusivity. A serious inorganic comparison must identify the object and its boundaries, the unity and its differentiations, and the process or structure through which those differentiations belong to the unity rather than merely to the observer's classification. It must then explain which Hegelian determination this establishes. If the argument concerns principled differentiation alone, it must not conclude that a natural subject has been demonstrated.

There are at least three possible outcomes, none fixed in advance. A comparison may remain within reciprocity. It may establish a substantive explanatory affinity without satisfying the stronger criterion of subjectivity. Or it may supply grounds for contesting the exclusive organic realization. In that last case, we must still distinguish finding a non-organic instance of Hegel's criterion from revising the criterion itself. The latter is a reconstruction, with a burden of justification of its own.

The method–system tension is established only if the systematic restriction requires a determination not warranted by the argument, or excludes a case that meets the warranted determination. We have identified the site of that test and some serious candidate material; we have not demonstrated the final result.

## VIII. What this investigation should keep us from forgetting

The earlier synopsis first overstated Hegel's rejection of natural history. Reading §339 and its Addition required preserving the geological concession alongside the rejection of descent. A later source check corrected a second claim: the organic/inorganic boundary is stated within the *Logic*, not merely imported from the *Philosophy of Nature*. Neither correction entitles us to freeze the category at that application. The latest return to the question required recovering Hegel's positive case as well as keeping its warrant open.

For future work, the unresolved question is precise: **does the self-determination derived through reciprocity require the kind of living individuality Hegel identifies, and what would count as an inorganic realization rather than an analogy?** The answer cannot be supplied by textual fidelity alone, by the abstract generality of the derivation, or by the success of a scientific theory taken without analysis of what it establishes.

This essay remains a working reference. Further inquiry should revise the relevant premise or comparison rather than inherit its conclusion as policy. The immediate tasks left open are the adequacy of the electroweak comparison to the determinations of the Concept, the force and scope of the transition to living subjectivity, and the empirical warrant of the natural applications. A developed Marxian reconstruction of the grounding of the Logic remains a separate task.
